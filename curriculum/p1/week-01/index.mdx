## Outcome

<Note>By Sunday you can take a C file, name every artifact the compiler produces from it, draw the stack and heap while it runs, implement and measure three classic algorithms, fit the growth exponent from your own timing data, and get the whole thing to zero sanitizer findings.</Note>

Concretely, five things you cannot do today and will be able to do by Sunday night:

1. Compute the exact representable range of BF16 from its bit layout — the format every GPU you touch in Week 26 uses — and say why fp16 needs loss scaling and BF16 does not.
2. Produce all four intermediate compilation artifacts from one `.c` file and explain what each one added or removed.
3. Write an arena allocator that is a measured **37× faster per allocation** than `malloc` (0.85 ns vs 31.85 ns on the reference machine), and state precisely what you gave up.
4. Plot sorting runtime on log-log axes and fit the exponent: **1.95** for selection sort, **1.06** for merge sort. Not "it's O(n²)" — the number, from your data.
5. Show, in assembly, a case where undefined behaviour caused the optimiser to delete a null check.

## Why C, and why first

Every abstraction you will meet later is a lie told on top of `malloc`. A Python object, a PyTorch tensor, a CUDA allocation, a KV cache — each one is a block of bytes with an ownership rule and a lifetime, dressed up. Learn the truth once, in the language that refuses to hide it, and every later "why is this slow / why did this leak / why did this corrupt" question has a place to land.

You will not write C after Week 2. You will read it constantly.

<Note>
**Measured.** Every figure in this week was produced on an Apple M3 Pro (11 cores, 18 GB),
macOS 15, Apple clang 15.0.0, arm64, CPython 3.13.2 — the machine this course was written on —
and each carries the command that produced it. Where a claim depends on a tool that does not
exist on Apple Silicon, the page says so rather than inventing a number: **Valgrind's slowdown
figures are quoted from its documentation, not measured here**, and `strace`, `perf` and `/proc`
are marked Linux-only throughout.

A handful of numbers are stronger than measured — the UTF-8 byte counts and the IEEE-754
arithmetic are fixed by their standards and will be identical on your machine, not merely close.
Those are labelled **derived**. See [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers) for what each class commits us to.
</Note>

## Set up before Monday

```bash
cc --version          # Apple clang 15+ or gcc 12+ / clang 16+ on Linux
make --version        # GNU make 3.81 (macOS) or 4.x
python3 --version     # 3.12+, for the plotting script only
```

On macOS: `xcode-select --install`. On Debian/Ubuntu: `sudo apt install build-essential gdb valgrind`.

<Warning>
**Valgrind does not run on Apple Silicon.** On an M-series Mac your memory-safety tooling is
`-fsanitize=address,undefined` plus `leaks -atExit`. Where a lab step calls for Valgrind, run
it in a Linux container (`docker run --rm -v "$PWD:/w" -w /w gcc:13 bash`) or use the
sanitizer path, which `make verify` selects automatically.
</Warning>

## What "done" looks like

- A public repo with `binviz`, the three sorts, the benchmark harness, the arena allocator and the log-log plot.
- A README that states your machine, your measured ns/alloc for `malloc` versus arena, and your two fitted exponents — with one paragraph explaining why merge sort's exponent is 1.06 and not 1.00.
- Zero findings under `-fsanitize=address,undefined` on every binary in the repo.
- One diagram redrawn from memory, photographed, and committed. Yes, really — it is the highest-retention hour of the week.
