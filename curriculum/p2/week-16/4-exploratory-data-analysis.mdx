## What you'll be able to do

1. Run a **repeatable EDA checklist** instead of poking at a dataset until something looks interesting.
2. Analyse a variable on its own, and against the target.
3. Measure association correctly — and know when correlation is **blind** to a strong relationship.
4. Detect multicollinearity and say what it does and does not break.
5. Find **leakage and class imbalance during EDA**, before you have wasted a week on a model.
6. Detect distribution shift with a statistical test, and know when that test is lying to you.
7. Write findings as a memo a stakeholder can act on.

    ## The idea in one paragraph

    Exploratory data analysis is the work you do *before* modelling, to find out what you actually
    have. It is not a phase you complete and tick off; it is the process of forming and killing
    hypotheses about the data. The failure mode is treating it as decoration — producing twelve plots
    because a tutorial did, looking at them, and moving on. **EDA is only useful if it changes what
    you do next**: a column you drop, a transformation you apply, a leak you find, a stakeholder
    conversation you have because the data cannot answer the question they asked.

    ### The analogy

    A mechanic looking over a car before quoting for a repair.

    - **They have a checklist**, and they run it in the same order every time. Not because every car
      has the same problem, but because a checklist is how you avoid missing the thing you were not
      thinking about.
    - **They look for what does not fit**: a new tyre on one corner, mismatched paint, a warning
      light. Anomalies relative to expectation, not anomalies in the abstract.
    - **They ask what the car is for.** A track car and a school run have different definitions of
      "fine".
    - **Then they write it down**, in language the owner can act on. "Your brake pads are at 2 mm;
      that is roughly 3,000 miles" — not a list of every measurement they took.

    EDA is that. **A checklist, run in order, looking for what does not fit, in the context of the
    decision, written up so someone can act.**

    ### Every term, defined

    - **Univariate analysis** — one variable at a time: distribution, centre, spread, missingness.
    - **Bivariate analysis** — two variables together, usually a feature against the target.
    - **Distribution** — how values are spread. Read from a histogram or an ECDF.
    - **Skew** — asymmetry. Income is right-skewed: a long tail of high values.
    - **Correlation (Pearson's r)** — how well a **straight line** fits two variables. −1 to +1.
      **Blind to anything that is not a line.**
    - **Spearman's ρ** — correlation of the *ranks*. Catches any monotonic relationship, still blind
      to a U shape.
    - **Mutual information** — how much knowing one variable tells you about the other. Catches
      **any** relationship, including non-monotonic ones.
    - **Multicollinearity** — features that are highly correlated with each other.
    - **Class imbalance** — one target class far rarer than another.
    - **Distribution shift / drift** — the data changing between two periods or two datasets.
    - **Target leakage** — a feature containing information about the target that would not be
      available at prediction time (module 3).
    - **ECDF** — empirical cumulative distribution function. Cumulative proportion below each value.
      **Better than a histogram** because it has no bin-width choice to bias it.

    ### The checklist

    Run it in this order, every time.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="1. Shape and types">
        `df.shape`, `df.dtypes`, `df.head()`. How many rows and columns? Is anything `object` that
        should be numeric or a date? **This catches the "one row contains 'N/A' so the whole column
        is a string" problem**, which is the most common data-loading failure there is.
      </Step>
      <Step title="2. Missingness">
        `df.isna().mean().sort_values(ascending=False)`. Which columns, how much, and — the part
        people skip — **is the missingness correlated with anything?** `df.groupby(df.col.isna()).mean()`
        tells you immediately whether it is MCAR or not.
      </Step>
      <Step title="3. Univariate">
        For numeric: `describe()`, plus a histogram and a boxplot. For categorical: `value_counts()`,
        and check the cardinality. **Look for impossible values** — negative ages, future dates,
        `-999` sentinels — and for a suspicious spike at one value, which usually means a default.
      </Step>
      <Step title="4. The target">
        Distribution of the target itself. **Class balance** if it is classification: a 1% positive
        rate changes your metric, your split and your loss function. Skew if it is regression.
      </Step>
      <Step title="5. Feature versus target">
        Each feature against the target. **And plot it** — a coefficient will not tell you the
        relationship is a U shape, and Layer 2 measures exactly how blind it is.
      </Step>
      <Step title="6. Feature versus feature">
        A correlation matrix for multicollinearity. Duplicated or near-duplicated columns. And
        **check for the ID column that correlates with the target**, which is the classic leak.
      </Step>
      <Step title="7. Leakage and drift">
        Any feature suspiciously predictive on its own? Does the train distribution match the
        holdout? **This is the step that saves a week**, and it is the one nobody runs.
      </Step>
    </Steps>

    ### The smallest working example

    ```python
    # eda.py — CPython 3.12, pandas 2.2.3
    import pandas as pd

    df = pd.read_parquet("txns.parquet")

    # 1. Shape and types
    print(df.shape)
    print(df.dtypes)

    # 2. Missingness
    miss = df.isna().mean().sort_values(ascending=False)
    print(miss[miss > 0])

    # 3. Univariate — numeric
    print(df.describe().T)
    # 3. Univariate — categorical, with cardinality
    for c in df.select_dtypes(["object", "category"]):
        print(f"{c}: {df[c].nunique()} distinct")
        print(df[c].value_counts(dropna=False).head())

    # 4. Target
    print(df.is_refund.value_counts(normalize=True))

    # 5. Feature vs target
    print(df.groupby("is_refund")["amount"].describe())

    # 6. Feature vs feature
    print(df.select_dtypes("number").corr().round(2))
    ```

    ### Line-by-line walkthrough

    - `df.isna().mean()` — the *proportion* missing, not the count. A proportion is comparable
      across columns and across datasets; a count is not.
    - `df.describe().T` — transposed, so each row is a variable. On a wide frame the untransposed
      version is unreadable and you will skip it, which defeats the point.
    - **`df[c].nunique()` before `value_counts()`.** A column with 400,000 distinct values is an
      identifier, and printing its value counts fills your screen with nothing. Cardinality tells you
      what kind of column it is.
    - `value_counts(dropna=False)` — **`dropna=False` is essential.** The default hides missing
      values, so a column that is 40% null looks complete.
    - `df.groupby("is_refund")["amount"].describe()` — the first genuinely analytical line. If
      refunds have a different amount distribution from non-refunds, `amount` predicts `is_refund`.
      **If the difference is enormous, ask why before you celebrate.**

    ### Reading a distribution

    ```python
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    import numpy as np

    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(14, 4))

    axes[0].hist(df.amount, bins=50)
    axes[0].set(title="raw — a spike and a long tail", xlabel="amount")

    axes[1].hist(np.log1p(df.amount), bins=50)
    axes[1].set(title="log1p — the shape becomes readable", xlabel="log(1+amount)")

    x = np.sort(df.amount.dropna())
    axes[2].plot(x, np.arange(1, len(x) + 1) / len(x))
    axes[2].set(title="ECDF — no bin choice to argue about", xlabel="amount", ylabel="F(x)")
    plt.tight_layout()
    ```

    Three views of one variable, and each answers a different question:

    - **The raw histogram** shows you the skew and any spike. A spike at exactly one value almost
      always means a default or a sentinel.
    - **The log histogram** makes a right-skewed distribution legible. If the log version is roughly
      symmetric, the variable is log-normal, which is true of income, transaction amounts, file
      sizes, city populations and session durations.
    - **The ECDF has no bins**, so it cannot mislead you through bin-width choice, and you can read
      any quantile straight off it. **Use the ECDF when you need to be careful and the histogram
      when you need to communicate.**

    <Warning>
    **A histogram's shape depends on its bin width**, and the default is a guess. Twenty bins and
    two hundred bins of the same data can look like different distributions — bimodality appears and
    disappears. Before concluding anything from a histogram's shape, redraw it with a different bin
    count. If the conclusion changes, it was a property of your bins.
    </Warning>

    ### Checkpoint — 5 questions before you continue

    <AccordionGroup>
      <Accordion title="1. Why run the checklist in a fixed order rather than exploring freely?">
        Because free exploration finds what you were already thinking about. A checklist finds what
        you were not — and it makes your EDA reproducible, comparable across datasets, and
        automatable. Interesting deviations are still worth chasing; the checklist is what you do
        *before* chasing, so you know what is normal.
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="2. Why is `value_counts(dropna=False)` important?">
        Because the default drops missing values, so a column that is 40% null shows complete-looking
        counts. Missing is a value in an exploratory context and you want to see it.
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="3. What does a correlation of 0.02 tell you?">
        That there is no *linear* relationship. It tells you **nothing at all** about non-linear
        ones. Layer 2 measures a clearly parabolic relationship with a Pearson r of −0.02 and a
        mutual information of 0.98. **Always plot before concluding from a coefficient.**
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="4. Why does class imbalance matter this early?">
        Because it changes three decisions you are about to make: your **metric** (accuracy is
        useless at a 1% positive rate — predicting "no" always gives 99%), your **split** (must be
        stratified, or a fold may contain no positives), and possibly your **loss** (class weights,
        or resampling). Discovering it after training wastes the training.
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="5. What is the single most useful EDA plot?">
        Each feature against the target, as a scatter for regression or grouped distributions for
        classification. It tells you whether a relationship exists, whether it is linear, and whether
        one feature is suspiciously perfect — which is leakage. **If you make one plot per feature,
        make this one.**
      </Accordion>
    </AccordionGroup>

    ## Correlation is blind, and here is how blind

    Four relationships between `x` and `y`, 5,000 points each:

    | Relationship | Pearson r | Spearman ρ | Mutual information |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | `y = 2x + noise` | **+0.9606** | +0.9632 | 1.2168 |
    | `y = x² + noise` | **−0.0218** | −0.0145 | **0.9824** |
    | `y = sin(3x) + noise` | **+0.2403** | +0.2445 | **0.9104** |
    | `y = noise` (no relationship) | +0.0154 | +0.0148 | **0.0106** |

    <Info>
    Method: `x ~ Uniform(−3, 3)`, n = 5,000, `scipy.stats` for the correlations and
    `sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression` for MI. Reproduce with `w16m4.py`.
    </Info>

    **The quadratic row is the one to remember.** A perfect parabola — a relationship so strong that
    knowing `x` almost determines `y` — has a Pearson correlation of **−0.02**, indistinguishable
    from the pure-noise row's +0.015. **If you filtered features by correlation you would have
    dropped it.**

    Spearman does not save you. It catches *monotonic* relationships, and a parabola is not
    monotonic — it goes down then up, and the two halves cancel. **Mutual information catches all of
    them**, at 0.98 for the parabola against 0.011 for noise, because it measures shared information
    rather than fitting a shape.

    The practical guidance:

    | Use | When | Blind to |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Pearson r** | you specifically want a *linear* relationship's strength and direction | everything non-linear |
    | **Spearman ρ** | monotonic but possibly curved (saturating, exponential) | non-monotonic (U shapes, periodicity) |
    | **Mutual information** | you want "is there *any* relationship?" | nothing — but it gives no direction and no shape |
    | **A scatter plot** | **always, first** | nothing; it is the only one that shows you *what* the relationship is |

    **MI's cost:** it is estimated (k-nearest-neighbour based in scikit-learn), has no natural upper
    bound so values are only comparable within a dataset, is slower, and gives no direction. Use it
    to *screen*, then plot the survivors.

    ## Finding leakage during EDA

    ```python
    from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
    from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
    import pandas as pd, numpy as np

    def single_feature_auc(df: pd.DataFrame, target: str) -> pd.Series:
        """Any single feature scoring above ~0.95 alone is almost certainly leakage.
        A depth-3 tree, so this measures the FEATURE, not a model's capacity."""
        y = df[target]
        scores = {}
        for col in df.columns.drop(target):
            X = df[[col]]
            if X[col].dtype == object or str(X[col].dtype) == "category":
                X = X[col].astype("category").cat.codes.to_frame()
            X = X.fillna(-999)
            scores[col] = cross_val_score(
                DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=0),
                X, y, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc").mean()
        return pd.Series(scores).sort_values(ascending=False)

    auc = single_feature_auc(df, "churned")
    print(auc.head(10))
    suspicious = auc[auc > 0.95]
    if len(suspicious):
        print(f"LEAKAGE SUSPECTS:\n{suspicious}")
    ```

    **A depth-3 decision tree on one feature reaching 0.95+ AUC means that feature nearly determines
    the target.** For a genuinely hard problem — churn, readmission, default — that is not a great
    feature; it is a feature that contains the answer.

    Three more checks worth running at EDA time:

    ```python
    # 1. Is any feature a deterministic function of the target?
    for col in df.columns.drop(target):
        if df.groupby(col)[target].nunique().max() == 1:
            print(f"{col}: every value maps to exactly one target class")

    # 2. Does any entity appear more than once? (Group leakage — module 3, +0.148 AUC)
    for col in df.select_dtypes(["object", "category", "int"]):
        if 1 < df[col].nunique() < len(df) * 0.9:
            dupe_rate = 1 - df[col].nunique() / len(df)
            if dupe_rate > 0.05:
                print(f"{col}: {dupe_rate:.0%} repeat rate — candidate grouping key")

    # 3. Are there exact duplicate rows?
    print(f"exact duplicate rows: {df.duplicated().sum()}")
    ```

    ## Distribution shift, and the trap in the test

    ```python
    from scipy import stats

    def drift_report(reference: pd.DataFrame, current: pd.DataFrame,
                     alpha: float = 0.01, min_effect: float = 0.1) -> pd.DataFrame:
        """Two-sample KS per numeric column, with BOTH a significance gate
        and an effect-size gate. The second gate is the important one."""
        rows = []
        for col in reference.select_dtypes("number").columns:
            a = reference[col].dropna()
            b = current[col].dropna()
            if len(a) < 30 or len(b) < 30:
                continue
            stat, p = stats.ks_2samp(a, b)
            rows.append({
                "column": col,
                "ks_statistic": stat,          # max gap between the two ECDFs, 0..1
                "p_value": p,
                "significant": p < alpha,
                "material": stat > min_effect,
                "drifted": (p < alpha) and (stat > min_effect),
                "ref_mean": a.mean(), "cur_mean": b.mean(),
                "shift_in_sd": (b.mean() - a.mean()) / a.std(),
            })
        return pd.DataFrame(rows).sort_values("ks_statistic", ascending=False)
    ```

    **Why the second gate exists.** Measured detection rates for a shift of just **0.01 standard
    deviations** — a difference far too small to affect any model:

    | Samples per side | Flagged at p &lt; 0.01 |
    | --- | --- |
    | 1,000 | 0% |
    | 10,000 | 4% |
    | 100,000 | **28%** |
    | 1,000,000 | **100%** |

    <Info>
    Method: two-sample KS test, 100 repetitions per cell, `N(0,1)` against `N(0.01, 1)`. The
    false-positive rate on genuinely identical distributions was 0–1% throughout, so the test is
    correctly calibrated — it is doing exactly what it is supposed to. Reproduce with `w16m4.py`.
    </Info>

    **At a million rows per side, a difference of one hundredth of a standard deviation is flagged
    every single time.** The test is not broken. It is answering "are these samples from *exactly*
    the same distribution?", and at large n it can answer that with great confidence for differences
    of no practical consequence.

    **This is the single most important thing to understand about drift monitoring**, and it is why
    week 32's production monitoring cannot be "alert when p &lt; 0.05". A p-value at large n is a
    statement about your sample size as much as about your data. **Gate on the effect size** — the
    KS statistic, which is the maximum gap between the two ECDFs and is directly interpretable — and
    use the p-value only to rule out coincidence.

    For calibration, the same test's power on shifts that *do* matter:

    | True shift | n=1,000 | n=10,000 | n=100,000 |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | 0.05 SD | 4.5% | 65.5% | 100% |
    | 0.10 SD | 23.5% | 100% | 100% |
    | 0.25 SD | 99.0% | 100% | 100% |
    | 0.50 SD | 100% | 100% | 100% |

    **At n = 1,000 you cannot reliably detect a 0.1 SD shift** (23.5%). At n = 100,000 you detect
    everything, including things you should ignore. **The useful window is narrow and it depends on
    your sample size**, which is an argument for fixing the sample size in your monitoring rather
    than letting it grow with traffic.

    ## Reference implementation: a reusable EDA library

    ```python
    # edalib.py — pandas 2.2.3, scipy 1.14.1
    from __future__ import annotations
    from dataclasses import dataclass, field

    import numpy as np
    import pandas as pd
    from scipy import stats


    @dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
    class ColumnProfile:
        name: str
        dtype: str
        n_missing: int
        pct_missing: float
        n_unique: int
        cardinality_ratio: float
        sample_values: tuple
        stats: dict = field(default_factory=dict)

        @property
        def looks_like_identifier(self) -> bool:
            return self.cardinality_ratio > 0.95

        @property
        def looks_like_constant(self) -> bool:
            return self.n_unique <= 1


    def profile_column(s: pd.Series) -> ColumnProfile:
        n = len(s)
        nu = int(s.nunique(dropna=True))
        st: dict = {}
        if pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(s) and s.notna().any():
            d = s.dropna()
            st = {
                "mean": float(d.mean()), "std": float(d.std()),
                "min": float(d.min()), "max": float(d.max()),
                "p01": float(d.quantile(0.01)), "p50": float(d.median()),
                "p99": float(d.quantile(0.99)),
                "skew": float(d.skew()), "zeros": int((d == 0).sum()),
                "negatives": int((d < 0).sum()),
            }
        return ColumnProfile(
            name=str(s.name), dtype=str(s.dtype),
            n_missing=int(s.isna().sum()),
            pct_missing=float(s.isna().mean()),
            n_unique=nu,
            cardinality_ratio=nu / n if n else 0.0,
            sample_values=tuple(s.dropna().unique()[:5]),
            stats=st,
        )


    def find_issues(df: pd.DataFrame, target: str | None = None) -> list[str]:
        """The list a human reads. Ordered by how much it should worry them."""
        issues: list[str] = []

        dupes = int(df.duplicated().sum())
        if dupes:
            issues.append(f"CRITICAL: {dupes:,} exact duplicate rows "
                          f"({dupes/len(df):.1%}) — random splits will leak (module 3: +0.148 AUC)")

        for col in df.columns:
            p = profile_column(df[col])
            if p.looks_like_constant:
                issues.append(f"DROP: '{col}' is constant — no information")
            elif p.pct_missing > 0.5:
                issues.append(f"WARN: '{col}' is {p.pct_missing:.0%} missing — "
                              f"consider dropping the values, keeping an indicator")
            if p.looks_like_identifier and p.dtype != "float64":
                issues.append(f"CHECK: '{col}' is {p.cardinality_ratio:.0%} unique — "
                              f"an identifier? If entities repeat, use GroupKFold")
            if p.stats.get("negatives", 0) and any(
                    k in col.lower() for k in ("amount", "price", "count", "age", "qty")):
                issues.append(f"CHECK: '{col}' has {p.stats['negatives']:,} negative values")
            if abs(p.stats.get("skew", 0.0)) > 3:
                issues.append(f"NOTE: '{col}' skew {p.stats['skew']:.1f} — consider log1p")

        if target and target in df.columns:
            vc = df[target].value_counts(normalize=True)
            if len(vc) <= 20 and vc.min() < 0.05:
                issues.append(f"IMBALANCE: rarest class of '{target}' is {vc.min():.2%} — "
                              f"stratify the split, and do not use accuracy")
        return issues


    def drift(reference: pd.DataFrame, current: pd.DataFrame,
              alpha: float = 0.01, min_ks: float = 0.1) -> pd.DataFrame:
        """Significance AND effect size. See Layer 2 for why the second gate matters."""
        out = []
        for col in reference.select_dtypes("number").columns:
            if col not in current:
                out.append({"column": col, "note": "MISSING from current"})
                continue
            a, b = reference[col].dropna(), current[col].dropna()
            if len(a) < 30 or len(b) < 30:
                continue
            stat, p = stats.ks_2samp(a, b)
            out.append({
                "column": col, "ks": stat, "p": p,
                "shift_sd": (b.mean() - a.mean()) / (a.std() or np.nan),
                "drifted": bool(p < alpha and stat > min_ks),
            })
        return pd.DataFrame(out).sort_values("ks", ascending=False, na_position="last")
    ```

    ```python
    # test_edalib.py — pytest 8.x
    import numpy as np
    import pandas as pd
    from edalib import profile_column, find_issues, drift


    def test_identifier_is_flagged():
        s = pd.Series([f"id{i}" for i in range(1000)])
        assert profile_column(s).looks_like_identifier


    def test_constant_column_is_flagged_for_dropping():
        df = pd.DataFrame({"a": [1] * 100, "b": range(100)})
        assert any("constant" in i for i in find_issues(df))


    def test_duplicates_are_critical():
        df = pd.concat([pd.DataFrame({"a": range(50)})] * 2, ignore_index=True)
        issues = find_issues(df)
        assert issues[0].startswith("CRITICAL")


    def test_class_imbalance_is_reported():
        df = pd.DataFrame({"x": range(1000), "y": [0] * 990 + [1] * 10})
        assert any("IMBALANCE" in i for i in find_issues(df, target="y"))


    def test_drift_ignores_a_tiny_shift_at_large_n():
        """The effect-size gate. Without it, this is flagged 100% of the time."""
        rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
        ref = pd.DataFrame({"x": rng.standard_normal(200_000)})
        cur = pd.DataFrame({"x": rng.standard_normal(200_000) + 0.01})
        r = drift(ref, cur)
        assert not r.drifted.any(), "a 0.01 SD shift must not be reported as drift"


    def test_drift_detects_a_real_shift():
        rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
        ref = pd.DataFrame({"x": rng.standard_normal(20_000)})
        cur = pd.DataFrame({"x": rng.standard_normal(20_000) + 1.0})
        assert drift(ref, cur).drifted.all()
    ```

    <Check>
    `test_drift_ignores_a_tiny_shift_at_large_n` is the test that encodes the module's most
    important finding. Without the `min_ks` gate, a shift of one hundredth of a standard deviation
    at 200,000 rows per side is flagged as drift **every single time**. A monitor that fires daily
    on differences of no consequence is a monitor that gets muted within a fortnight, and then it is
    not there when something real happens. **Alert fatigue is a correctness problem**, and the test
    is how you keep it out.
    </Check>

    ## Trade-offs table

    | Situation | Choose | Instead of | Because |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Screening for *any* relationship | mutual information | Pearson r | measured 0.98 vs −0.02 on a parabola |
    | You need direction and a linear effect size | Pearson r | MI | MI has no sign and no natural scale |
    | Monotonic but curved | Spearman ρ | Pearson r | rank-based; robust to outliers and to the shape |
    | **Understanding the relationship** | **a scatter plot** | any coefficient | only the plot tells you *what* the relationship is |
    | Comparing distributions carefully | ECDF | histogram | no bin-width choice to bias the shape |
    | Communicating a distribution | histogram | ECDF | more people read it correctly |
    | Drift detection, large samples | KS statistic with an effect-size floor | p-value alone | measured: at n=10⁶ a 0.01 SD shift is flagged 100% of the time |
    | Drift on a categorical | population stability index, or a chi-squared test | KS | KS is for continuous distributions |
    | First look at an unknown dataset | the checklist | free exploration | you find what you were not looking for |
    | A 40-column dataset for the tenth time | an automated profile | the manual checklist | consistency and speed; but read Layer 3 on its blind spots |

    ## What breaks in the real world

    A retail forecasting team retrained a demand model quarterly. Their EDA compared the new quarter
    against the previous one with a KS test per feature at `p < 0.05`, and blocked retraining if any
    feature drifted.

    Traffic grew. Each quarter's sample went from ~50,000 rows to ~2,000,000.

    **The drift check began firing on every feature, every quarter.** Not because the data changed
    materially — mean shifts were on the order of 0.02 standard deviations — but because at two
    million rows the KS test detects a 0.01 SD difference essentially always.

    The team did what teams do: they raised the threshold to `p < 0.001`. It kept firing. They
    raised it to `p < 1e-10`. It kept firing, because the p-values were `0.0` to floating-point
    precision.

    **So they disabled the check.**

    Four months later a supplier changed its product-code scheme and one feature's distribution
    changed genuinely and substantially — a KS statistic around 0.4. Nothing fired. The model
    retrained on shifted data, forecast accuracy fell 12%, and it was found by a category manager
    noticing that the reorder suggestions had become strange.

    The fix was to gate on the effect size rather than significance:

    ```python
    # Before: significance only. Fires on everything at scale.
    drifted = p_value < 0.05

    # After: BOTH, and the effect size is the one that matters.
    drifted = (p_value < 0.01) and (ks_statistic > 0.1)
    ```

    They also fixed the sampling: **draw a fixed 50,000-row sample from each period** rather than
    using everything. That holds the test's sensitivity constant as traffic grows, which is what you
    want from a monitor — a threshold that means the same thing next year as it does today.

    What generalises:

    - **A statistical test's sensitivity scales with sample size, and a monitor's should not.** If
      your alerting threshold has to be re-tuned as you grow, it is measuring your growth.
    - **The dangerous outcome of a noisy alert is not the noise — it is the disabling.** The check
      was correct in principle and was removed because it was unusable in practice. **An alert
      people mute is worse than no alert**, because it creates the belief that the thing is
      monitored.
    - **"Is this difference real?" and "is this difference material?" are different questions.**
      Significance answers the first. Only an effect size answers the second, and only the second
      determines whether you should act.

    ### Design checkpoint

    <Accordion title="You are handed a 40-column dataset and asked 'can we predict churn?'. You have four hours before the meeting. What do you do, and what do you say if the answer is no?">
    **Model answer.**

    **Spend the first thirty minutes not touching the data.** Ask: what decision does the prediction
    drive? What action is taken on a predicted churner? What is the cost of a false positive versus a
    false negative? **What is the prediction point in time?** These determine everything — most
    importantly the last one, because it determines which of the 40 columns you are allowed to use.

    **Hour 1: the checklist, mechanically.** Shape, dtypes, missingness, univariate distributions,
    target balance. Write down anything impossible. Expect to find: two columns that are constants,
    one identifier, three columns over 60% missing, and at least one date stored as text.

    **Hour 2: leakage, before anything else.** Single-feature AUC across all 40 columns. Anything
    above 0.95 gets a temporal audit — when is this field populated? In a churn dataset the usual
    suspects are `cancellation_reason`, `final_invoice_date`, `account_status`, and any
    `days_since_*`. **This hour is what separates a useful meeting from an embarrassing one four
    weeks later.** Also: check whether a customer appears more than once, because that decides
    whether you need `GroupKFold`.

    **Hour 3: does signal exist at all?** Mutual information of each remaining feature against the
    target, then plot the top ten. Then a deliberately dumb baseline: logistic regression on the five
    features that survive, with proper CV. **A number, however modest, is worth far more than a
    promise.**

    **Hour 4: write the memo.** One page. Findings, risks, recommendation.

    **If the answer is no — and it often is — say so precisely.** "No" is not one answer; it is four,
    and they have different remedies:

    - **"The data cannot support it."** Only 340 churn events; a model needs roughly 10–20 events per
      feature. *Remedy: collect more, or wait.*
    - **"The signal is not in this data."** The features are all account metadata; churn is likely
      driven by product usage and support interactions, which are not here. *Remedy: get those
      tables.*
    - **"The label is wrong."** "Churn" is defined as a cancelled subscription, but 40% of those are
      administrative reissues rather than real departures. *Remedy: fix the definition first — and
      this is the most common answer.*
    - **"It is predictable, but not usefully."** AUC 0.71, and at a 3% base rate the top decile
      contains 9% churners. Whether that is useful depends entirely on what the intervention costs
      and what a retained customer is worth — **which is a question for them, not for you.**

    **The framing that makes a "no" land well:** bring the arithmetic. "At 0.71 AUC, contacting the
    top 10% reaches 30% of the churners. If retention outreach costs £4 per customer and a retained
    customer is worth £200, that is profitable at a 2% conversion rate. Here is the break-even." **A
    "no" with a decision table is a contribution. A "no" without one is a disappointment.**

    ## EDA as hypothesis generation, and the multiple-comparisons problem

    Looking at 40 features against a target is running 40 hypothesis tests. At `α = 0.05`, on data
    with **no signal whatsoever**, you expect two "significant" results.

    ```python
    import numpy as np
    from scipy import stats

    counts, mins = [], []
    for trial in range(1000):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(trial)
        y = rng.standard_normal(200)
        ps = [stats.pearsonr(rng.standard_normal(200), y)[1] for _ in range(40)]
        counts.append(sum(v < 0.05 for v in ps))
        mins.append(min(ps))

    c = np.array(counts)
    print(f"mean number significant at p<0.05: {c.mean():.2f}   (expected 40 * 0.05 = 2.0)")
    print(f"at least one significant:  {(c > 0).mean():.0%} of trials")
    print(f"three or more significant: {(c >= 3).mean():.0%} of trials")
    print(f"median smallest p-value:   {np.median(mins):.4f}")
    ```

    ```
    mean number significant at p<0.05: 2.00   (expected 40 * 0.05 = 2.0)
    at least one significant:  88% of trials
    three or more significant: 32% of trials
    median smallest p-value:   0.0178
    ```

    **On forty features of pure noise, you find at least one "significant" result 88% of the time,
    and three or more 32% of the time.** The median smallest p-value is 0.018 — comfortably
    "significant" by any conventional standard, on data containing nothing. And EDA makes this worse than a
    pre-registered analysis, because you do not run 40 tests — you look at plots, notice patterns,
    and test the ones that looked promising. **The number of implicit comparisons is unbounded and
    unrecorded.**

    This is the same mechanism as module 3's feature-selection leak, measured there at **+0.356
    accuracy on pure noise with 20,000 candidates**. It is the multiple-comparisons problem wearing
    two different hats.

    The defences, in increasing order of rigour:

    | Defence | How | Cost |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Hold out a confirmation set** | EDA on 70%; never look at the other 30% until the end | you lose 30% of your data for exploration |
    | **Bonferroni** | require `p < α/m` for m tests | very conservative; kills real weak signals |
    | **Benjamini–Hochberg FDR** | control the *proportion* of false discoveries | **the right default for exploratory work** |
    | **Pre-register** | write down the hypotheses before looking | impossible for genuine exploration, which is the point of exploration |
    | **Treat EDA output as hypotheses, not findings** | every EDA claim is provisional until tested on held-out data | free, and it is mostly a matter of how you write it up |

    **The last row is the practical answer.** EDA generates hypotheses. It does not confirm them.
    Write your memo in those terms — "amount appears bimodal, which suggests two customer
    populations; worth testing" rather than "there are two customer populations" — and you will be
    right more often and trusted more.

    ## Automated profiling: what it is for and what it misses

    `ydata-profiling` (formerly pandas-profiling) generates a full HTML report from one line. It is
    genuinely useful and it has specific blind spots.

    | It finds | It misses |
    | --- | --- |
    | missing values, cardinality, dtypes | **domain-impossible values** — it does not know an age of 199 is wrong |
    | distributions, skew, outliers by IQR | **temporal leakage** — it has no concept of when a field is populated |
    | pairwise correlations | **non-linear relationships**, unless it computes MI |
    | duplicate rows | **near-duplicate entities** — "Ann Patel" and "ann patel" |
    | constant and highly-correlated columns | **whether the relationship makes sense** |
    | | **the question you are trying to answer** |

    **The cost is the other consideration.** `ydata-profiling` is `O(n·p²)` in the correlation
    matrix and computes several correlation types plus interaction plots. On a wide frame it is slow
    enough that people stop running it. Measured on a 1,000,000-row, 21-column frame:

    | Check | Time |
    | --- | --- |
    | Targeted checks (shape, nulls, describe, nunique, Pearson correlation) | **1.38 s** |
    | Pearson correlation matrix alone | 0.46 s |
    | **Spearman** correlation matrix alone | **3.06 s (7× Pearson)** |
    | `nunique` on all columns | 0.61 s |

    <Info>
    Method: pandas 2.2.3, M3 Pro, 1,000,000 rows × 21 columns. Reproduce with `w16m4.py`.
    </Info>

    **Spearman is 7× the cost of Pearson**, because it must rank every column first. On a wide frame
    that is the dominant cost of a profile report, and it is worth knowing before you enable "all
    correlations" on a 200-column dataset.

    **The right pattern: a fast targeted profile you run every time, plus the full report
    occasionally.** 1.4 seconds runs in CI on every data update; a 4-minute report does not.

    ## Drift detection beyond KS

    | Method | Data type | Detects | Limitation |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Kolmogorov–Smirnov** | continuous | any distributional difference; most sensitive near the median | over-sensitive at large n; **weak in the tails**, which is where drift often is |
    | **Anderson–Darling** | continuous | as KS, but **weighted toward the tails** | less well known; a better default for drift |
    | **Wasserstein distance** | continuous | the "cost" of moving one distribution onto the other | an interpretable *magnitude*, not a test; **use it as the effect size** |
    | **Population Stability Index** | binned, either type | shifts in binned proportions | depends on bin choice; conventions are 0.1 = moderate, 0.25 = major |
    | **Chi-squared** | categorical | frequency changes | same large-n over-sensitivity |
    | **Domain classifier** | anything | **any** difference, including multivariate | needs training; but AUC is a beautifully interpretable effect size |

    **The domain classifier is the underused one and it deserves more attention.** Train a model to
    distinguish reference rows from current rows:

    ```python
    from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
    from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
    import numpy as np, pandas as pd

    def drift_auc(reference: pd.DataFrame, current: pd.DataFrame) -> tuple[float, pd.Series]:
        """AUC ~= 0.5 means indistinguishable. AUC >> 0.5 means drift,
        and feature importances tell you WHICH columns moved."""
        X = pd.concat([reference, current], ignore_index=True)
        y = np.r_[np.zeros(len(reference)), np.ones(len(current))]
        clf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, max_depth=6, random_state=0)
        auc = cross_val_score(clf, X, y, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc").mean()
        clf.fit(X, y)
        return auc, pd.Series(clf.feature_importances_, index=X.columns).sort_values(
            ascending=False)
    ```

    Why it is better than per-column tests:

    - **AUC is an effect size on a bounded, interpretable scale.** 0.5 is identical, 1.0 is trivially
      separable. **No threshold tuning as sample size grows** — which is precisely the failure in the
      war story.
    - **It catches multivariate drift** that per-column tests cannot. If `age` and `income` each keep
      their marginal distributions but their *correlation* reverses, every univariate test passes and
      the classifier finds it immediately.
    - **Feature importances tell you which columns moved**, so the alert is actionable.

    Its cost is that it needs training, and on high-dimensional data a flexible classifier can
    separate on noise — so cross-validate it and treat AUC below about 0.55 as "no drift".

    ## Class imbalance: what it actually changes

    At a 1% positive rate:

    | Consequence | Detail |
    | --- | --- |
    | **Accuracy is useless** | "always negative" scores 99%. Never report accuracy on imbalanced data |
    | **ROC-AUC is optimistic** | it uses the false-positive *rate*, and with 99% negatives even a large absolute number of false positives is a small rate. **Use precision-recall AUC** |
    | **Splits must be stratified** | a random 5-fold on 100 positives may give a fold with 12 positives and one with 28 |
    | **CV variance explodes** | with 100 positives, each fold has 20; one unusual case moves the metric visibly |
    | **The threshold matters more than the model** | 0.5 is arbitrary and almost always wrong here. **This is week 19's subject** |
    | **Resampling is usually not the answer** | SMOTE and class weights change the predicted *probabilities*, which breaks calibration. Often better: keep the data, move the threshold |

    **The rule of thumb worth carrying: you need roughly 10–20 positive events per feature.** 340
    churn events supports perhaps 20–30 features, not 200. If you have fewer, the honest answer is
    regularisation and fewer features, not a more sophisticated model.

    ## Production failure modes

    | Failure mode | Signature | Detection |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Drift alert fires on everything | significance-only threshold; sample grew | gate on effect size (KS statistic, Wasserstein, classifier AUC); fix the sample size |
    | Drift alert disabled because it was noisy | no alerts at all; real drift missed for months | effect-size gate; alert on the *absence* of checks running |
    | Feature dropped because correlation was low | a strong non-linear relationship discarded — measured r = −0.02 with MI = 0.98 | mutual information for screening; plot the top candidates |
    | Multivariate drift missed | every univariate test passes; model degrades | domain-classifier AUC |
    | EDA "findings" that do not replicate | multiple comparisons over an unbounded number of implicit tests | hold out a confirmation set; report findings as hypotheses |
    | Accuracy reported on imbalanced data | 99% accuracy, useless model | precision-recall AUC; report the base rate alongside every metric |
    | Unstratified split with rare positives | wild CV variance; a fold with no positives | `StratifiedKFold`, always, for classification |
    | Profile report too slow to run | nobody runs it; drift found by a stakeholder | fast targeted checks in CI (1.4 s), full report on demand |
    | Histogram shape drives a conclusion | bimodality that is a bin-width artifact | ECDF, or redraw with three bin counts |
    | Leakage found after modelling | a week wasted, and a difficult conversation | single-feature AUC as step 7 of the checklist, before any modelling |

    ## Frontier and research pointers

    - **Tukey (1977), *Exploratory Data Analysis*.** — **Takeaway:** the book that named the field
      and made the case that looking at data is a discipline with methods, not a preliminary to the
      real statistics; the boxplot and the stem-and-leaf plot are both from here.
    - **Anscombe (1973), "Graphs in Statistical Analysis."** *The American Statistician* 27(1). —
      **Takeaway:** four datasets with identical means, variances, correlations and regression lines
      that look completely different when plotted; **the two-page paper behind "always plot it"**,
      and Layer 2's parabola is the same lesson with a number attached.
    - **Matejka & Fitzmaurice (2017), "Same Stats, Different Graphs."** CHI. — **Takeaway:** the
      Datasaurus Dozen — an algorithm that generates arbitrarily many datasets with identical
      summary statistics, including one shaped like a dinosaur; Anscombe generalised and made
      unavoidable.
    - **Benjamini & Hochberg (1995), "Controlling the False Discovery Rate."** *JRSS B* 57(1). —
      **Takeaway:** controlling the *proportion* of false discoveries rather than the probability of
      any false discovery is far more powerful and is the right correction for exploratory work.
    - **Rabanser, Günnemann & Lipton (2019), "Failing Loudly: An Empirical Study of Methods for
      Detecting Dataset Shift."** NeurIPS. — **Takeaway:** across many shift types and detectors, a
      **domain classifier** and dimensionality-reduction-plus-test were the most reliable; per-feature
      univariate tests performed worst, which is the argument for the classifier approach above.
    - **Gama et al. (2014), "A Survey on Concept Drift Adaptation."** *ACM Computing Surveys* 46(4).
      — **Takeaway:** the taxonomy separating *covariate* shift (features move) from *concept* drift
      (the relationship between features and target moves); **only the second necessarily requires
      retraining**, and conflating them is why teams retrain too often.

    ## Stretch challenge

    **Build a drift detector that does not need re-tuning as your data grows.**

    The war story's failure was structural: a threshold that means one thing at 50,000 rows and
    something else at 2,000,000. Build the version that does not have that property, and prove it.

    Build:

    1. **Four detectors** behind one interface: per-column KS with an effect-size gate, Wasserstein
       distance, Population Stability Index, and a domain classifier.
    2. **A calibration harness.** For each detector, inject known shifts — mean shift, variance
       change, a new category, a correlation reversal, a proportion of outliers — at known magnitudes,
       across sample sizes from 10³ to 10⁷. **Produce a detection-probability surface: shift
       magnitude against sample size, per detector.**
    3. **A false-positive study.** Run each detector on genuinely identical distributions across the
       same sample sizes. **Any detector whose false-positive rate rises with n is disqualified**,
       and demonstrating that is the point.
    4. **A recommended configuration** with a defended threshold, and an argument for why it is
       stable as n grows.

    The open questions, which are the actual research:

    - **Which detector has a detection probability that depends on the shift and *not* on n?** My
      prediction is the domain classifier and Wasserstein, because both produce a bounded effect size
      — but the classifier may separate on noise at high dimension, so measure it.
    - **What is the smallest shift that matters?** This cannot be answered statistically; it depends
      on the downstream model. **Measure it directly**: train a model, inject shifts of increasing
      magnitude, and record where its metric degrades by more than 1%. **That is your threshold**,
      and deriving it this way rather than from a convention is the genuinely original contribution.
    - **Can you detect the shift that matters and ignore the one that does not?** Only *concept*
      drift — a change in the feature-target relationship — necessarily requires retraining.
      Covariate drift may be harmless. Can you distinguish them without labels for the current
      period? (Partially: compare the classifier's importances against the model's.)
    - **What is the right monitoring cadence and sample size?** Fixing the sample at 50,000 stabilises
      sensitivity. But how often, and does a sliding window beat fixed periods for detecting gradual
      drift?

    A calibrated drift detector with a published detection surface, and a threshold derived from
    measured model degradation rather than from convention, is a genuinely useful piece of ML
    infrastructure. **Bring it to week 32** — it is exactly what production monitoring needs, and
    almost every team builds the version that gets muted.

| `value_counts()` prints 400,000 lines | It is an identifier column | Check `nunique()` first |
| Leakage discovered after a week of modelling | No leakage step in the checklist | Single-feature AUC as step 7, before any modelling |
| Profile report is never run | It takes four minutes on a wide frame | Fast targeted checks (1.4 s) in CI; full report on demand |

## Sources & further reading

**Primary docs**

- [`scipy.stats.ks_2samp`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy-1.14.1/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ks_2samp.html) (SciPy 1.14.1) — read this for what the statistic is (the maximum gap between the two ECDFs) and the `method` parameter; the statistic is your effect size and it is directly interpretable.
- [`scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy-1.14.1/reference/generated/scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance.html) — read this as the effect-size alternative to a p-value; it is in the units of your variable, which makes thresholds meaningful.
- [`sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression`](https://scikit-learn.org/1.6/modules/generated/sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression.html) (1.6.0) — read this for the k-nearest-neighbour estimator and the note that values have no natural upper bound, so they are only comparable within a dataset.
- [`ydata-profiling`](https://docs.profiling.ydata.ai/) — read the configuration docs for `minimal=True`, which skips the expensive correlations; on a wide frame that is the difference between a report people run and one they do not.
- [`evidently`](https://docs.evidentlyai.com/) — read the data-drift presets for the detector-per-datatype defaults; it is a good survey of what the field considers reasonable, and its thresholds are worth arguing with.

**Papers**

- [Anscombe (1973), *Graphs in Statistical Analysis*, The American Statistician 27(1), 17–21.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.1973.10478966) — **Takeaway:** four datasets with identical summary statistics and completely different shapes; two pages, and it is the origin of "always plot it".
- [Matejka & Fitzmaurice (2017), *Same Stats, Different Graphs*, CHI.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025912) — **Takeaway:** you can generate arbitrarily many datasets with identical statistics and any shape you like, including a dinosaur; Anscombe made unavoidable.
- [Benjamini & Hochberg (1995), *Controlling the False Discovery Rate*, JRSS B 57(1), 289–300.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x) — **Takeaway:** controlling the proportion of false discoveries is far more powerful than Bonferroni and is the right correction for exploratory analysis.
- [Rabanser, Günnemann & Lipton (2019), *Failing Loudly: An Empirical Study of Methods for Detecting Dataset Shift*, NeurIPS.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11953) — **Takeaway:** across shift types, a domain classifier was among the most reliable detectors and per-feature univariate tests among the worst — the empirical basis for the recommendation in Layer 3.
- [Gama, Žliobaitė, Bifet, Pechenizkiy & Bouchachia (2014), *A Survey on Concept Drift Adaptation*, ACM Computing Surveys 46(4).](https://doi.org/10.1145/2523813) — **Takeaway:** covariate shift and concept drift are different things requiring different responses, and conflating them causes unnecessary retraining.

**Go deeper**

- John Tukey, *Exploratory Data Analysis* (1977) — the founding text; read chapters 1–3 for the philosophy, which is that looking at data is a method with its own discipline rather than a preliminary to statistics.
- [Claus Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization* (2019)](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/) — free online. Read chapters 5–7 for distribution visualisation; the treatment of why histograms mislead and when to use an ECDF is the best available.
- Chip Huyen, *Designing Machine Learning Systems* (2022), **Chapter 8 (Data Distribution Shifts and Monitoring)** — the production framing of everything in Layer 3, and the direct bridge to week 32.
