    ## The idea in one paragraph

    A chart is an argument. It takes numbers and turns them into a visual claim, and the reader
    absorbs that claim in about two seconds without checking it. That makes charts powerful and it
    makes them easy to mislead with — usually by accident, because the defaults in every plotting
    library will happily produce something that overstates your result. **Learning to make good
    charts is mostly learning to audit your own**: what does this picture claim, is the claim true,
    and would someone reach the same conclusion from the numbers?

    ### The analogy

    A chart is a sentence about data.

    - **"Sales grew"** is a claim. A line going up is the same claim, made faster and more
      convincingly.
    - **"Sales grew enormously"** is a stronger claim. Cutting the y-axis so the line goes from the
      bottom-left corner to the top-right makes that claim, whether or not the growth was enormous.
    - **The reader cannot easily fact-check a picture.** They can question "enormously" in a
      sentence. They will not think to check the axis on a chart, because charts feel like data
      rather than like assertions.

    **So the standard is higher, not lower.** You are making a claim in a medium the reader will not
    audit.

    ### Every term, defined

    - **Figure** — the whole canvas, the thing that becomes a file.
    - **Axes** — one plot within the figure. Confusingly named: an `Axes` object is a *panel*, not a
      pair of axis lines. A figure can hold many.
    - **Axis** — one of the x or y scales on an `Axes`.
    - **`plt` (pyplot)** — the stateful interface. `plt.plot()` draws on whatever is "current".
      Convenient, and unpredictable in a script.
    - **Object-oriented interface** — `fig, ax = plt.subplots()`, then `ax.plot(...)`. Explicit.
      **Use this.**
    - **Small multiples** — the same chart repeated for each subgroup, on shared axes. Often the
      single best answer to "how do these groups differ?".
    - **Log scale** — an axis where equal distances mean equal *ratios*. Essential for skewed data.
    - **Overplotting** — so many points that they cover each other and structure vanishes.
    - **Colorblind-safe palette** — one distinguishable to people with colour vision deficiency,
      which is about **8% of men** and 0.5% of women.
    - **Truncated axis** — an axis not starting at zero. **A lie in a bar chart, often fine in a
      line chart.**
    - **Chart junk** — decoration that carries no information: 3-D effects, gradients, background
      images, unnecessary gridlines.

    ### The figure/axes model

    ```python
    # plotting.py — CPython 3.12, matplotlib 3.9.3
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    import numpy as np

    x = np.linspace(0, 10, 100)

    # DON'T: stateful. Which figure did that draw on?
    plt.plot(x, np.sin(x))
    plt.title("sin")

    # DO: explicit objects. Every call names its target.
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4))
    ax.plot(x, np.sin(x), label="sin")
    ax.plot(x, np.cos(x), label="cos", linestyle="--")
    ax.set(xlabel="x", ylabel="value", title="Two waves")
    ax.legend()
    fig.savefig("waves.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
    plt.close(fig)
    ```

    ### Line-by-line walkthrough

    - `fig, ax = plt.subplots()` — creates a figure and one axes, and hands you both. **Every
      subsequent call names its target explicitly**, so there is no ambiguity about where anything
      is drawn.
    - `figsize=(6, 4)` — in inches. Combined with `dpi`, this determines the pixel size. **Set both
      deliberately**: a chart sized for a slide and one sized for a paper are different charts, and
      the default is for neither.
    - `linestyle="--"` on the second line — **not only a different colour.** This is the accessibility
      rule and it is one keyword.
    - `ax.set(...)` — sets several properties in one call. Equivalent to `ax.set_xlabel()`,
      `ax.set_ylabel()`, `ax.set_title()` and considerably less to read.
    - `bbox_inches="tight"` — crops surrounding whitespace and, importantly, stops a long axis label
      or a legend from being cut off in the saved file. **The single most common "why is my label
      missing?" fix.**
    - `plt.close(fig)` — releases the figure. In a loop generating many plots, omitting this leaks
      memory and eventually emits a warning about too many open figures.

    ### Four jobs, and the chart for each

    | The question | The chart | Not |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Distribution** — how is one variable spread? | histogram, ECDF, violin, boxplot | a bar chart of means, which hides the spread entirely |
    | **Relationship** — how do two variables move together? | scatter, hexbin (when dense), line for time | a table of correlations |
    | **Comparison** — how do groups differ? | grouped or dot plot, small multiples | a pie chart, which makes comparison nearly impossible |
    | **Composition** — what are the parts of a whole? | stacked bar, treemap | a pie chart with more than about three slices |

    <Warning>
    **A bar chart of group means is the most common bad chart in analysis**, because it shows one
    number per group and hides everything: the spread, the sample size, the outliers, and whether
    the distributions overlap almost entirely. Two groups with means of 10 and 12 might be cleanly
    separated or might overlap by 95%, and the bar chart looks identical either way. **Use a box
    plot, a violin, or — best for small n — a strip plot showing every point.**
    </Warning>

    ### Log scales

    ```python
    import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    amounts = rng.lognormal(mean=4, sigma=1.5, size=10_000)

    fig, (a1, a2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))

    a1.hist(amounts, bins=50)
    a1.set(title="Linear: one bar, and a long empty tail", xlabel="amount (£)")

    a2.hist(amounts, bins=np.logspace(np.log10(amounts.min()),
                                      np.log10(amounts.max()), 50))
    a2.set_xscale("log")
    a2.set(title="Log: the shape is visible", xlabel="amount (£), log scale")
    fig.savefig("skew.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
    ```

    On right-skewed data the linear histogram puts 98% of the mass in the first bar and shows a wide
    empty tail. The log-scale version reveals the distribution's actual shape — usually roughly
    symmetric, which tells you it is log-normal.

    **Two rules for log axes:**

    1. **Label the axis as logarithmic.** A reader who does not notice will misread every distance
       on the chart by orders of magnitude.
    2. **Use log-spaced bins**, not linear bins on a log axis. Linear bins rendered on a log axis
       produce bars of wildly varying visual width, which distorts the apparent density — a subtle
       error that looks fine.

    ### Colour

    ```python
    # Bad: red and green, meaning encoded in colour alone.
    ax.plot(x, y1, color="red", label="control")
    ax.plot(x, y2, color="green", label="treatment")

    # Good: colorblind-safe palette, AND a redundant channel.
    ax.plot(x, y1, color="#0173B2", linestyle="-",  marker="o", label="control")
    ax.plot(x, y2, color="#DE8F05", linestyle="--", marker="s", label="treatment")
    ```

    **Red and green are indistinguishable to about 8% of men.** A chart whose entire meaning is
    "the red line is above the green line" is unreadable to one person in twelve in a typical
    audience.

    The fix is **redundant encoding**: colour *plus* line style, *plus* markers, or — better than a
    legend — labelling the lines directly at their right-hand end, so the reader never has to match
    a colour to a key at all.

    Palettes that work:

    ```python
    import seaborn as sns                     # seaborn 0.13.2
    sns.set_palette("colorblind")             # Okabe-Ito, 8 distinguishable colours

    # For continuous data:
    # 'viridis'  — perceptually uniform, colorblind-safe, prints in greyscale
    # 'cividis'  — designed specifically for colour vision deficiency
    # NEVER 'jet' or 'rainbow' — they create false boundaries where the data is smooth
    ```

    <Warning>
    **The `jet` / rainbow colormap is actively misleading and still ubiquitous.** It is not
    perceptually uniform: the cyan and yellow bands are much brighter than their neighbours, so the
    eye sees sharp edges where the data is perfectly smooth. Readers reliably perceive structure
    that is not there. It also collapses to mush in greyscale. `viridis` — matplotlib's default
    since 2.0 — was designed to fix exactly this.
    </Warning>

    ### Checkpoint — 5 questions before you continue

    <AccordionGroup>
      <Accordion title="1. Why use `fig, ax = plt.subplots()` instead of `plt.plot()`?">
        Because `plt` draws on whatever figure is "current", which in a script or a loop is
        ambiguous and produces plots on the wrong figure or blank files. The object-oriented
        interface names its target on every call. It is also the only workable style for
        multi-panel figures.
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="2. When must a bar chart's y-axis start at zero?">
        **Always.** A bar encodes a value as a *length*, and a truncated axis makes lengths
        proportional to differences rather than to values — so three values 0.6% apart can be drawn
        six times apart. A line chart encodes *position*, so a truncated axis is often fine and
        sometimes necessary. **The rule follows from the visual encoding, not from convention.**
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="3. What is wrong with a bar chart of group means?">
        It shows one number per group and hides the distribution: spread, sample size, outliers, and
        whether the groups overlap. Two groups with means of 10 and 12 look identical on a bar chart
        whether they are cleanly separated or overlap by 95%. Use a box plot, a violin, or a strip
        plot showing every point.
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="4. Why is 'jet' a bad colormap?">
        It is not perceptually uniform. Its bright cyan and yellow bands make the eye perceive sharp
        boundaries where the underlying data changes smoothly, so readers see structure that does
        not exist. It also does not survive greyscale printing or colour vision deficiency. Use
        `viridis` or `cividis`.
      </Accordion>
      <Accordion title="5. Why encode meaning redundantly?">
        Because roughly 8% of men have red-green colour vision deficiency, charts get printed in
        greyscale, and projectors distort colour. Colour plus line style plus markers — or direct
        labels instead of a legend — means the chart still works when the colour channel is
        unavailable. It costs one keyword argument.
      </Accordion>
    </AccordionGroup>

    ## Plotting 10 million points honestly, measured

    The same data — correlated bivariate normal — drawn three ways:

    | Points | Method | Time | PNG size | Result |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | 10,000 | scatter | 0.29 s | 38 KB | fine |
    | 10,000 | hexbin | 0.04 s | 94 KB | fine |
    | 10,000 | hist2d | 0.02 s | 16 KB | fine |
    | 100,000 | scatter | 0.03 s | 75 KB | getting dense |
    | 1,000,000 | scatter | 0.08 s | **61 KB** | **a solid blob** |
    | 1,000,000 | hexbin | 0.07 s | 87 KB | clear structure |
    | 10,000,000 | scatter | — | — | **not attempted; unreadable** |
    | 10,000,000 | **hexbin** | **0.66 s** | 93 KB | **clear structure** |
    | 10,000,000 | hist2d (log colour) | 1.05 s | 41 KB | clear structure |

    <Info>
    Method: matplotlib 3.9.3 with the Agg backend, M3 Pro, 5×4 inches at 100 dpi, `alpha=0.1` and
    `s=1` for the scatter. Peak process RSS across the whole sweep was 1,438 MB. Reproduce with
    `w16m5.py`.
    </Info>

    **The interesting number is the PNG size.** At 100,000 points the scatter PNG is 75 KB; at
    1,000,000 it is **61 KB — smaller.** The file got smaller because the plot became a solid black
    region, which compresses well. **File size shrinking as you add data is a mechanical signal of
    overplotting**, and it is a genuinely useful diagnostic: if adding ten times the data makes your
    PNG smaller, your chart has stopped showing anything.

    The alternatives at scale:

    ```python
    # 1. hexbin — hexagonal bins, count per bin as colour. Best default.
    ax.hexbin(x, y, gridsize=100, bins="log", cmap="viridis")
    #   bins="log" is important: density is usually heavily skewed, and a linear
    #   colour scale puts everything except the mode in the same shade.

    # 2. hist2d — rectangular bins. Slightly faster to read, hexagons tile better.
    ax.hist2d(x, y, bins=200, norm=matplotlib.colors.LogNorm())

    # 3. Sample, and SAY SO in the title.
    idx = rng.choice(len(x), 50_000, replace=False)
    ax.scatter(x[idx], y[idx], s=1, alpha=0.3)
    ax.set_title("50,000-point random sample of 10,000,000")

    # 4. Datashader, for 10^8+ or for interactive panning.
    #    Rasterises to a fixed grid with a chosen aggregation, in C.
    ```

    **Sampling is legitimate and under-used, but it must be disclosed.** A scatter of 50,000 sampled
    points is honest and readable; the same chart captioned as if it showed all ten million is not.
    Put the sample size in the title.

    ## The dishonest chart, and how to audit your own

    ```python
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

    models = ["baseline", "v2", "v3"]
    accuracy = [95.1, 95.4, 95.7]

    fig, (bad, good) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))

    bad.bar(models, accuracy, color="#0173B2")
    bad.set_ylim(94, 96)                      # <-- the lie
    bad.set(title="v3 is a huge improvement", ylabel="accuracy (%)")

    good.bar(models, accuracy, color="#0173B2")
    good.set_ylim(0, 100)
    good.set(title="v3 is 0.6 points better than baseline", ylabel="accuracy (%)")
    for i, v in enumerate(accuracy):
        good.text(i, v + 1, f"{v}%", ha="center")
    ```

    **The left panel makes the bars appear roughly six times different. The values differ by 0.6%.**
    Nothing is falsified — the numbers are correct, the labels are correct — and the visual claim is
    false.

    The audit questions, which take thirty seconds:

    | Ask | Because |
    | --- | --- |
    | Does the y-axis start at zero? | required for bars; optional for lines |
    | Would a different axis range change the conclusion? | if yes, the conclusion is about your axis |
    | Is the aspect ratio doing work? | a wide, short chart flattens trends; a tall, narrow one exaggerates them |
    | Are both axes labelled with units? | "accuracy" and "accuracy (%)" are different claims |
    | Is the sample size shown? | a difference between n=8 and n=8,000 is a different finding |
    | Would this survive greyscale? | if not, meaning is encoded in colour alone |
    | **Does the title state a fact or an interpretation?** | "v3 is a huge improvement" is an argument; "v3 is 0.6 points better" is a finding |

    **The title question is the one people skip and it matters most.** A reader remembers the title
    long after the chart. A title that states the number rather than the conclusion is much harder
    to be wrong with.

    ### When a truncated axis is legitimate

    | Chart | Truncated axis | Why |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Bar chart | **never** | bars encode length; a truncated axis makes length proportional to difference, not to value |
    | Line chart, time series | **usually fine** | lines encode position and the reader is comparing *changes*; a temperature series from 0 K is useless |
    | Scatter | fine | positions are compared to each other, not to an origin |
    | Area chart | **never** | filled area is a length encoding, same as a bar |
    | Any chart where the change is the subject | fine, **if the axis is clearly labelled** | a stock price, a latency percentile |

    **The rule follows from the encoding, not from etiquette.** Ask what visual property carries the
    meaning: if it is *length* or *area*, the scale must start at zero. If it is *position*, it need
    not.

    ## Small multiples

    ```python
    import seaborn as sns
    sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid", palette="colorblind")

    g = sns.FacetGrid(df, col="region", row="segment",
                      margin_titles=True, height=2.6, aspect=1.3,
                      sharex=True, sharey=True)          # <-- shared scales are the point
    g.map_dataframe(sns.scatterplot, x="tenure_months", y="monthly_spend",
                    s=8, alpha=0.4)
    g.set_axis_labels("tenure (months)", "monthly spend (£)")
    g.figure.suptitle("Spend against tenure, by region and segment", y=1.02)
    g.savefig("facets.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
    ```

    **Twelve small panels on shared axes beat one crowded panel with twelve colours**, because the
    reader compares *positions across panels* rather than trying to separate twelve overlapping
    series. This is Tufte's point about small multiples and it is the highest-leverage layout choice
    available.

    **`sharex=True, sharey=True` is what makes it work.** With independent scales the panels are not
    comparable and the layout is actively misleading — a small bump in one panel and a large one in
    another can be drawn the same size. If you take one thing from this section: **shared axes, or
    it is not a small-multiples chart.**

    ## Reference implementation: a reusable plotting module

    ```python
    # plots.py — matplotlib 3.9.3, seaborn 0.13.2
    from __future__ import annotations
    from pathlib import Path

    import matplotlib
    matplotlib.use("Agg")          # no display needed; safe in CI and in a container
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    import numpy as np
    import pandas as pd

    # Okabe-Ito: eight colours distinguishable under all common forms of
    # colour vision deficiency. https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/
    OKABE_ITO = ["#0173B2", "#DE8F05", "#029E73", "#D55E00",
                 "#CC78BC", "#CA9161", "#FBAFE4", "#949494"]

    plt.rcParams.update({
        "figure.dpi": 110,
        "savefig.dpi": 200,
        "savefig.bbox": "tight",
        "font.size": 11,
        "axes.grid": True,
        "axes.grid.axis": "y",
        "grid.alpha": 0.3,
        "axes.spines.top": False,
        "axes.spines.right": False,
        "axes.prop_cycle": plt.cycler(color=OKABE_ITO),
        "figure.constrained_layout.use": True,
    })


    def distribution(s: pd.Series, *, log: bool = False, out: Path | None = None):
        """Histogram + ECDF side by side. The histogram communicates;
        the ECDF is the one you should trust, because it has no bins."""
        d = s.dropna()
        fig, (h, e) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))

        bins = (np.logspace(np.log10(max(d.min(), 1e-9)), np.log10(d.max()), 50)
                if log else 50)
        h.hist(d, bins=bins, color=OKABE_ITO[0], edgecolor="white", linewidth=0.4)
        if log:
            h.set_xscale("log")
        h.set(xlabel=f"{s.name}{' (log scale)' if log else ''}", ylabel="count",
              title=f"{s.name}: distribution  (n={len(d):,})")

        x = np.sort(d.to_numpy())
        e.plot(x, np.arange(1, len(x) + 1) / len(x), color=OKABE_ITO[0])
        if log:
            e.set_xscale("log")
        for q in (0.5, 0.95):
            v = float(d.quantile(q))
            e.axvline(v, color=OKABE_ITO[3], linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
            e.annotate(f"p{int(q*100)} = {v:,.0f}", (v, q),
                       xytext=(6, -12), textcoords="offset points", fontsize=9)
        e.set(xlabel=s.name, ylabel="cumulative proportion", title="ECDF")

        if out:
            fig.savefig(out)
            plt.close(fig)
        return fig


    def dense_scatter(x, y, *, xlabel: str, ylabel: str,
                      out: Path | None = None, gridsize: int = 100):
        """Above ~50,000 points a scatter is a blob. Bin it, and say n in the title."""
        x = np.asarray(x); y = np.asarray(y)
        fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 5))
        hb = ax.hexbin(x, y, gridsize=gridsize, bins="log", cmap="viridis", mincnt=1)
        fig.colorbar(hb, ax=ax, label="points per bin (log scale)")
        ax.set(xlabel=xlabel, ylabel=ylabel,
               title=f"{ylabel} against {xlabel}  (n={len(x):,})")
        if out:
            fig.savefig(out)
            plt.close(fig)
        return fig


    def group_comparison(df: pd.DataFrame, value: str, group: str,
                         out: Path | None = None):
        """A box plot PLUS every point when n is small. Never a bar of means."""
        fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
        groups = [g[value].dropna().to_numpy() for _, g in df.groupby(group, observed=True)]
        labels = [str(k) for k, _ in df.groupby(group, observed=True)]
        ax.boxplot(groups, tick_labels=labels, showfliers=False, widths=0.55)
        rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
        for i, g in enumerate(groups, start=1):
            if len(g) <= 500:                       # show the raw data when we can
                ax.scatter(rng.normal(i, 0.055, len(g)), g,
                           s=9, alpha=0.45, color=OKABE_ITO[0], zorder=3)
        ns = ", ".join(f"{lab} n={len(g)}" for lab, g in zip(labels, groups))
        ax.set(xlabel=group, ylabel=value, title=f"{value} by {group}\\n{ns}")
        if out:
            fig.savefig(out)
            plt.close(fig)
        return fig
    ```

    ```python
    # test_plots.py — pytest 8.x
    import matplotlib
    import numpy as np
    import pandas as pd
    import pytest
    from plots import distribution, dense_scatter, group_comparison, OKABE_ITO


    @pytest.fixture
    def s():
        return pd.Series(np.random.default_rng(0).lognormal(3, 1.2, 5000), name="amount")


    def test_distribution_reports_n_in_the_title(s):
        fig = distribution(s)
    ```

    <Check>
    `test_every_axis_is_labelled` and `test_log_scale_is_labelled` are unusual — most people do not
    test plots at all. They are worth having because **an unlabelled axis or an unmarked log scale
    is a correctness bug, not a cosmetic one**: the reader draws a wrong conclusion, and no
    downstream check catches it. Testing the *metadata* of a figure is cheap and it is the part that
    determines whether the chart tells the truth. Testing the pixels is not worth it.
    </Check>

    ## Trade-offs table

    | Situation | Choose | Instead of | Because |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Under ~50,000 points | scatter | hexbin | individual points carry information |
    | Over ~50,000 points | **hexbin with `bins="log"`** | scatter | measured: at 10⁶ the scatter PNG gets *smaller*, which means structure is gone |
    | Over ~10⁸ points, or interactive | datashader | hexbin | rasterises in C at a fixed resolution |
    | Comparing distributions carefully | ECDF | histogram | no bin-width choice to bias the shape |
    | Communicating a distribution | histogram | ECDF | more readers interpret it correctly |
    | Comparing groups, n under ~500 | box plot **plus** the points | bar of means | shows spread, sample size and overlap |
    | Comparing groups, large n | violin or box | strip plot | overplotting |
    | Many subgroups | small multiples, shared axes | one panel with many colours | position across panels beats colour discrimination |
    | Right-skewed data | log scale, **labelled**, with log-spaced bins | linear | otherwise 98% of the mass is one bar |
    | Continuous colour | `viridis` or `cividis` | `jet` / `rainbow` | perceptually uniform; `jet` invents boundaries |
    | Categorical colour | Okabe-Ito / seaborn `colorblind` | default red/green | ~8% of men cannot distinguish red from green |
    | Any chart with several series | colour **plus** line style or direct labels | colour alone | greyscale printing, projectors, colour vision deficiency |

    ## What breaks in the real world

    An ML team presented a quarterly model review. The headline chart compared four model versions
    on validation accuracy: 94.2%, 94.6%, 95.1%, 95.4%. The y-axis ran from 94 to 95.5.

    **The bars appeared to grow by roughly 4× across the four versions.** The actual improvement was
    **1.2 percentage points**. Nothing was falsified: the axis was labelled, the values were correct,
    the chart was made in good faith with matplotlib's defaults after someone set `ylim` to "make the
    difference visible".

    Leadership approved a headcount increase for the ML team on the strength of the trajectory. The
    next quarter's chart, drawn the same way, showed 95.4% → 95.5% — a nearly flat bar. The visual
    story went from "rapid progress" to "we have plateaued", and the team spent a difficult meeting
    explaining that the second chart represented **the same rate of progress as the first**, and that
    the first chart had been the misleading one.

    **The credibility cost landed on the team that made the honest second chart.**

    The fix was a house style enforced in a shared plotting module:

    ```python
    def comparison_bars(labels, values, ylabel, baseline=0.0):
        """Bars always start at the baseline. Values are always printed.
        The title states the number, not the conclusion."""
        fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4))
        ax.bar(labels, values, color=OKABE_ITO[0])
        ax.set_ylim(baseline, max(values) * 1.15)
        for i, v in enumerate(values):
            ax.text(i, v, f"{v:.1f}", ha="center", va="bottom")
        delta = max(values) - min(values)
        ax.set(ylabel=ylabel, title=f"range: {delta:.1f} {ylabel}")
        return fig
    ```

    Plus a review rule: **the title states the magnitude, and the numbers appear on the bars.** If
    the difference is genuinely too small to see from zero, that is information — it means the
    difference is small — and the honest presentation is a table or a dot plot with a clearly
    labelled zoomed axis, not a bar chart pretending otherwise.

    What generalises:

    - **A chart that overstates a result borrows credibility from the future.** You have to keep
      making that chart. The team that told a modest true story quarter after quarter would have had
      an easier fourth quarter.
    - **Printing the value on the bar makes the axis manipulation self-defeating**, which is why it
      is a good default: the reader sees both the visual and the number, and they cannot disagree.
    - **A title stating the conclusion is the most persuasive and least auditable element of a
      chart.** "v3 is a huge improvement" is an argument. "range: 1.2 percentage points" is a fact.
      Prefer the fact.

    ### Design checkpoint

    <Accordion title="You are given a chart to review before it goes to the board. It shows a metric rising steeply over six quarters. What do you check, in order, and what would make you send it back?">
    **Model answer.**

    **In order, cheapest first.**

    1. **The y-axis range.** Does it start at zero? For a line chart that is not automatically wrong
       — but *what fraction of the axis does the change occupy?* If the metric moved 3% and fills the
       whole chart, the visual claim is much stronger than the numeric one. **Ask for the numbers
       printed on the chart**; if the presenter resists, that resistance is itself informative.
    2. **The x-axis spacing.** Are the six quarters evenly spaced, and are any missing? An omitted
       bad quarter with the remaining points spaced evenly is the most effective chart lie there is,
       and it is nearly invisible.
    3. **The metric definition, and whether it changed.** Six quarters is long enough for a
       definition to have been revised. If "active users" was redefined in Q3, the chart is two
       series drawn as one. **Ask when the definition last changed** — this is the question that
       most often finds a real problem.
    4. **The denominator.** A rising count during a period of rising traffic may be a falling rate.
       Ask which one supports the claim being made.
    5. **Sample size and uncertainty.** Six points with no error bars. If each quarter's value has a
       confidence interval wider than the total change, there is no trend — there is noise with a
       flattering line through it.
    6. **The aspect ratio.** A tall narrow chart exaggerates slope; a wide flat one suppresses it.
       Ask to see it at a 1:1-ish aspect and check the story survives.
    7. **The title.** Fact or interpretation? "Engagement up 4.2% over six quarters" is a finding.
       "Engagement is accelerating" is a claim that requires the second derivative and probably an
       error bar.

    **What would make me send it back:**

    - A **bar chart** with a truncated axis. Non-negotiable; it is a length encoding.
    - A **metric definition change** not marked on the chart. Draw a vertical line and annotate it.
    - **No sample size or uncertainty** on a chart with fewer than ~20 points where the change is
      comparable to the noise.
    - A **title stating a conclusion** the chart cannot support on its own.
    - **A missing period** with no acknowledgement.

    **What I would not object to:** a truncated axis on a line chart where the change *is* the
    subject, clearly labelled. Insisting a latency chart start at zero milliseconds is pedantry that
    destroys the chart's usefulness, and being right about the general rule while being wrong about
    this case makes people stop listening to the rule.

    **The framing I would use:** "this chart claims the metric roughly tripled; the numbers say it
    rose 4.2%. Both can be true of the same data and the board will remember the picture. Can we put
    the values on it?" **That is a question about clarity, not integrity**, and it gets the chart
    fixed rather than getting you a reputation.
    </Accordion>

    ## Perceptual accuracy: which encodings people read well

    Cleveland and McGill (1984) ran controlled experiments asking people to judge the *ratio* between
    two encoded values, and ranked encodings by measured error. The ordering, best to worst:

    | Rank | Encoding | Where it appears |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | 1 | **Position on a common scale** | scatter, line, dot plot, grouped bars on shared axes |
    | 2 | Position on non-aligned scales | small multiples with shared ranges |
    | 3 | **Length** | bars, and this is why they must start at zero |
    | 4 | Angle / slope | pie charts, and slope in line charts |
    | 5 | **Area** | bubble charts, treemaps |
    | 6 | Volume | 3-D anything |
    | 7 | Colour saturation / density | heatmaps, choropleths |

    **The practical consequences:**

    - **A dot plot beats a bar chart** for comparing values, because it uses position (rank 1) rather
      than length (rank 3). It also does not require a zero baseline, which means it handles values
      in a narrow range honestly — exactly the case the war story's bar chart got wrong.
    - **A pie chart is rank 4**, and worse than the ranking suggests because it asks for many
      simultaneous angle comparisons. **The exception is one slice against the whole** — "37% of
      revenue" — where a single angle is being judged and it works fine.
    - **Bubble charts are rank 5 and consistently misread.** People judge bubble *diameter*, not
      area, so a bubble encoding 4× the value looks like 2×. If you must, size by diameter with the
      caveat stated, or use a different encoding.
    - **Heatmaps are rank 7 and are for pattern-finding, not value-reading.** A heatmap that shows
      where the structure is, with the numbers in an adjacent table, is the right combination.

    **The rule that falls out: to compare quantities, put them on a common position scale.** A dot
    plot with a shared axis is almost always the honest, readable answer, and it is the chart people
    reach for least.

    ## Overplotting: three mechanisms, three fixes

    The measured result — a 1,000,000-point scatter PNG being *smaller* than a 100,000-point one —
    reflects three distinct problems that get lumped together:

    | Problem | What it hides | Fix |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Occlusion** — points cover points | density; you cannot tell 10 points from 10,000 | binning (hexbin, hist2d), or alpha |
    | **Saturation** — alpha stacking reaches full opacity | density above the saturation threshold | binning with a **log** colour scale |
    | **Rendering cost** — millions of vector marks | nothing, but the file is huge and slow | rasterise the data layer, keep axes vector |

    Alpha blending is the usual first attempt and it only fixes the first problem. With `alpha=0.1`,
    ten overlapping points reach full opacity — so any region with more than ten points looks
    identical to a region with ten thousand. **Alpha has a hard ceiling and the ceiling is `1/alpha`
    points.**

    ```python
    # Rasterise the dense layer, keep the axes and text as vector.
    # A 5 MB PDF becomes ~200 KB and stays sharp when zoomed.
    ax.scatter(x, y, s=1, alpha=0.3, rasterized=True)
    fig.savefig("plot.pdf", dpi=300)
    ```

    `rasterized=True` is the underused one for publication figures: the data becomes a bitmap at the
    stated dpi while axes, ticks and labels stay as scalable vector.

    ## Automated report generation

    The goal is a figure that is **reproducible from a script**, never a screenshot of a notebook.

    ```python
    # report.py — generates a self-contained HTML report with embedded figures.
    from __future__ import annotations
    import base64
    import io
    from pathlib import Path

    import matplotlib
    matplotlib.use("Agg")
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt


    def fig_to_data_uri(fig, fmt: str = "png", dpi: int = 150) -> str:
        """Embed the figure in the HTML so the report is a single portable file."""
        buf = io.BytesIO()
        fig.savefig(buf, format=fmt, dpi=dpi, bbox_inches="tight")
        plt.close(fig)
        mime = "image/svg+xml" if fmt == "svg" else f"image/{fmt}"
        return f"data:{mime};base64,{base64.b64encode(buf.getvalue()).decode()}"


    def render(title: str, sections: list[tuple[str, str, object]]) -> str:
        """sections: (heading, prose, figure-or-None)."""
        parts = [
            "<!doctype html><meta charset='utf-8'>",
            "<style>body{font:16px/1.55 system-ui;max-width:56rem;margin:2rem auto;"
            "padding:0 1rem}img{max-width:100%}h2{margin-top:2.2rem}"
            "figure{margin:1rem 0}</style>",
            f"<h1>{title}</h1>",
        ]
        for heading, prose, fig in sections:
            parts.append(f"<h2>{heading}</h2><p>{prose}</p>")
            if fig is not None:
                parts.append(f"<figure><img alt='{heading}' "
                             f"src='{fig_to_data_uri(fig)}'></figure>")
        return "\\n".join(parts)
    ```

    **Why a single self-contained HTML file rather than a directory of PNGs:** it can be emailed,
    attached to a ticket, or committed, and it cannot arrive with broken images. The cost is size —
    base64 inflates by about 33% — so use SVG for line art (smaller *and* sharp) and PNG for anything
    dense.

    **Why not a notebook:** a notebook is a record of a process, it requires the reader to have an
    environment, its output can be stale relative to its code, and it invites the reader into your
    exploration rather than presenting your conclusion. **A script that produces a report is
    reproducible; a notebook that produced a screenshot is an anecdote.**

    For anything beyond a report, **Quarto** renders `.qmd` files (Markdown plus executable Python)
    to HTML, PDF and slides with figures regenerated on every render. It is what I would use for
    anything recurring.

    ## Colour, properly

    ```python
    # Check any figure for colour-vision safety before shipping it.
    # pip install colorspacious==1.1.2
    from colorspacious import cspace_convert
    import numpy as np

    def simulate_deuteranopia(rgb: np.ndarray, severity: int = 100) -> np.ndarray:
        space = {"name": "sRGB1+CVD", "cvd_type": "deuteranomaly", "severity": severity}
        return np.clip(cspace_convert(rgb, space, "sRGB1"), 0, 1)
    ```

    | Deficiency | Prevalence (men) | Confuses |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Deuteranomaly / deuteranopia | ~6% | red and green |
    | Protanomaly / protanopia | ~2% | red and green; red appears darker |
    | Tritanopia | &lt;0.01% | blue and yellow |
    | Monochromacy | ~0.003% | everything |

    **About 8% of men and 0.5% of women** have some red-green deficiency. In a room of 25 engineers,
    expect two.

    **Continuous colormaps and why `viridis` won.** A good sequential colormap must be **monotonic in
    perceived lightness**, so that the value ordering survives greyscale, colour deficiency and a bad
    projector. `jet` is not: its lightness goes up, down, up, creating bright bands at cyan and
    yellow that the eye reads as edges. Published examples exist of `jet` making a smooth medical
    scan appear to show a discrete lesion boundary. `viridis`, `cividis`, `magma` and `inferno` are
    all lightness-monotonic by construction.

    **For diverging data** — anything with a meaningful midpoint, like a correlation matrix or a
    difference from baseline — use a diverging map (`RdBu`, `coolwarm`) **centred on the midpoint**:

    ```python
    from matplotlib.colors import TwoSlopeNorm
    ax.imshow(corr, cmap="RdBu_r", norm=TwoSlopeNorm(vmin=-1, vcenter=0, vmax=1))
    ```

    Without `vcenter=0`, the white point lands wherever the data's midpoint happens to be, and
    "white means zero correlation" silently becomes false.

    ## Production failure modes

    | Failure mode | Signature | Detection |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Truncated bar-chart axis | small differences look large; the next honest chart looks like a regression | house style enforcing `ylim` from the baseline; print values on the bars |
    | Structure invisible at scale | the plot is a solid blob; the PNG gets *smaller* as you add data | hexbin or hist2d above ~50,000 points; watch the file size |
    | Alpha saturation | density above `1/alpha` points is indistinguishable | binning with a log colour scale |
    | Meaning in colour alone | unreadable in greyscale or to ~8% of men | redundant encoding: line style, markers, direct labels |
    | `jet` colormap | readers report boundaries that are not in the data | `viridis`; check lightness monotonicity |
    | Diverging map not centred | "white means zero" is silently false | `TwoSlopeNorm(vcenter=0)` |
    | Axis label cut off in the saved file | the file differs from the on-screen figure | `bbox_inches="tight"` |
    | Memory grows generating many plots | figures never closed | `plt.close(fig)` in the loop |
    | Figure differs between machines | fonts, backend, or an rcParams file | pin the backend (`Agg`), set rcParams explicitly, ship the fonts |
    | Chart shows a count where a rate was meant | trend follows traffic, not behaviour | always ask what the denominator is |
    | Metric definition changed mid-series | a step that reads as a trend | annotate definition changes on the chart |
    | Figure exists only as a notebook screenshot | cannot be regenerated or corrected | generate from a script; render with Quarto |

    ## Frontier and research pointers

    - **Cleveland & McGill (1984), "Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and Application to
      the Development of Graphical Methods."** *JASA* 79(387). — **Takeaway:** the measured ranking
      of visual encodings by human judgement accuracy; position on a common scale beats length beats
      angle beats area, and this single ordering justifies most chart-choice advice.
    - **Heer & Bostock (2010), "Crowdsourcing Graphical Perception."** CHI. — **Takeaway:**
      Cleveland and McGill replicated at scale on Mechanical Turk, extending the results to
      rectangular-area encodings and confirming the ordering holds.
    - **Borkin et al. (2011), "Evaluation of Artery Visualizations for Heart Disease Diagnosis."**
      *IEEE TVCG* 17(12). — **Takeaway:** switching from a rainbow colormap to a
      perceptually-uniform one **measurably improved diagnostic accuracy** in a clinical task; the
      strongest available evidence that colormap choice is a correctness issue.
    - **Nuñez, Anderton & Renslow (2018), "Optimizing colormaps with consideration for color vision
      deficiency."** *PLoS ONE* 13(7). — **Takeaway:** the design of `cividis`, and the method for
      evaluating a colormap under simulated colour vision deficiency.
    - **Tufte (1983), *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*.** — **Takeaway:** the
      data-ink ratio, the lie factor, and small multiples; the lie-factor idea — the ratio of the
      visual effect's size to the data effect's size — is exactly what the war story's chart got
      wrong, quantified.
    - **Wickham (2010), "A Layered Grammar of Graphics."** *J. Comp. Graph. Stat.* 19(1). —
      **Takeaway:** decomposing a chart into data, aesthetic mappings, geometries, scales and facets
      makes chart *choice* a compositional decision rather than a menu selection; this is the model
      behind ggplot2, plotnine and Vega-Lite.

    ## Stretch challenge

    **Build a chart auditor, and measure how often real charts fail.**

    Take an image or a matplotlib `Figure` and produce a structured critique. Then find out how
    common each failure actually is.

    Build:

    1. **A `Figure` auditor** that inspects a live matplotlib figure and reports: bar charts with a
       non-zero baseline; unlabelled axes; log scales not mentioned in the label; a title stating a
       conclusion rather than a value (a keyword heuristic); missing sample sizes; estimated
       data-ink ratio; and an aspect ratio outside a sensible band.
    2. **A colour-safety check**: extract every colour used, simulate deuteranopia, protanopia and
       greyscale, and report any pair whose perceptual distance falls below a threshold under any
       simulation. **Report which two series become confusable**, so the fix is obvious.
    3. **A lie-factor estimator** for bar charts: compute the ratio of the visual length difference
       to the data value difference. Tufte's lie factor. **1.0 is honest; the war story's chart was
       around 6.**
    4. **An empirical study.** Run the auditor across a corpus of real charts — matplotlib and
       seaborn gallery examples, your own past work, figures scraped from arXiv papers in one field —
       and report the failure-rate distribution.

    The open questions:

    - **How common is each failure in published work?** My guess is that unlabelled axes and missing
      sample sizes are far more common than truncated bar axes, and that colour-safety failures are
      near-universal in older papers. **Measure it.**
    - **Can you detect a misleading chart from an image alone?** Much harder than from a `Figure`
      object — it needs axis-label OCR and tick-value extraction. There is published work on chart
      data extraction; how far does it get, and what is the false-positive rate?
    - **Which failures actually mislead readers?** The auditor flags rule violations. Do they change
      conclusions? **Run a small experiment**: show people the same data as an honest and a
      misleading chart and measure the difference in their estimated effect size. Cleveland and
      McGill's methodology is directly reusable and this is the part that would make the project
      genuinely novel.
    - **What should the tool do?** A linter that fails a build on an unlabelled axis is usable. One
      that fails on a "conclusion-stating title" is not, because that judgement is contextual. **Draw
      the line between hard rules and advisory notes**, and defend it.

    A chart linter that runs in CI, plus a measured study of how often published figures violate
    perceptual rules, is a genuinely publishable piece of work — and the linter alone would improve
    every figure you produce for the rest of the course.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small differences look dramatic | Truncated y-axis on a bar chart | Bars start at the baseline; print values on the bars |
| Scatter plot is a solid blob | Overplotting — measured: the PNG gets smaller as data grows | `hexbin` with `bins="log"` above ~50,000 points |
| Alpha blending still shows no structure | Saturation ceiling is `1/alpha` points | Binning with a log colour scale, not more alpha |
| Chart is unreadable in greyscale or to some readers | Meaning encoded in colour alone | Colour **plus** line style or markers; direct labels |
| Readers see boundaries that are not in the data | `jet` / rainbow colormap is not lightness-monotonic | `viridis` or `cividis` |
| "White means zero" is false on a correlation heatmap | Diverging colormap not centred | `TwoSlopeNorm(vcenter=0)` |
| Axis label missing from the saved file | Figure cropped at save | `bbox_inches="tight"` |
| Memory grows while generating many figures | Figures never closed | `plt.close(fig)` in the loop |
| Bar chart of means hides the real story | One number per group; spread and overlap invisible | Box plot, violin, or a strip plot with every point |
| Log-scale histogram bars look wrong | Linear bins rendered on a log axis | `np.logspace` bins |
| A reader misreads every distance | Log scale not mentioned in the axis label | Say "(log scale)" in the label |
| Figure looks different on CI | Backend, fonts or a stray rcParams file | `matplotlib.use("Agg")`; set rcParams explicitly |
| Trend follows traffic, not behaviour | A count plotted where a rate was meant | Always state the denominator |

## Sources & further reading

**Primary docs**

- [matplotlib: the object-oriented interface](https://matplotlib.org/3.9.3/users/explain/quick_start.html) (3.9.3) — read the "Coding styles" section; it is matplotlib's own recommendation to use the explicit interface, and it explains why the stateful one exists at all.
- [matplotlib: choosing colormaps](https://matplotlib.org/3.9.3/users/explain/colors/colormaps.html) — read the lightness-profile plots; they show visually why `jet` invents boundaries and `viridis` does not.
- [matplotlib: constrained layout](https://matplotlib.org/3.9.3/users/explain/axes/constrainedlayout_guide.html) — read this for the modern replacement for `tight_layout`; it handles colorbars and suptitles correctly, which `tight_layout` does not.
- [seaborn: choosing color palettes](https://seaborn.pydata.org/tutorial/color_palettes.html) (0.13.2) — read this for the `colorblind` palette (Okabe-Ito) and for when to use qualitative, sequential and diverging palettes.
- [Okabe & Ito, *Color Universal Design*](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/) — the source of the eight-colour palette; read it for the design constraints and the simulation images.

**Papers**

- [Cleveland & McGill (1984), *Graphical Perception*, JASA 79(387), 531–554.](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080) — **Takeaway:** the measured ranking of visual encodings by human accuracy; position beats length beats angle beats area, and almost every piece of chart advice derives from this ordering.
- [Heer & Bostock (2010), *Crowdsourcing Graphical Perception*, CHI.](https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753357) — **Takeaway:** the 1984 results replicate at scale and extend to rectangular areas; the ranking is robust.
- [Borkin et al. (2011), *Evaluation of Artery Visualizations for Heart Disease Diagnosis*, IEEE TVCG 17(12).](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2011.192) — **Takeaway:** a perceptually-uniform colormap **measurably improved diagnostic accuracy** over a rainbow map in a clinical task; colormap choice is a correctness issue, not a style one.
- [Nuñez, Anderton & Renslow (2018), *Optimizing colormaps with consideration for color vision deficiency*, PLoS ONE 13(7).](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0199239) — **Takeaway:** the design and validation of `cividis`, and a reusable method for evaluating any colormap under simulated deficiency.
- [Wickham (2010), *A Layered Grammar of Graphics*, J. Comp. Graph. Stat. 19(1).](https://doi.org/10.1198/jcgs.2009.07098) — **Takeaway:** treating a chart as a composition of data, mappings, geoms, scales and facets turns chart choice into a design decision rather than a menu pick.

**Go deeper**

- [Claus Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization* (2019)](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/) — free online. Read chapters 5–7 (distributions), 17 (proportional ink, which is the formal version of the zero-baseline rule) and 19 (common pitfalls). **The single best book on this subject for a working analyst.**
- [Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983)](https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/the-visual-display-of-quantitative-information/) — read chapters 2 and 4 for the lie factor and the data-ink ratio; the lie factor is what the war story's chart violated and it is worth being able to compute.
- [Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, *Storytelling with Data* (2015)](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/books) — the practitioner's counterpart to Tufte, and better on the specific question of what a chart in a business presentation should do; read chapters 2 and 6.
