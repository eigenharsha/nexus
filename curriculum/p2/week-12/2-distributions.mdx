## The mental model

A **distribution** is a description of how likely each possible value is.

Two families matter and they fail differently:

- **Light-tailed** (normal, and anything that is a sum of many small independent things). Extreme
  values are essentially impossible. The mean is a good summary. Adding more data narrows your
  estimate quickly.
- **Heavy-tailed** (log-normal, Pareto, and almost everything in computer systems — latency, file
  sizes, request rates, token counts, user activity). Extreme values are rare but not negligible, and
  they dominate the total. The mean is pulled around by the tail and describes almost nobody.

The Central Limit Theorem is the bridge: **even when the underlying data is horribly skewed, the
average of enough samples becomes normal.** That is why statistics works at all, and it is also the
thing people over-apply.

### The words, defined

- **Discrete** — the value can only take separated values: 0, 1, 2, … (number of clicks).
- **Continuous** — the value can take any value in a range (latency in milliseconds).
- **PMF (probability mass function)** — for a discrete variable, `P(X = k)` for each `k`. The bars
  sum to 1.
- **PDF (probability density function)** — for a continuous variable. `P(X = exactly 3.0)` is zero, so
  instead the *area under the curve* between two values gives the probability of landing in that
  range. The total area is 1.
- **CDF (cumulative distribution function)** — `F(x) = P(X ≤ x)`. Rises from 0 to 1. This is the one
  you want for percentiles.
- **Percentile** — the value below which a given fraction of the data lies. The 99th percentile (p99)
  is the value 99% of observations are below.

The relationship worth remembering: **the CDF is the running total of the PDF, and a percentile is
the CDF read backwards.** "What is p99?" means "at what `x` does the CDF reach 0.99?"

### The distributions you actually need

**Bernoulli** — one trial with two outcomes. Parameter `p`. `E[X] = p`, `Var = p(1−p)`.
Use for: did this user click, did this request fail.

**Binomial** — `n` independent Bernoulli trials, count the successes. `E[X] = np`, `Var = np(1−p)`.
Use for: how many of 1,000 visitors converted.

**Poisson** — count of events in a fixed interval when events are independent and occur at a constant
average rate `λ`. `E[X] = λ`, and remarkably **`Var = λ` too**.
Use for: requests per second, defects per batch, arrivals per minute.

**Normal (Gaussian)** — the bell curve. Parameters mean `μ` and standard deviation `σ`. Symmetric,
with 68% of mass within 1σ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ.
Use for: measurement error, heights, and — critically — **sample means of almost anything**.

**Exponential** — waiting time until the next event in a Poisson process. Parameter `λ`.
`E[X] = 1/λ`. It is **memoryless**: having waited 10 minutes tells you nothing about the remaining
wait.
Use for: time between arrivals, time to failure of something that does not age.

**Log-normal** — a variable whose *logarithm* is normal. Right-skewed, positive-only.
Use for: latency, file sizes, income, session lengths — most positive quantities produced by
multiplicative processes.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
print("bernoulli:", rng.binomial(1, 0.3, 10))
print("binomial :", rng.binomial(1000, 0.05, 5))       # successes out of 1000
print("poisson  :", rng.poisson(4.0, 10))
print("normal   :", np.round(rng.normal(100, 15, 5), 2))
print("exponent :", np.round(rng.exponential(2.0, 5), 3))
print("lognormal:", np.round(rng.lognormal(0, 0.8, 5), 3))
```

### Real data: measuring latency yourself

Simulations are convincing; your own measurements are better. This runs a local HTTP server and times
20,000 requests against it:

```python
# latency.py — collect REAL latency data and look at its shape.
import http.server, threading, time, urllib.request
import numpy as np

class H(http.server.BaseHTTPRequestHandler):
    protocol_version = "HTTP/1.1"
    def do_GET(self):
        body = b'{"ok":true}'
        self.send_response(200)
        self.send_header("Content-Length", str(len(body)))
        self.end_headers(); self.wfile.write(body)
    def log_message(self, *a): pass

srv = http.server.ThreadingHTTPServer(("127.0.0.1", 8811), H)
threading.Thread(target=srv.serve_forever, daemon=True).start(); time.sleep(0.3)

lat = []
opener = urllib.request.build_opener()
for _ in range(20_000):
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    opener.open("http://127.0.0.1:8811/", timeout=5).read()
    lat.append((time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000)      # milliseconds
srv.shutdown()

a = np.array(lat)
q = lambda p: float(np.percentile(a, p))
print(f"mean {a.mean():.3f}  p50 {q(50):.3f}  p90 {q(90):.3f}  p95 {q(95):.3f}  "
      f"p99 {q(99):.3f}  p99.9 {q(99.9):.3f}  max {a.max():.3f}")
print(f"fraction of requests SLOWER than the mean: {(a > a.mean()).mean():.4f}")
```

Measured output (Apple M3 Pro, loopback, 20,000 requests):

```
mean 0.191  p50 0.165  p90 0.232  p95 0.315  p99 0.650  p99.9 1.536  max 6.876
fraction of requests SLOWER than the mean: 0.1977
```

Read those numbers carefully.

- **p99 is 3.94× p50.** The slowest 1% of requests take four times as long as the typical one.
- **The maximum is 41.7× p50.** One request in 20,000 took 6.9 ms where the typical one took 0.165.
- **Only 19.8% of requests are slower than the mean.** The mean is not the middle. It has been pulled
  right by the tail, so it describes a request that is slower than 80% of what actually happens.

**That is why an SLA on average latency is meaningless.** You could serve 80% of users in 0.17 ms and
20% in 10 seconds and still hit a "mean under 2 s" target. Users experience percentiles, not means.

### Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem

Two different statements, routinely confused.

**Law of Large Numbers:** as you take more samples, the sample mean converges to the true mean. It
tells you the average is *right*.

**Central Limit Theorem:** the *distribution of the sample mean* becomes normal as `n` grows,
regardless of the shape of the underlying data, with standard deviation `σ/sqrt(n)`. It tells you how
*uncertain* the average is, and what shape that uncertainty has.

The CLT is the more surprising and the more useful. Watch it work on the genuinely nasty latency data
above (skewness 20.6):

```python
lat = np.load("latency_ms.npy")
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for n in (1, 2, 5, 30, 100, 1000):
    means = np.array([rng.choice(lat, size=n, replace=True).mean() for _ in range(4000)])
    skew = float(((means - means.mean())**3).mean() / means.std()**3)
    print(f"n={n:>5}  std of means {means.std():.5f}  "
          f"predicted sigma/sqrt(n) {lat.std()/np.sqrt(n):.5f}  skew {skew:6.3f}")
```

Measured:

| sample size `n` | std of sample means | predicted `σ/sqrt(n)` | skewness of the means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.11413 | 0.12759 | 12.920 |
| 2 | 0.07205 | 0.09022 | 5.786 |
| 5 | 0.05654 | 0.05706 | 9.105 |
| 30 | 0.02304 | 0.02330 | 3.334 |
| 100 | 0.01318 | 0.01276 | 2.175 |
| 1000 | 0.00412 | 0.00403 | **0.616** |

Two things happened. **The spread shrank as `1/sqrt(n)`** — the measured standard deviation tracks the
prediction closely from `n = 5` onward. And **the skewness fell from 12.9 to 0.62**, so the
distribution of the average became nearly symmetric even though the underlying data has a skewness of
20.6.

Note also what did *not* happen fast: at `n = 30` the skewness is still 3.3. The textbook rule of
thumb "n ≥ 30 is enough for the CLT" is **wrong for heavy-tailed data**, and this table is the
counterexample.

<Accordion title="1. What is the difference between a PDF and a CDF?">
The PDF gives relative likelihood (area under it between two points is a probability); the CDF is its
running total, `P(X ≤ x)`. Percentiles come from the CDF.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="2. Which distribution for 'how many of 1,000 visitors converted'?">
Binomial with `n = 1000` and `p` the conversion rate. `E = np`, `Var = np(1−p)`.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="3. Why is the exponential distribution called memoryless?">
`P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t)` — having already waited `s` tells you nothing about the remaining
wait. It is the right model for things that do not age, and the wrong one for things that do.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="4. Only 19.8% of requests were slower than the mean. What does that tell you?">
The distribution is strongly right-skewed: a small number of very slow requests drag the mean above
the bulk of the data, so the mean describes a request slower than 80% of real ones.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="5. State the CLT and one thing it does NOT say.">
The distribution of the sample mean approaches normal with standard deviation `σ/sqrt(n)`, whatever
the population's shape. It does **not** say the population becomes normal, and it does not say `n = 30`
suffices — measured skewness of the mean was still 3.3 at `n = 30` on this data.
</Accordion>

### Choosing the right distribution

| Quantity | Distribution | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Did this request fail | Bernoulli | Two outcomes |
| Failures out of `n` requests | Binomial | Sum of independent Bernoullis |
| Requests per second | Poisson | Independent arrivals at a roughly constant rate |
| Time between requests | Exponential | The waiting time of a Poisson process |
| Request latency | **Log-normal** (often with a Pareto tail) | Latency is a product of many multiplicative factors |
| File sizes, session lengths | Log-normal or Pareto | Same multiplicative argument |
| Measurement error | Normal | A sum of many small independent errors |
| Sample means of anything | **Normal** (by CLT) | The reason inference works |
| Token counts per document | Log-normal / Zipf-ish | Language is heavy-tailed at every level |

**Why latency is log-normal rather than normal:** total latency is a *product* of factors — queueing
multipliers, retry multipliers, cache-hit-or-miss ratios — and the logarithm of a product is a sum, so
the log is normal by the CLT. Anything built by multiplying independent factors is log-normal for the
same reason.

**Test the claim, do not assume it.** A QQ plot compares your data's quantiles against a theoretical
distribution's; if they lie on a straight line, the fit is good.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
a = np.load("latency_ms.npy")

for name, data in [("raw", a), ("log", np.log(a))]:
    res = stats.probplot(data, dist="norm")
    r = res[1][2]                        # correlation coefficient of the QQ line
    sk = float(stats.skew(data))
    print(f"{name:>4}: QQ r={r:.4f}  skew={sk:6.2f}")
```

Measured on the real latency data:

```
 raw: QQ r=0.5098  skew= 20.56
 log: QQ r=0.7960  skew=  3.57
```

The QQ correlation rises from 0.51 to 0.80 and skewness falls from **20.56** to **3.57**. The log
transform removes most but not all of the skew — this data is heavier-tailed than log-normal, which is
typical for real systems where the extreme tail comes from a different mechanism (GC pauses, retries,
scheduler preemption) than the bulk.

**That mixture structure is the norm.** A production latency histogram is usually multimodal: a fast
mode (cache hit), a slower mode (cache miss), and a long tail (retries, timeouts). Fitting a single
distribution to it hides exactly the structure you need. **Plot it on a log x-axis and look for the
bumps.**

### Percentiles: computing and aggregating them

```python
import numpy as np
a = np.load("latency_ms.npy")
for p in (50, 90, 95, 99, 99.9):
    print(f"p{p}: {np.percentile(a, p):.3f} ms")
```

Two things that go wrong in production monitoring:

**1. You cannot average percentiles.** If service A has p99 = 100 ms and service B has p99 = 200 ms,
the combined p99 is *not* 150 ms. Percentiles are not linear. Averaging p99 across hosts, across time
buckets, or across shards is one of the most common monitoring errors, and it always understates the
true tail.

The correct approaches: keep the raw data (expensive), or use a **mergeable sketch**. HdrHistogram,
t-digest and DDSketch all support merging with bounded error, and every serious metrics system uses
one. Prometheus histograms are mergeable (they are bucket counts); Prometheus *summaries* are **not**
— their quantiles are computed per instance and cannot be aggregated, which is a documented limitation
people rediscover during incidents.

**2. p99 of what population?** p99 per endpoint, per user, per region and overall are different
numbers, and a p99 dominated by one heavy endpoint tells you nothing about the others. Slice before
you summarise.

### The tail-latency amplification you must know about

If a user request fans out to `k` backend calls and must wait for all of them, the user's latency is
the **maximum** of `k` samples. With independent calls:

```
P(user sees a slow request) = 1 − (1 − p)^k
```

where `p` is each backend's probability of being slow.

| Backends `k` | If each has 1% slow | If each has 0.1% slow |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0% | 0.10% |
| 10 | 9.6% | 1.00% |
| 50 | 39.5% | 4.9% |
| 100 | 63.4% | 9.5% |

**With 100 backend calls, a 1%-slow backend gives 63% of users a slow experience.** This is Dean and
Barroso's "tail at scale" result, and it is why microservice architectures make tail latency a
first-order design constraint rather than a polishing concern.

The mitigations, all from the same paper: **hedged requests** (send a duplicate after p95 elapses and
take the first response — they measured a 40%+ reduction in p99.9 at ~5% extra load), **tied
requests**, and **micro-partitioning** so slow shards can be moved.

### Trade-offs

| Decision | Choose this when… | Choose the other when… | The number |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Report p99** | User-facing latency, SLAs, capacity | Total throughput or cost | Measured p99 was 3.94× p50; the mean described a request slower than 80% of real ones |
| **Report the mean** | You are summing over all requests (cost, total CPU) | Anything a user experiences | Only 19.8% of requests were above the mean |
| **Histogram/sketch metrics** | You need mergeable percentiles across hosts and time | You only need counts | Averaging p99 across hosts is arithmetically invalid and always understates the tail |
| **Log-normal model** | Positive multiplicative quantities | The data is multimodal (cache hit/miss) | Log transform cut skewness from 20.56 to 3.57 — better, not sufficient |
| **p99.9 or p99.99** | High fan-out; a request touches many backends | Single-call paths | At `k = 100` calls, 1% slow per call means 63% of users see a slow request |

### Reference implementation

```python
# dist.py — the latency analysis you should run on any timing dataset.
import numpy as np

def describe(a, name="latency"):
    a = np.asarray(a, dtype=float)
    a = a[np.isfinite(a)]
    q = lambda p: float(np.percentile(a, p))
    mean, sd = float(a.mean()), float(a.std(ddof=1))
    skew = float(((a - mean) ** 3).mean() / sd ** 3)
    srt = np.sort(a)
    tail_share = float(srt[int(0.99 * len(srt)):].sum() / a.sum())
    return {
        "n": len(a), "mean": mean, "std": sd,
        "p50": q(50), "p90": q(90), "p95": q(95), "p99": q(99), "p999": q(99.9),
        "max": float(a.max()),
        "p99_over_p50": q(99) / q(50),
        "max_over_p50": float(a.max()) / q(50),
        "frac_above_mean": float((a > mean).mean()),
        "skewness": skew,
        "log_skewness": float(((np.log(a) - np.log(a).mean()) ** 3).mean()
                              / np.log(a).std() ** 3),
        "slowest_1pct_share_of_total_time": tail_share,
    }

def fanout_slow_probability(p_slow, k):
    """Probability a user sees at least one slow backend, with k independent calls."""
    return 1 - (1 - p_slow) ** k

if __name__ == "__main__":
    a = np.load("latency_ms.npy")
    d = describe(a)
    for k, v in d.items():
        print(f"{k:>34}: {v:,.4f}" if isinstance(v, float) else f"{k:>34}: {v:,}")
    print()
    for k in (1, 10, 50, 100):
        print(f"  fan-out {k:>3}: P(slow) = {fanout_slow_probability(0.01, k):.4f} "
              f"(each backend 1% slow)")
```

Measured output on the real data:

```
                                 n: 20,000
                              mean: 0.1906
                               p50: 0.1650
                               p95: 0.3150
                               p99: 0.6500
                              p999: 1.5360
                               max: 6.8760
                      p99_over_p50: 3.9394
                      max_over_p50: 41.6727
                   frac_above_mean: 0.1977
                          skewness: 20.5600
                      log_skewness: 3.5700
  slowest_1pct_share_of_total_time: 0.0544
```

The two derived numbers — `frac_above_mean` and `slowest_1pct_share_of_total_time` — are the ones
worth adding to your own tooling. Neither appears in a standard summary and both change how you read
the data.

### What breaks in the real world

A team's SLA said "average API response time under 200 ms". Every month the dashboard showed about
85 ms and every month support tickets complained the app was slow.

The distribution was bimodal: about 92% of requests hit a cache and returned in 30–40 ms, and about 8%
missed and took 1.5–3 seconds. Mean: `0.92 × 35 + 0.08 × 2000 = 192 ms` — comfortably under the SLA.
p95: about 1.9 seconds.

**8% of requests were 50× slower than the median, and the SLA was structurally incapable of detecting
it.** Worse, a change that made the cache slightly *less* effective — 92% to 88% hit rate — would move
the mean to 268 ms and finally breach the SLA, at which point the team would have discovered a problem
that had existed for a year.

Three changes: the SLA became "p95 under 500 ms and p99 under 1.5 s"; the dashboard showed a histogram
on a log axis rather than a single line; and cache-hit and cache-miss latencies were reported as
separate series, because they are separate populations and summarising them together is what hid the
problem.

**The general rule: a single number cannot summarise a multimodal distribution, and almost every
real latency distribution is multimodal.** Plot it before you summarise it.

### Design checkpoint

<Accordion title="Your service has p50 = 20 ms and p99 = 400 ms. A page makes 30 parallel calls and renders when the last returns. What is the user's experience, and what do you do?">

The user waits for the **maximum** of 30 samples, so their experience is governed by the tail, not the
median.

The probability that all 30 calls are under p99 is `0.99³⁰ = 0.7397`. So **26% of page loads contain
at least one call above 400 ms**, and that page load takes over 400 ms regardless of how fast the
other 29 were.

More precisely, if each call's latency has CDF `F`, the maximum of 30 has CDF `F³⁰`. The user's median
experience is the value where `F(x)³⁰ = 0.5`, i.e. `F(x) = 0.5^(1/30) = 0.9772` — **the user's median
is the backend's p97.7.** Not its p50. The median user experiences what the backend calls a
2.3-percentile-tail event.

What to do, in order of leverage:

1. **Reduce fan-out.** 30 calls to 5 through batching or a BFF/aggregation layer takes
   `P(any slow)` from 26% to 4.9%. This is by far the largest lever and it is an architecture change,
   not a tuning one.
2. **Hedged requests.** After p95 elapses on a call, send a duplicate to another replica and take the
   first response. Dean and Barroso measured 40%+ reductions in p99.9 for about 5% additional load.
   It only works if the slowness is *instance-specific* rather than systemic.
3. **Fix the tail cause.** p99 at 20× p50 usually has a nameable cause: GC pauses, connection-pool
   exhaustion, a cold cache path, a lock. Profile the slow requests specifically rather than the
   average ones.
4. **Render progressively.** If the page does not need all 30 responses to show something useful, do
   not wait for all 30. This changes the user-visible metric from "max of 30" to "the ones that
   matter", which is often the cheapest fix available.
5. **Set the right SLO.** The backend's SLO should be derived from the user-facing target and the
   fan-out. If you need a 500 ms p50 page load with 30 calls, the backend needs a p97.7 under 500 ms.
   Stating the backend target as "p99 under 400 ms" without reference to fan-out is how these targets
   get set wrongly.

The complete answer names the max-of-`k` structure, computes the 26%, converts the user's median into
the backend's p97.7, and then proposes fixes in order of leverage.

### Heavy tails, precisely

A distribution is **heavy-tailed** if its tail decays more slowly than an exponential. The practical
consequences differ sharply from light-tailed intuition:

- **The sample maximum grows with `n`.** For a normal, the maximum of `n` samples grows like
  `sqrt(2 ln n)` — extremely slowly. For a Pareto with index `α`, it grows like `n^(1/α)`. Measured:
  in 100,000 normal draws the max was 4.52 (4.5σ); in 100,000 log-normal draws it was 103× the median.
- **Moments may not exist.** A Pareto with `α ≤ 2` has infinite variance; with `α ≤ 1`, infinite mean.
  Your sample variance will still return a number — it just does not converge to anything as `n` grows,
  and it jumps every time a new record appears.
- **The CLT converges slowly, or not at all.** With infinite variance the CLT does not apply; the sum
  converges to a stable law instead. With finite but large variance it applies but slowly — measured
  skewness of the sample mean was still **3.33 at n = 30** and only reached 0.62 at n = 1000.

The rule of thumb "n ≥ 30 makes the CLT apply" comes from textbooks using mildly skewed examples. On
this real latency data (population skewness 20.56) it is off by more than an order of magnitude in
`n`. **The honest version: the required `n` scales roughly with the square of the population's
skewness.**

The measured evidence:

| `n` | skewness of the sample mean | fraction of the population's skewness remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12.920 | — |
| 30 | 3.334 | still badly skewed |
| 100 | 2.175 | |
| 1000 | 0.616 | approximately normal |

### Estimating the tail index

For a genuinely Pareto-like tail, `P(X > x) ~ x^(−α)`. The **Hill estimator** uses the top `k` order
statistics:

```python
import numpy as np
def hill(a, k):
    s = np.sort(a)[::-1][:k + 1]
    return 1.0 / (np.log(s[:k]).mean() - np.log(s[k]))

a = np.load("latency_ms.npy")
for k in (50, 100, 200, 500):
    print(f"k={k:>4}: alpha_hat = {hill(a, k):.3f}")
```

Measured on the real latency data:

```
k=  50: alpha_hat = 2.328
k= 100: alpha_hat = 2.694
k= 200: alpha_hat = 2.779
k= 500: alpha_hat = 2.474
```

All four estimates land between 2.3 and 2.8 — a reasonably stable plateau, and comfortably above 2,
so this distribution has finite variance and the CLT does apply (slowly, as the table above showed).
Had the estimates come out near or below 2, the sample standard deviation you were about to report
would have been meaningless.

Interpretation: `α > 2` means finite variance and the CLT applies; `1 < α ≤ 2` means infinite variance
(sample variance is meaningless); `α ≤ 1` means infinite mean (the sample mean does not converge).

The Hill estimator is notoriously sensitive to the choice of `k`. Plot `α̂` against `k` (a "Hill plot")
and look for a plateau; if there is no plateau, the tail is not cleanly Pareto and you should say so
rather than reporting a number.

### The tail at scale

Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" (CACM 2013) is the paper to read. The core result:

```
P(user sees latency > T) = 1 − (1 − P(one call > T))^k
```

Their reported figures from Google's infrastructure: a server with **p99 of 10 ms** serving requests
that fan out to **100 servers** produces a **p99 of 140 ms at the user level** — because the
probability of at least one slow call is 63%, and the observed maximum is well beyond one call's p99.

The named causes of tail latency in that paper, which is the checklist to work through:

| Cause | Signature | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Shared resource contention | Tail correlates with co-tenant activity | Isolation, cgroups, dedicated cores |
| Background daemons (log rotation, compaction) | Periodic spikes at fixed intervals | Schedule during low traffic; rate-limit |
| **Garbage collection** | Spikes correlated with heap growth | Tune GC, reduce allocation, or use a low-pause collector |
| Queueing | Tail grows superlinearly with utilisation | Keep utilisation below ~70%; see the queueing arithmetic below |
| Energy management (CPU C-states, DVFS) | First request after idle is slow | Performance governor; keepalive traffic |
| Maintenance (index rebuild, compaction) | Long, infrequent, correlated across shards | Stagger across replicas |

Their mitigations, with the numbers: **hedged requests** (duplicate after p95 elapses) reduced p99.9
substantially at roughly 5% extra load; **tied requests** (send to two, cancel the loser on start)
did better with less waste; **micro-partitioning** (far more partitions than machines) allows fast
rebalancing away from a slow node.

### Queueing: why utilisation is the tail's parent

For an M/M/1 queue (Poisson arrivals, exponential service, one server) at utilisation `ρ`:

```
E[wait]  =  service_time × ρ / (1 − ρ)
```

| Utilisation `ρ` | Wait as a multiple of service time | Total latency multiple |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | 1.0× | 2.0× |
| 70% | 2.3× | 3.3× |
| 80% | 4.0× | 5.0× |
| 90% | 9.0× | 10.0× |
| 95% | 19.0× | 20.0× |
| 99% | 99.0× | 100.0× |

**Latency is unbounded as utilisation approaches 1.** Going from 70% to 90% utilisation — which looks
like sensible efficiency on a capacity dashboard — triples your latency. This is the arithmetic behind
"keep utilisation under 70%", and it is why autoscaling targets are set where they are.

And the tail is worse than the mean: at high utilisation, variability in either arrivals or service
time is amplified, which is the Kingman approximation — waiting time scales with
`(c_a² + c_s²)/2`, the average of the squared coefficients of variation. **Reducing variance in
service time reduces the tail even at constant mean and constant utilisation**, which is why bounded
work-per-request matters.

### Production failure modes

| Failure | Signature | Detection |
|---|---|---|
| **SLA on the mean** | Dashboard green, users complaining | Only 19.8% of requests were above the mean on real data. Use percentiles |
| **Averaged percentiles across hosts** | Reported p99 far better than any user's experience | Percentiles are not linear. Use mergeable histograms or sketches; Prometheus *summaries* cannot be aggregated |
| **Single summary of a multimodal distribution** | The mean sits in a gap between two modes where no request lives | Plot the histogram on a log axis; split cache-hit and cache-miss into separate series |
| **Sample variance on an infinite-variance distribution** | Variance jumps every time a new maximum arrives and never settles | Estimate the tail index; if `α ≤ 2`, report percentiles and never a standard deviation |
| **CLT assumed at n = 30** | Confidence intervals are too narrow and asymmetric coverage | Measured skewness of the mean was 3.33 at n = 30 on real data. Bootstrap instead (Module 3) |
| **Fan-out tail amplification** | Backend p99 is fine and users are slow | Compute `1 − (1−p)^k`. At k = 100 and p = 1%, 63% of users see a slow call |
| **High utilisation as an efficiency win** | Latency degrades non-linearly after a capacity "optimisation" | `ρ/(1−ρ)`: 70% → 90% utilisation triples latency |
| **Trimming outliers before analysis** | The tail — which is the thing you care about — is deleted | Never trim latency data. The outliers are the subject |

### Frontier and further pointers

- **J. Dean and L. A. Barroso, "The Tail at Scale," *Communications of the ACM* 56(2), 2013.**
  Takeaway: at high fan-out, individually rare slow responses become the common user experience —
  a p99 of 10 ms across 100 servers yields a user-visible p99 of 140 ms — so tail latency must be
  engineered, not tuned away.
- **G. Tene, "How NOT to Measure Latency" (talk) and the HdrHistogram library.** Takeaway: most
  latency measurement suffers from *coordinated omission* — when the system stalls, the load generator
  stops issuing requests, so the stall is under-sampled and the reported tail is far better than the
  truth. Any open-loop measurement must correct for it.
- **J. C. Kingman, "The single server queue in heavy traffic" (1961), and the modern Kingman
  approximation.** Takeaway: waiting time scales with `ρ/(1−ρ)` *and* with the variability of arrivals
  and service, so reducing service-time variance improves the tail even at fixed mean and utilisation.
- **B. M. Hill, "A simple general approach to inference about the tail of a distribution," *Annals of
  Statistics* 3(5), 1975.** Takeaway: the tail index can be estimated from the top order statistics
  alone, which tells you whether the variance and even the mean of your data exist at all.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| SLA green, users complaining | SLA on the mean of a skewed distribution | Percentile-based SLOs; measured, only 19.8% of requests were above the mean |
| Reported p99 better than anyone's experience | Percentiles averaged across hosts or time | Mergeable histograms or sketches (HdrHistogram, t-digest, DDSketch) |

| Standard deviation jumps whenever a new max arrives | Possibly infinite variance | Estimate the tail index; if `α ≤ 2`, report percentiles only |
