## Outcome

<Note>
By Sunday you will be able to answer two questions properly: **"is model B actually better than model
A?"** and **"is this A/B result real?"** Both have the same shape, both are answered with a confidence
interval on a difference, and both are answered wrongly by most practitioners.
</Note>

Week 11 gave you the mathematics that models are made of. This week gives you the mathematics for
deciding whether a model, a change, or a claim is any good — which is the difference between doing
machine learning and doing it responsibly.

## The two questions this week exists to answer

**1. "Is model B actually better than model A?"** (Modules 1 and 3.)

You will meet a case with two real classifiers where model A's 95% confidence interval is
`[0.890, 0.953]`, model B's is `[0.925, 0.974]` — **overlapping** — and B is nonetheless decisively
better, with a paired difference interval of `[+0.0074, +0.0538]`. Comparing overlapping intervals is
the most common statistical error in machine-learning practice, and this week is where you stop making
it. This is exactly the Week 33 eval problem.

**2. "Is this A/B result real?"** (Module 4.)

An experiment produces a number regardless of whether it was run correctly. You will learn the checks
that decide whether to believe it: SRM (four lines of chi-square, and it invalidates more experiments
than anything else), peeking (measured: 20 looks turns a 5% false-positive rate into **25%**), and
interference.

## The three sentences to carry out of this week

1. **"At what prevalence?"** — the first question about any detector, because precision depends on the
   base rate and sensitivity and specificity do not.
2. **"What is the interval on the *difference*?"** — never compare two overlapping intervals; the
   paired difference is what decides.
3. **"Did you check SRM, and was the sample size fixed in advance?"** — the two questions that
   invalidate more experiment results than every other consideration combined.
