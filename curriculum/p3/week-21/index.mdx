## Outcome

<Note>You build a working reverse-mode autograd engine and a neural network on top of it, from
nothing but NumPy. By Sunday night you can derive backpropagation on a whiteboard, explain why
your gradient check passes, and state the exact number of gigabytes it takes to fine-tune a 7B
model. Nothing in Phase 4 is magic after this week.</Note>

## Why this week is the hinge of the course

Weeks 17–20 taught you models that fit in your head: a linear regression has one closed-form
solution, a decision tree is a nested `if`. From this week on, every model you touch is a
composition of differentiable functions trained by gradient descent — CNNs in Week 22,
Transformers in Week 25, LoRA fine-tunes in Week 26. All of them run the same loop:
forward pass, loss, backward pass, optimizer step.

Two specific things you build this week get cashed in later:

  <Card title="The autograd engine (Module 2)" icon="git-branch">
    A 180-line `Value` class with a topological backward pass. When Week 25 shows you a
    Transformer block, you will read it as a graph you already know how to differentiate,

    bf16, batch size, sequence length.
  </Card>

## Where this week's numbers come from

Read [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers) once before you trust any figure on these pages. **All three of this week's
"reference machines" are modelled** — none of them was run. The authors had no CUDA GPU, no cloud
instance, and no PyTorch installed at all, so the training curves and timings below describe what
these machines would plausibly do rather than what they did.

| Label | Machine | Class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Machine A** | MacBook Pro M3 Pro, 12-core CPU, 18 GB unified memory, macOS 14.6, Python 3.12.4, PyTorch 2.4.0 (MPS backend) | **Reference figure** — modelled; PyTorch was never installed by the authors, so nothing was run on this machine either |
| **Machine B** | Ubuntu 22.04, Ryzen 9 7950X (16C/32T), 64 GB DDR5-5600, RTX 4090 24 GB, CUDA 12.4, PyTorch 2.4.0+cu124 | **Reference figure** — modelled from published benchmarks and vendor specs; the authors did not have this machine |
| **Machine C** | AWS `c7i.xlarge` — 4 vCPU Sapphire Rapids, 8 GB RAM, Ubuntu 22.04 (serving figures in Weeks 23–24) | **Reference figure** — modelled, not run |

What you *can* trust completely is the **derived** material: the VRAM arithmetic in Module 4, the
parameter counts, the activation-memory formula and the gradient-check tolerances are computed term
by term from first principles and are independent of any machine. That is the backbone of the week,
and Module 4's calculation is the single most valuable number in it — check the algebra rather than
the hardware.

For everything else, trust the *ratio* the lesson is teaching, not the decimal. The lab hands you
the harness: your own MNIST run on your own laptop is worth more than any number on this page.

## Exit checks

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Can you do these five things by Sunday?" icon="circle-check">
    1. Derive `dL/dW1` for a 2-layer network on paper, with correct matrix shapes at every step.
    2. Explain why `relu` backward is `grad * (x > 0)` and what happens to a unit whose pre-activation is negative for every sample in the dataset.
    3. Make `gradcheck` pass on a 3-layer network you wrote yourself, to within 1e-7 relative error.
    4. State from memory the four things that occupy GPU memory during training, and their sizes for a 7B model.
    5. Look at a loss curve and name the pathology — bad LR, dead units, leakage, or overfitting — before you look at the code.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
