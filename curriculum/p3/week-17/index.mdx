## The story this week tells

Show a person five house prices and ask about a sixth, and they answer without being given a
formula. The rule fell out of the examples.

That is all machine learning is, and this week you build it from nothing: a guess, a score, a
nudge — repeated until a rule nobody wrote down is sitting inside a handful of numbers. You
will derive the mathematics by hand, make the same machinery answer yes-or-no questions, meet
the failure that makes a perfect score worthless, and only then pick up the library everyone
uses.

<Card title="This week — learning, from the arithmetic up" icon="sparkles">

  - **Make a line say “probably fraud”** — and see where the business decision hides

## The through-line

Five modules, one argument. **M1** says a model is a function chosen from a family by minimising a loss on data you are allowed to see. **M2** picks the simplest family — a line — derives its gradient, and shows that feature scale decides whether training converges at all (condition number `5.6e10` down to `42.8`, and a learning rate that diverges to `inf` becomes one that reaches the optimum). **M3** swaps the output through a sigmoid and the loss to cross-entropy, derives that gradient too, and proves why mean squared error is the wrong partner for a sigmoid — the curvature goes negative and the gradient is **203x** too small exactly where the model is most wrong. **M4** adds a penalty and measures the bias-variance trade-off directly: ridge cuts a degree-15 polynomial's variance from **21,765 to 0.198**. **M5** hands the whole thing to scikit-learn and shows the one structural mistake that turns pure noise into **81.5% cross-validated accuracy**.

Every gradient you derive this week reappears in Week 21 as a layer of a neural network. That is not a metaphor: `X^T (p - y)` is literally the backward pass of a linear layer with a cross-entropy head.

## Prerequisites check

Before Monday you should be able to: multiply two matrices by hand and say the output shape; take a partial derivative with the chain rule; explain what a standard deviation is; and write a `for` loop in Python that accumulates a running total. If any of those is shaky, revisit Week 11 (linear algebra and calculus) and Week 12 (probability) first — this week goes fast and does not re-teach them.
