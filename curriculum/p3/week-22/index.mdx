## Outcome

<Note>You train a real computer-vision model to a stated accuracy bar — ≥90% on a blood-cell
dataset that is genuinely messy — and you understand transfer learning well enough to see that
it is the *same idea* you will apply to a large language model in Week 26.</Note>

## Why this week matters more than "learn CNNs"

Convolutional networks are not the frontier any more. This week is not really about CNNs; it is
about three things that transfer directly:

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Transfer learning is LoRA (Module 4)" icon="git-branch">
    Frozen backbone + new head is structurally identical to frozen LLM weights + LoRA adapter.
    Same freeze/unfreeze schedule, same discriminative learning rates, same catastrophic-forgetting
    failure. Module 4 draws the mapping explicitly, term by term.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Embeddings + nearest neighbour is RAG (Module 5)" icon="search">
    An image similarity search over 50,000 images is the *same architecture* as text retrieval
    in Week 27 — encoder, index, ANN search, recall/latency trade-off. You build it here on data
    you can look at.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Mixed precision is quantization (Module 3)" icon="binary">
    FP16 overflow, `GradScaler`, BF16's wider exponent — the numerics you meet here are exactly
    the numerics of Week 26's 8-bit and 4-bit weights, one step less extreme.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Residual connections are why Transformers train (Module 2)" icon="link">
    ResNet's skip connection makes the block Jacobian `I + ∂F/∂x`. Every Transformer block has
    two of them, for exactly this reason.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Where this week's numbers come from

Every figure on these pages is labelled with its class. **None of the three machines below was
run** — the authors had no GPU, no cloud instance and no PyTorch installed — so every training
curve and timing in this week is modelled. See [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers).

| Label | Machine | Class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Machine A** | MacBook Pro M3 Pro, 12-core CPU, 18 GB unified memory, macOS 14.6, Python 3.12.4, PyTorch 2.4.0 (MPS) | **Reference figure** — modelled; PyTorch was never installed by the authors |
| **Machine B** | Ubuntu 22.04, Ryzen 9 7950X (16C/32T), 64 GB DDR5-5600, RTX 4090 24 GB, CUDA 12.4, PyTorch 2.4.0+cu124 | **Reference figure** — modelled from published benchmarks and vendor specs; not run by the authors |
| **Machine C** | AWS `c7i.xlarge` — 4 vCPU Sapphire Rapids, 8 GB RAM, Ubuntu 22.04 (CPU inference figures) | **Reference figure** — modelled, not run |

Convolution output-size arithmetic, parameter counts and activation-memory sums are **derived**:
they follow from the formulas and hold on any machine. Those are the numbers to carry. Everywhere
else the *ratio* is the teachable claim — re-measure the absolute on your own hardware before
quoting it, and let your lab run replace these figures.

Pinned versions used throughout: `torch==2.4.0`, `torchvision==0.19.0`,
`opencv-python-headless==4.10.0.84`, `onnx==1.16.2`, `onnxruntime==1.19.2`, `faiss-cpu==1.8.0`,
`pillow==10.4.0`, `numpy==2.1.1`.

## The dataset

Every module uses the same one so the numbers compose: the **BCCD blood-cell images** —
12,500 augmented 320×240 JPEGs across four white-blood-cell classes (eosinophil, lymphocyte,
monocyte, neutrophil). It is a good teaching dataset because it is genuinely imperfect: the
classes are close to balanced but the augmentation is visible, two of the four classes are hard
to tell apart even for a human, and the JPEG quality varies.
