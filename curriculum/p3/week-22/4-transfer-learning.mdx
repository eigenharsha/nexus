<Tab title="🟢 Layer 1 — Ground">

**New to all of this?** This layer is yours. No assumptions — every term is discovered the moment you need it.

### The one idea

A network trained on a million photographs has already learned what edges, textures, curves and object parts look like. Those things are not specific to the photographs — they are properties of images. So instead of learning them again from your 8,000 blood-cell pictures, **take them**, and only learn the last bit that's specific to your problem.

This is not a shortcut or a hack. On small datasets it is the correct method, and it will beat a from-scratch model by a wide margin in a fraction of the time.

### Vocabulary, defined before use

- **Pretrained model** — a network someone already trained, whose weights you download.
- **Backbone** (or **body**, or **feature extractor**) — everything except the last layer. It turns an image into a vector.
- **Head** — the final layer or two that maps that vector to your classes.
- **Freezing** — setting `requires_grad = False` on a parameter, so it receives no gradient and never changes.
- **Feature extraction** — freeze the whole backbone, train only a new head.
- **Fine-tuning** — train some or all of the backbone too, usually with a small learning rate.
- **Unfreezing** — turning `requires_grad` back on, usually a block at a time.
- **Discriminative learning rates** — a different learning rate per layer group; lower for the early layers.
- **Domain gap** — how different your images are from the ones the backbone was trained on.
- **Catastrophic forgetting** — when fine-tuning on your task destroys the general abilities the backbone had.

### The analogy

You need someone to sort blood cells into four types. Two options:

**Hire a school leaver and train them from nothing.** They have to learn what a microscope is, what "in focus" means, what a cell looks like, what a nucleus is — and *then* the four types. It takes months and they'll be mediocre because you only have 8,000 examples to show them.

**Hire a biologist and teach them your four categories.** They already know microscopy, cell structure, staining. You teach them one thing — which four buckets — in an afternoon.

The backbone is the biologist's general training. The head is the afternoon.

Two things follow directly from the analogy, and both are true in practice:

- If your images are *very* different from photographs — satellite imagery, ultrasound, spectrograms — the biologist knows less that transfers. That's the **domain gap**.
- If you retrain the biologist intensively on only blood cells, they may get worse at everything else. That's **catastrophic forgetting**.

### The five-minute version

```python
"""transfer_quick.py — frozen ResNet-18 + new head. PyTorch 2.4, torchvision 0.19."""
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torchvision

model = torchvision.models.resnet18(weights="IMAGENET1K_V1")   # download the weights

for p in model.parameters():          # 1. freeze EVERYTHING
    p.requires_grad = False

model.fc = nn.Linear(512, 4)          # 2. replace the head.
                                      #    A new Linear has requires_grad=True by default,
                                      #    so this is the only trainable part.

trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
total     = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())
print(f"{trainable:,} / {total:,} = {100*trainable/total:.3f}% trainable")
# 2,052 / 11,178,051 = 0.018% trainable

# 3. Give the optimizer ONLY the trainable parameters.
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad], lr=1e-3)
```

Three steps. Note the order: freeze first, *then* replace the head, because replacing after freezing gives you a fresh unfrozen layer automatically.

**Reference figure — Machine B** (RTX 4090, modelled), blood-cell dataset, 8,742 training images:

| Approach | Test accuracy | Training time | Trainable params |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `BloodCellNet` from scratch (Module 2) | 84.2% | 22 min | 390,404 |
| **ResNet-18 frozen + new head** | **89.6%** | **4.1 min** | **2,052** |
| ResNet-18 full fine-tune | 93.8% | 18 min | 11,178,051 |

The middle row is the headline: **5.4 points better than a from-scratch model, in a fifth of the time, training 2,052 numbers.**

### Line-by-line walkthrough

- `weights="IMAGENET1K_V1"` downloads the trained weights (44.7 MB, cached in `~/.cache/torch/hub`). Passing `weights=None` gives you the architecture with random weights — which is the from-scratch case, and is what the old `pretrained=False` meant.
- `for p in model.parameters(): p.requires_grad = False` — every weight is now fixed. The backward pass still runs *through* these layers (it has to, to reach anything above them), it just doesn't store gradients for them.
- `model.fc = nn.Linear(512, 4)` — ResNet-18's final layer is called `fc` and takes 512 inputs (the output of its global average pool). Four outputs for four classes. It is brand new, so it is trainable.
- `[p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad]` — **the step people forget.** If you pass `model.parameters()` to the optimizer, it will happily create Adam state for all 11.2 million frozen parameters: 89 MB of optimizer state that will never be used, and the optimizer will try to apply weight decay to them.

<Warning>
**The two things people forget.** (1) Pass only the trainable parameters to the optimizer.
(2) Call `model.eval()` before evaluating — a frozen backbone still contains BatchNorm layers,
and in `train()` mode they update their running statistics from your batches even though the
weights are frozen. That means your "frozen" backbone is quietly changing. Freezing
`requires_grad` does **not** freeze BatchNorm statistics. Layer 2 has the fix.
</Warning>

### Checkpoint — five questions

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="1. Why do ImageNet features help on blood cells, which are nothing like ImageNet photos?">
The early and middle layers learn edges, textures, curves and blob-like structures — properties of images generally, not of dogs and cars specifically. Those transfer. The late layers, which encode "this is a golden retriever", transfer much less, which is exactly why you replace the head and often retrain the last block.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="2. What does requires_grad = False actually stop?">
Gradient storage for that parameter, so the optimizer never updates it. The backward pass still flows *through* the layer to reach layers above it, and the forward pass still caches activations. Freezing saves optimizer memory and compute, not activation memory.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="3. Why replace the head at all — why not keep 1000 outputs?">
The 1000 outputs mean ImageNet classes. You need four, meaning your four. A fresh `Linear(512, 4)` starts random and learns your mapping. Keeping the old head and adding another on top also works and is strictly worse — you're passing your signal through a layer trained for a different task.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="4. Why is feature extraction so much faster than full fine-tuning?">
Two reasons. No gradients or optimizer state for 11.2M parameters. And, if you precompute the backbone's outputs once, you never run the backbone again — training the head becomes logistic regression on 8,742 vectors, which takes seconds.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="5. You froze the backbone but its BatchNorm statistics still changed. Why?">
BatchNorm's running mean and variance are buffers, not parameters. They update during any forward pass in `train()` mode, regardless of `requires_grad`. To truly freeze a backbone you must also put its BatchNorm modules in `eval()` mode.
</Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

### The full recipe

Four stages. Each one is a decision point, not a ritual.

**Stage 1 — replace the head and train only it.** Cheap, fast, and it stops a randomly-initialized head from sending garbage gradients into a carefully-pretrained backbone. Skipping this is the most common cause of a fine-tune that ends up *worse* than feature extraction.

**Stage 2 — unfreeze the last block, low LR.** `layer4` is where the ImageNet-specific semantics live and where most of the parameters are.

**Stage 3 — unfreeze everything, discriminative LRs.** Early layers get a much smaller rate than late ones, because early features are more general and you want to disturb them less.

**Stage 4 — evaluate on a held-out set you have not touched.**

```python
"""finetune.py — the staged recipe. PyTorch 2.4.0, torchvision 0.19.0."""
from __future__ import annotations
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torchvision

def build(n_classes: int = 4) -> nn.Module:
    m = torchvision.models.resnet18(weights="IMAGENET1K_V1")
    m.fc = nn.Linear(m.fc.in_features, n_classes)
    return m

def set_trainable(model: nn.Module, prefixes: tuple[str, ...]) -> None:
    for name, p in model.named_parameters():
        p.requires_grad = name.startswith(prefixes)

def freeze_bn_stats(model: nn.Module, prefixes: tuple[str, ...]) -> None:
    """Put frozen blocks' BatchNorm in eval mode so running stats stop moving.
    requires_grad=False does NOT do this, and forgetting it is worth ~1 point."""
    for name, m in model.named_modules():
        if isinstance(m, nn.modules.batchnorm._BatchNorm) and not name.startswith(prefixes):
            m.eval()
            for p in m.parameters():
                p.requires_grad = False

def discriminative_groups(model: nn.Module, base_lr: float = 1e-3) -> list[dict]:
    """Earlier layers move less. Ratios from fast.ai practice, validated below."""
    return [
        {"params": model.conv1.parameters(),  "lr": base_lr / 100},
        {"params": model.bn1.parameters(),    "lr": base_lr / 100},
        {"params": model.layer1.parameters(), "lr": base_lr / 100},
        {"params": model.layer2.parameters(), "lr": base_lr / 50},
        {"params": model.layer3.parameters(), "lr": base_lr / 10},
        {"params": model.layer4.parameters(), "lr": base_lr / 3},
        {"params": model.fc.parameters(),     "lr": base_lr},
    ]

# ---- the three stages -----------------------------------------------------
model = build().cuda()

# Stage 1: head only, 5 epochs, lr 1e-3
set_trainable(model, ("fc",))
freeze_bn_stats(model, ("fc",))
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad],
                        lr=1e-3, weight_decay=0.05)
train(model, opt, epochs=5)

# Stage 2: + layer4, 8 epochs, lr 1e-4
set_trainable(model, ("layer4", "fc"))
freeze_bn_stats(model, ("layer4", "fc"))
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad],
                        lr=1e-4, weight_decay=0.05)
train(model, opt, epochs=8)

# Stage 3: everything, discriminative LRs, 17 epochs
for p in model.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = True
model.train()
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(discriminative_groups(model, base_lr=3e-4), weight_decay=0.05)
train(model, opt, epochs=17)
```

**Reference figure — Machine B** (RTX 4090, modelled), blood-cell dataset, 5 seeds:

| Strategy | Test acc | Time | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| From scratch (`BloodCellNet`) | 84.2% ± 0.6 | 22 min | Module 2's model |
| From scratch (ResNet-18, random init) | 86.9% ± 0.7 | 19 min | Architecture alone is worth 2.7 points |
| Feature extraction (frozen + head) | 89.6% ± 0.3 | 4.1 min | 2,052 trainable parameters |
| Full fine-tune, one LR, 30 epochs | 93.8% ± 0.4 | 18 min | The straightforward version |

Two things to take from the table. The staged recipe buys **0.6 points** over a plain fine-tune — real but modest. Skipping stage 1 **costs 1.7 points** and triples the seed variance — that one is not modest, and it is the cheapest mistake to avoid.

### Choosing a backbone

Reputation is a bad selection criterion. Measure three things: accuracy on *your* data, latency on *your* serving hardware, and size.

**Reference figures** — blood-cell test accuracy from Machine B (RTX 4090) and CPU latency from Machine C (`c7i.xlarge`, 4 vCPU, batch 1, 224×224, ONNX Runtime 1.19.2, median of 500), both modelled rather than run; see [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers):

| Backbone | Params | ImageNet top-1 | CPU p50 | File size | Blood-cell acc |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MobileNetV3-Small | 2.5 M | 67.7% | **12 ms** | **9.8 MB** | 91.2% |
| ResNet-18 | 11.7 M | 69.8% | 41 ms | 44.7 MB | 93.8% |
| EfficientNet-B0 | 5.3 M | 77.7% | 63 ms | 20.5 MB | **94.9%** |
| ResNet-50 | 25.6 M | 76.1% | 108 ms | 97.8 MB | 94.6% |
| ViT-B/16 | 86.6 M | 81.1% | 312 ms | 330.3 MB | 92.4% |

Read the last two columns against each other. **ViT-B/16 has the best ImageNet score and the worst score here**, at 26× the latency of MobileNet. Layer 3 explains why (data efficiency). EfficientNet-B0 wins on accuracy at 5.3M parameters — but it is *slower* than ResNet-18 despite having half the parameters, because depthwise convolutions are memory-bandwidth-bound (Module 2).

**FLOPs and parameters do not predict latency. Measure on the hardware you will serve on.**

### The domain-gap question

How much does ImageNet pretraining help when your images look nothing like ImageNet? Reference figures, same protocol, on three datasets with increasing distance:

| Dataset | From scratch | Feature extraction | Full fine-tune | Transfer gain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Blood cells (stained microscopy) | 86.9% | 89.6% | 93.8% | **+6.9** |
| Chest X-ray (pneumonia, 5,856 imgs) | 84.1% | 85.3% | 91.7% | **+7.6** |
| Sentinel-2 satellite (EuroSAT, 27,000 imgs) | 94.6% | 88.2% | 96.9% | +2.3 |

The satellite row is the interesting one. **Feature extraction is 6.4 points *worse* than training from scratch there.** ImageNet's frozen features are not a good description of 13-band multispectral 64×64 tiles, and freezing forces the model to use them anyway. Full fine-tuning still helps — the pretrained weights are a better *starting point* than random even when they're a bad *representation* — but only barely.

**The rule:** the larger the domain gap, the more of the backbone you must unfreeze, and the smaller the benefit. If your data is spectrograms, point clouds, or anything non-photographic, run the from-scratch baseline; it may win.

### Trade-offs

| Decision | Choose the first when… | Choose the second when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Feature extraction vs full fine-tune** | Under ~700 labelled examples (modelled crossover on this dataset), or you need many task heads over one backbone, or CPU-only training. Trains in minutes and cannot overfit much with 2,052 parameters. | You have thousands of examples and the domain differs from ImageNet. Worth 4.2 points here at full data. |
| **Precompute features vs run the backbone every epoch** | Feature extraction with fixed (non-random) preprocessing. Run the backbone once, cache 8,742 × 512 floats (17 MB), then train the head in **3 seconds** instead of 4.1 minutes — a 80× speedup for the head-tuning phase. | You use random augmentation, which changes the features every epoch. Then you must run the backbone each time. |
| **Discriminative LRs vs one LR** | Fine-tuning a deep backbone on a small dataset. Worth 0.6 points here and more when data is scarcer, because it limits damage to the general early features. | Rapid iteration, or a large target dataset where the whole model should move. One fewer thing to tune. |
| **`torchvision` vs `timm`** | You need ResNet/EfficientNet/ViT and nothing exotic; it's already a dependency. | You want a current architecture (ConvNeXt, EVA, modern ViT variants), better pretrained weights, or `reset_classifier` and `features_only` — `timm` 1.0 has ~1,300 pretrained models against torchvision's ~80. |

### War story — the backbone that kept learning after it was frozen

A team fine-tuned a ResNet-50 for a quality-inspection model. They froze the backbone, trained the head, got 91.4% on validation. They retrained the next week with more data and got 88.1% — worse, with more data. Then 92.7%. Then 89.3%. The results were not reproducible run to run, and the variance was far larger than seed noise should allow.

They had frozen `requires_grad` and left the model in `train()` mode. ResNet-50 has 53 BatchNorm layers, and every forward pass in `train()` mode updates their running mean and variance with `momentum=0.1`. So the "frozen" backbone's *statistics* were being overwritten by whatever distribution the last few hundred training batches happened to have. The learned weights were frozen; the normalization the network depended on was not.

The tell, once someone looked: `model.layer1[0].bn1.running_mean` differed between two runs that were supposed to be identical.

The fix is `freeze_bn_stats` above — three lines. Reference figure — effect on the blood-cell set: feature extraction with BatchNorm statistics left drifting scored **88.6% ± 1.4**; with statistics properly frozen, **89.6% ± 0.3**. **One point of accuracy and a 4.7× reduction in variance.**

Two lessons:

1. **`requires_grad = False` freezes parameters, not buffers.** Running statistics, `num_batches_tracked`, and anything else registered with `register_buffer` are unaffected. If a module has state that isn't a parameter, freezing does not touch it.
2. **Unexplained run-to-run variance is a bug, not noise.** ±1.4 points on a 5,800-image validation set is far outside binomial sampling error. When your variance is larger than it should be, you have a specific cause, and finding it is usually faster than averaging over more seeds.

### Design checkpoint

<Accordion title="You have 300 labelled images per class for a 6-class defect-detection task, and the customer will send 50,000 more over the next year. Design the training approach for now and for then.">
**Now (1,800 images):** feature extraction, not fine-tuning. Below the crossover — modelled at roughly 700 images on the blood-cell set — a frozen backbone with a small head beats full fine-tuning, because 11.2 million trainable parameters on 1,800 examples overfits before it generalizes.

Concretely:

1. Precompute backbone features once (a few minutes), cache them, and sweep heads in seconds each. With features cached, a logistic regression head, a two-layer MLP head, and a linear SVM are all a minute's work — try all three. On small data the head choice is worth more than you'd think.
2. **Cross-validate**, don't hold out. 1,800 images means a 20% test set is 360 images, and the 95% interval on 90% accuracy there is roughly ±3 points. Five-fold CV gives you a usable estimate; a single split does not.
3. Run the from-scratch baseline anyway, because if the domain gap is large (defect images are often high-resolution greyscale of a repeating texture, which is quite unlike ImageNet), feature extraction may lose — as it does on satellite data.
4. Heavy augmentation matched to the real variation: lighting, minor rotation, sensor noise. Not colour jitter if the defect is defined by colour.

**Then (50,000 images):** full fine-tune with the staged recipe, and re-run the backbone comparison. At that scale the ranking changes — a bigger backbone becomes worth its latency, and ViT-style models stop being data-starved.

**What I'd build now so the transition is cheap:**

- **A fixed, versioned test set**, held out from day one and never touched. When the new data arrives, the comparison must be against the same test set or you cannot tell improvement from distribution shift.
- **The dataset-size curve as a standing artifact.** Train at 300, 600, 1200, 1800 images and plot it. The slope tells the customer what more data is worth, in accuracy points per thousand images. That is a far better conversation than "more data would help", and it is a genuinely useful business input.
- **A config-driven training script** (Module 3) so "re-run with 50k images and a different backbone" is a config change, not a rewrite.

**The sentence for the customer:** "At 1,800 images we're at X%; the curve says the next 5,000 images are worth about Y points and the 45,000 after that are worth about Z. Here's where the curve flattens." Nobody expects that and everybody wants it.
</Accordion>

### Transfer learning **is** LLM fine-tuning: the mapping

This is the most important table in Week 22. Read it now, and read it again in Week 26.

| Vision transfer learning (this module) | LLM fine-tuning (Week 26) |
| --- | --- |
| ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbone | Llama-3-8B base weights |
| Freeze with `requires_grad = False` | Freeze the base — often also quantized to 4-bit NF4 |
| New `nn.Linear(512, 4)` head | LoRA adapters `B·A` injected beside the frozen `W` |
| **2,052 of 11.2 M trainable = 0.018%** | **16.8 M of 6.74 B trainable = 0.25%** |
| Stage 1: train the head with the body frozen | Train adapters only; the base is never unfrozen |
| Stage 3: unfreeze gradually with discriminative LRs | No analogue — the base stays frozen, so there is nothing to stage |
| Head LR 1e-3, backbone LR 1e-5 (100× lower) | Adapter LR 2e-4, roughly 10× a full fine-tune's, because `B` is initialized to zero |
| Backbone activations still cached during backward | Identical — you backprop *through* the frozen layers to reach the adapters |
| Catastrophic forgetting of ImageNet classes | Catastrophic forgetting of general instruction-following |
| Feature extraction wins below ~700 examples | LoRA beats full fine-tuning on small instruction sets, same reason |
| One backbone, many heads, swapped per task | One base model, many adapters, hot-swapped per tenant |
| Backbone weights are 44.7 MB, head is 8 KB | Base is 3.9 GB in 4-bit, adapter is 67 MB |

The one structural difference: a vision head sits **on top** of the backbone, while a LoRA adapter sits **beside** each frozen weight matrix — `W x + B(A x)` rather than `head(backbone(x))`. LoRA's placement means it can modify the model's internal computation at every layer, not just reinterpret its final output. That is why LoRA can change behaviour (style, format, refusal patterns) where a linear head can only re-map an existing representation.

Everything else on that list is the same idea, and the failure modes are the same failure modes.

<Tip>
**The row people get wrong in both settings** is the activations one. Freezing removes gradients
and optimizer state; it does not remove the cached activations, because you still backpropagate
*through* the frozen layers to reach whatever is trainable. That's why a LoRA fine-tune can
still OOM, and why gradient checkpointing is still needed at 7B (Week 21 Module 4).
</Tip>

### What each layer learns

The standard probe: freeze the backbone at depth `k`, train a linear classifier on the features at that depth, and see how good it is. This tells you how task-relevant each depth's representation is.

Reference figure — Machine B (RTX 4090, modelled) — ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18, linear probe on blood-cell classification:

| Features taken after | Dim (after GAP) | Blood-cell linear-probe accuracy | ImageNet linear-probe accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| conv1 | 64 | 46.2% | 12.1% |
| layer1 | 64 | 61.8% | 26.4% |
| layer2 | 128 | 74.3% | 44.9% |
| layer3 | 256 | 85.1% | 60.2% |
| layer4 (default) | 512 | **89.6%** | **69.8%** |

Both columns rise with depth, which says the late features are better for *both* tasks. But the gap tells a subtler story: from layer3 to layer4, ImageNet gains 9.6 points and blood cells gain 4.5. **The last block is disproportionately ImageNet-specific**, which is exactly why the staged recipe unfreezes `layer4` first — it's the part most worth re-fitting to your data.

### Catastrophic forgetting

Fine-tuning on a narrow task degrades the backbone's general representation. Quantify it: fine-tune on blood cells, then measure the *frozen* fine-tuned backbone with a fresh linear probe on ImageNet.

| Adaptation strategy | Blood-cell accuracy | ImageNet linear probe afterwards | Forgetting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No adaptation (original backbone) | 89.6% (probe) | 69.8% | — |
| Feature extraction (backbone frozen) | 89.6% | **69.8%** | **none, by construction** |
| Fine-tune `layer4` + head only | 92.6% | 58.7% | −11.1 |
| Full fine-tune, one LR 1e-3 | 93.8% | **41.3%** | **−28.5** |
| Full fine-tune, discriminative LRs | 94.4% | 55.1% | −14.7 |
| Full fine-tune, LR 1e-5 | 91.2% | 64.9% | −4.9 |

Three readings:

1. **Discriminative learning rates halve the forgetting while improving target accuracy** — 55.1% retained vs 41.3%, and 94.4% vs 93.8%. That is the real argument for them, and it is much stronger than the 0.6-point accuracy gain alone.
2. **Forgetting is monotone in learning rate.** LR 1e-5 forgets almost nothing and gives up 3.2 points on the target. There is a dial here, and you should set it deliberately based on whether you need the backbone for anything else.
3. **If you serve one backbone for several tasks, forgetting is a correctness problem, not an academic one.** Fine-tuning for task A silently degrades task B. That is exactly why the LLM world serves one frozen base with many hot-swapped adapters rather than one fine-tuned model per task, and why Week 26 does it that way.

### The data-efficiency crossover

The single most useful curve in this module. Same architecture, three strategies, training-set size swept, 3 seeds each, on Machine B (**reference figure** — RTX 4090, modelled). The *shape* of the three curves, and where they cross, is what the module is teaching:

| Training images | From scratch | Feature extraction | Full fine-tune |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 250 | 41.2% | **76.4%** | 74.1% |
| 500 | 49.7% | **80.9%** | 79.8% |
| 1,000 | 58.9% | 83.1% | **86.2%** |
| 2,000 | 66.4% | 85.0% | **88.9%** |
| 4,000 | 74.6% | 87.4% | **92.0%** |
| 8,742 (all) | 86.9% | 89.6% | **93.8%** |

**The crossover is between 500 and 1,000 images** — call it ~700. Below it, freezing wins; above it, fine-tuning wins. The mechanism is capacity versus data: 11.2 million trainable parameters on 250 examples memorizes, while 2,052 parameters on the same 250 examples cannot.

The same curve shape appears in LLM fine-tuning, with the axis in instruction examples instead of images, and the crossover somewhere in the low thousands. When Week 26 says "LoRA is the right default for small instruction datasets", this is the reason, and you will have measured it.

### ViT vs CNN: the data-efficiency trade

Vision Transformers have no convolutional inductive bias — no built-in locality, no translation equivariance. They must learn those from data. Dosovitskiy et al. (2021) showed ViT underperforms ResNets on ImageNet-1k and overtakes them when pretrained on JFT-300M.

Reference figures, both ImageNet-1k-pretrained, fine-tuned on blood cells:

| Training images | ResNet-18 | ViT-B/16 | ViT advantage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 250 | 74.1% | 61.8% | **−12.3** |
| 1,000 | 86.2% | 79.2% | −7.0 |
| 4,000 | 92.0% | 89.8% | −2.2 |
| 8,742 | 93.8% | 92.4% | −1.4 |

ViT is behind at every size and closing. Extrapolating the trend, it would need somewhere north of 30,000 images to draw level here — and it costs **about 7.6× the CPU inference latency** (312 ms vs 41 ms on Machine C — reference figures; the ratio is the claim) throughout.

The practical rule: **ViT wins at scale and loses on small datasets; on the datasets most companies actually have, a ResNet or a ConvNeXt is usually the better engineering choice.** ConvNeXt (Liu et al., 2022) is the interesting middle — a CNN redesigned with Transformer-era training recipes — and often beats both on mid-size data.

### Production failure modes

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="BatchNorm statistics drifting in a 'frozen' backbone">
**Signature:** run-to-run variance far larger than seed noise (±1.4 points on 5,800 validation images), and a "frozen" model whose predictions change between runs. **Cause:** `requires_grad=False` does not stop BatchNorm's running statistics updating in `train()` mode. **Detection:** compare `bn.running_mean` across two supposedly identical runs. **Fix:** put frozen BatchNorm modules in `eval()`. **Measured:** +1.0 accuracy point and 4.7× less variance.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Optimizer given the frozen parameters">
**Signature:** memory usage is much higher than expected for a feature-extraction run; weight decay applied to frozen weights (which does nothing, since they aren't stepped — but AdamW allocates state for them). **Detection:** `sum(p.numel() for g in opt.param_groups for p in g['params'])` versus your trainable count. **Fix:** filter on `requires_grad`. **Cost:** 89 MB of pointless Adam state on ResNet-18; scale that to a 7B model and it is 54 GB.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Wrong normalization for the pretrained weights">
**Signature:** fine-tuning underperforms feature extraction, or the first epoch's loss is much higher than expected. **Cause:** using dataset statistics instead of the ImageNet statistics the backbone was trained with. **Detection:** compare first-epoch loss under both. **Fix:** match the backbone's preprocessing exactly — `torchvision`'s `weights.transforms()` returns the correct pipeline for the weights you loaded, and using it removes the whole class of error. **Measured:** 1.1 points.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Head replaced but the old head's output size still assumed downstream">
**Signature:** an index error, or silently wrong class names in the serving path. **Cause:** the class-index-to-name mapping lives in a different file from the model. **Detection:** save `class_names` inside the checkpoint. **Fix:** the checkpoint dict should contain the label mapping. This is a Week 23 Module 1 packaging rule and it prevents a genuinely nasty production bug where every prediction is confidently the wrong label.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Fine-tuning one task silently degrading another">
**Signature:** task B's metrics regress after a deployment that only touched task A. **Cause:** shared fine-tuned backbone; catastrophic forgetting. **Detection:** a regression suite that evaluates *every* task on every backbone change. **Fix:** freeze the shared backbone and use per-task heads/adapters — which is precisely the architecture Week 26 uses for multi-tenant LLM serving.
</Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

### Papers

- **Yosinski, Clune, Bengio &amp; Lipson (2014), "How transferable are features in deep neural networks?"** *Takeaway:* early-layer features are general and transfer almost losslessly, late-layer features are task-specific and transfer poorly, and there is a measurable middle where co-adaptation between layers means splitting them hurts — the empirical foundation of everything in this module.
- **Kornblith, Shlens &amp; Le (2019), "Do Better ImageNet Models Transfer Better?"** *Takeaway:* ImageNet accuracy correlates strongly with transfer accuracy for fine-tuning (r ≈ 0.96) but much less for frozen features, and the correlation breaks down entirely for datasets far from ImageNet — which is why the satellite row above looks the way it does.
- **Kumar et al. (2022), "Fine-Tuning can Distort Pretrained Features and Underperform Out-of-Distribution."** *Takeaway:* fine-tuning with a randomly-initialized head distorts the pretrained features in the first few steps; training the head first (LP-FT: linear probe then fine-tune) fixes it and improves out-of-distribution accuracy — this is the measured 1.7-point cost of skipping stage 1.
- **Howard &amp; Ruder (2018), "Universal Language Model Fine-tuning" (ULMFiT).** *Takeaway:* discriminative learning rates and gradual unfreezing, developed for vision, transfer to language models — the direct ancestor of the Week 26 recipe, and worth reading precisely because it is a language paper describing this module's method.
- **Hu et al. (2021), "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models."** *Takeaway:* constrain the weight update to a low-rank product `B·A` with `B` initialized to zero, and you can adapt a large frozen model by training 0.01–1% of its parameters with no inference latency penalty — the mapping table's right-hand column.
- **Dosovitskiy et al. (2021), "An Image is Worth 16x16 Words" (ViT).** *Takeaway:* a pure Transformer on image patches matches CNNs only when pretrained on 100M+ images; with less data the CNN's built-in locality and translation equivariance are worth more than the Transformer's flexibility.
- **Liu et al. (2022), "A ConvNet for the 2020s" (ConvNeXt).** *Takeaway:* most of ViT's advantage over ResNets came from the training recipe rather than the architecture; applying the same recipe to a modernized CNN matches ViT at equal compute — the honest control experiment the field needed.

### Stretch challenge

**Compare from-scratch, feature-extraction and full fine-tuning on the same dataset, and plot accuracy against training time and dataset size.**

Part one: reproduce the crossover table. Training-set sizes 250 / 500 / 1,000 / 2,000 / 4,000 / all, three strategies, 3 seeds each — 54 runs, but feature extraction with cached features takes seconds, so budget about 4 hours on a single GPU. Plot accuracy against `log(n)` with error bars, and mark the crossover.

Part two: measure forgetting. After each fine-tuning run, freeze the resulting backbone and train a fresh linear probe on a held-out ImageNet subset (5,000 images across 100 classes is enough and takes 2 minutes with cached features). Add a forgetting column to your table. Then vary the backbone LR over 1e-6 to 1e-3 and plot **target accuracy against retained ImageNet accuracy** — this is a Pareto frontier, and picking a point on it is a design decision most people make by accident.

Part three, the open one: **does the crossover move, and what moves it?** Candidate factors: domain gap (repeat on EuroSAT and on a chest-X-ray set), backbone size (ResNet-18 vs ResNet-50 — a bigger backbone has more parameters to overfit, so the crossover should move right), augmentation strength (more augmentation is more effective data, so it should move left), and head capacity (a 2-layer MLP head sits between the two strategies). Pick two, measure, and write down a rule someone could use to *predict* the right strategy without running the sweep.

There is no accepted formula for this. The best available heuristics are "examples per trainable parameter" and "examples per class", and both are crude. If you can produce something better on three datasets, that is a genuinely publishable-adjacent piece of work and an excellent portfolio artifact.

## Apply it at work

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="If you're a student" icon="graduation-cap">
**Portfolio line:** "Measured the feature-extraction versus fine-tuning crossover at ~700 training images on a 4-class medical imaging task, and quantified catastrophic forgetting: a full fine-tune at LR 1e-3 costs 28.5 points of the backbone's retained ImageNet linear-probe accuracy, while discriminative learning rates cut that to 14.7 points *and* improved target accuracy by 0.6."

**The interview question this earns you.** "When would you fine-tune versus freeze?" Most candidates say "it depends on data size." You say: "I measured the crossover on a 4-class medical set at around 700 examples — below that, freezing wins because 11 million trainable parameters on 250 examples memorizes. It also depends on domain gap: on EuroSAT satellite data, frozen ImageNet features are 6.4 points *worse* than training from scratch, because ImageNet features aren't a good description of multispectral tiles. And it depends on whether the backbone serves anything else, because fine-tuning forgets — I measured 28.5 points of ImageNet retention lost at LR 1e-3."

Then the move that lands: "It's the same decision as LoRA versus full fine-tuning on an LLM, for the same reasons." Very few candidates connect those, and it demonstrates that you understand the principle rather than two separate recipes.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="If you're working" icon="briefcase">
**1. Check whether any 'frozen' backbone at your company is actually frozen.** `requires_grad=False` plus `train()` mode leaves BatchNorm statistics drifting. Three lines to fix, worth about a point of accuracy and a large reduction in unexplained run-to-run variance. Look for it in any codebase with a feature-extraction path.

**2. Build the dataset-size curve for whatever model your team owns.** Train at 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of your data. The slope answers "is it worth buying more labels?" in accuracy points per thousand examples, which is a number a budget conversation can use. It costs four training runs and it consistently changes what the team does next.

**3. If one backbone serves several products, stop fine-tuning it per product.** Freeze it and use per-task heads. Otherwise every deployment for task A is an untested change to task B, and you will find out from a customer. This is the same architectural argument that makes multi-tenant LLM serving use one base with hot-swapped adapters, and it is worth making before someone ships the incident.
</Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Frozen" model gives different results run to run | BatchNorm running statistics still updating in `train()` mode | Put frozen BatchNorm modules in `eval()`; `requires_grad=False` does not do it |
| Feature-extraction run uses far more memory than expected | Optimizer given all parameters including frozen ones | Filter on `requires_grad` before constructing the optimizer |
| Fine-tune ends up worse than feature extraction | No stage 1 — a random head sent large gradients into the pretrained backbone | Train the head first with the body frozen. Measured cost of skipping: 1.7 points |
| Fine-tuning helps much less than expected | Large domain gap, or wrong normalization statistics | Run the from-scratch baseline; use `weights.transforms()` to match the backbone's preprocessing |
| Frozen features are worse than training from scratch | Domain gap too large — ImageNet features don't describe your data | Unfreeze more, or train from scratch. Measured on EuroSAT: freezing is 6.4 points worse |
| Model with the best ImageNet score performs worst on your data | Data efficiency — ViT needs far more data than a CNN | Choose the backbone by measurement on your data, not by ImageNet rank |
| A deployment for task A regressed task B | Shared fine-tuned backbone; catastrophic forgetting | Freeze the shared backbone, use per-task heads or adapters |
| Confidently wrong class names in production | The index-to-label mapping lives outside the checkpoint | Save `class_names` inside the checkpoint dict |
| `AttributeError: 'EfficientNet' object has no attribute 'fc'` | Every architecture family names its head differently | `timm.create_model(...).reset_classifier(n)`, or look up the attribute per family |
| Fine-tune diverges at a LR that worked for the head | Pretrained weights need a much smaller LR than a random head | Discriminative LRs — backbone at 1/100 of the head's |

- [Yosinski et al. (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1792) — *early features are general and transfer, late features are specific and don't; the measurement everything here rests on.*
