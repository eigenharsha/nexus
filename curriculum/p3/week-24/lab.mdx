**`LAB-P3-W24`** — one lab, three tracks. Beginners start at `basic` and climb.
Working professionals start at `standard`. `hard` is where the interview stories come from.
</Note>

## The ticket

> **From: Platform Ops**
> **To: you**
> **Subject: the Lambda has to go**
>
> The Week-23 service works and we are now at 180,000 predictions a day and climbing, which is
> past the crossover you computed. Cold starts are also showing up in the p99 and Pathology Ops
> have started complaining that the first request after a quiet period is slow.
>
> Move it to the cluster. Three things we will hold you to:
>
> 1. **Deploys must not drop requests.** Our current deploy process loses a handful every time
>    and everyone has learned to ignore it. Prove yours does not.
> 2. **It has to scale, and we need to know how fast.** Not "it autoscales" — a number, and
>    whether that number is fast enough for our traffic.
> 3. **A runbook that someone other than you can follow at 3 a.m.**, because you will not always
>    be the one holding the pager.

**Time box: 15–20 hours** across the three tracks. This is the largest lab in Phase 3, and it is
the one whose artifacts you will reuse most.

<Info>
**Everything runs on `kind`. No cloud spend at all**, except the optional managed-Grafana step.
`kind load docker-image` puts your Week-23 image straight into the cluster's containerd, so
there is no registry to set up and no pull to wait for.
</Info>

## Tracks

<Tabs>
<Tab title="basic">

**2–3 hours.** A Deployment and a Service for the Week-23 image on a `kind` cluster, reachable via `kubectl port-forward`, returning a prediction.

About 60% is written, with `TODO`s marked. You fill in:

- The Deployment's `selector` and the pod template's `labels` — and they must match.
- The container spec: image, `imagePullPolicy`, port, and the environment (`OMP_NUM_THREADS`).
- The readiness probe pointing at `/readyz`.
- The Service's selector and `targetPort`.

**What you must make true**

- `make verify TRACK=basic` is green.
- `kubectl port-forward` plus a `curl` returns a prediction.
- `kubectl get endpointslices` shows the right number of addresses.
- You can explain why `imagePullPolicy: IfNotPresent` is required with `kind load`.

</Tab>
<Tab title="standard">

**8–9 hours.** The full deployment. Spec and tests only.

**Acceptance criteria**

1. **Full manifests** — Deployment, Service, Ingress, ConfigMap, Secret, liveness/readiness/**startup** probes, and resource requests and limits **chosen from measured usage rather than guessed**.
2. **An HPA scaling on CPU**, demonstrated scaling up under load and back down, with the `behavior` block configured (not the defaults).
3. **A TensorFlow Serving deployment alongside the FastAPI one**, with the trade-off written down — throughput at three batch sizes, and a stated regime for each.
4. **Prometheus metrics exposed and scraped**, plus a Grafana dashboard with request rate, latency percentiles, error rate and pod count.
5. **A load-test report showing scale-up behaviour with the timing of each phase.**

**Design notes**

Resource requests are the whole game in Kubernetes and everyone guesses them. Measure actual usage under representative load, set requests from the p95, and **write down what you observed** — that paragraph is what a platform engineer reads first.

Watch the CPU limit. A limit with multiple inference threads produces CFS stalls that show up as p99, not as anything you would notice otherwise (Module 3). `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1` and scale with replicas.

<Info>Spec and tests only. `make verify TRACK=standard`.</Info>

</Tab>
<Tab title="hard">

**5–8 hours.** A **zero-downtime rolling update under sustained load, proven** with a request log, plus autoscaling on a custom queue-depth metric.

**The constraint the `standard` solution fails:** a default rolling update at 300 rps drops around 187 requests of 22,500 (0.83%). The `standard` track never measures it, so it never sees them.

**Acceptance criteria**

1. **Zero failed requests across a rolling update under sustained load**, with the client-side request log committed as evidence. Use a k6 threshold (`http_req_failed: ['rate<0.0001']`) so the test *fails* on any loss rather than being judged by eye. Apply the fixes one at a time and report the failure count at each stage — 187 → 41 → 3 → 0 is the expected shape, and the **error-type breakdown** at each stage is what shows you understood the mechanism.
2. **Custom-metric autoscaling on queue depth** (Prometheus Adapter or KEDA), **with the reason CPU is the wrong signal for this workload** — and a measurement showing it, not an assertion.
3. **p50/p95/p99 at three load levels, plus a cost-per-1M-predictions estimate compared against the Week-23 serverless number.**

<Warning>
**No number, no credit.** "Zero downtime achieved" is not a deliverable. "22,500 requests during
a 75-second rollout, 0 failures at a k6 threshold of 1e-4, `preStop` sized at 8 s from a measured
p95 endpoint-propagation of 3.4 s over 20 trials" is.
</Warning>

</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Milestones, in order

<Steps>
<Step title="Cluster and a Pod (25 min)">
Four nodes, image loaded, one Pod running and answering through `port-forward`. Then break it five ways (Module 1) and diagnose each from `describe` before looking at logs.
</Step>
<Step title="Deployment, Service, Ingress (45 min)">
Three replicas, matching labels, a readiness probe on `/readyz`. Confirm `kubectl get endpointslices` shows three addresses — that command is how you debug every future "the Service isn't routing" question.
</Step>
<Step title="Size requests from measurement (40 min)">
Run representative load for 10 minutes; take p95 CPU and max working-set memory. Set requests from those, memory limit at the request, and think hard before setting a CPU limit at all. **Write down what you measured** — it is a graded deliverable.
</Step>
<Step title="Baseline the rolling update (35 min)">
6 replicas, `maxSurge: 1`, `maxUnavailable: 0`, k6 at 300 rps with a constant arrival rate, `kubectl set image` twenty seconds in. Record the failure count **and the error-type breakdown**. Expect around 187, mostly ingress 503s.
</Step>
<Step title="Measure endpoint propagation (30 min)">
Twenty pod deletions, timing until every node's iptables has forgotten the pod IP (Module 2 has the script). Take the p95. Expect roughly 3.4 s on a four-node `kind` cluster. **Size `preStop` from this**, not from a default.
</Step>
<Step title="Get to zero, one fix at a time (50 min)">
Readiness probe → `preStop` → graceful shutdown. Each fix should zero one error category. Check the arithmetic: `preStop + drain_timeout < terminationGracePeriodSeconds`. Assert zero with a k6 threshold, and add a p99 threshold so you notice if you traded failures for latency.
</Step>
<Step title="HPA and the scaling report (60 min)">
CPU at 60%, a `behavior` block, `minReplicas: 3`. Step the load 10 → 200 rps thirty times and time from the step to the first request served by a new pod. Report the median and p95, then **reconcile against the six predicted components**. Expect roughly 31 s and 47 s against a predicted 27.3 s.
</Step>
<Step title="Deploy TF Serving alongside (75 min)">
Export to SavedModel and **verify parity to 1e-4 first**. ConfigMap with `models.config` and `batching.config`, readiness on `/v1/models/bloodcell`, and a warmup file. Benchmark both at client batch 1, 8 and 32 at a fixed offered load.
</Step>
<Step title="Monitoring and the dashboard (60 min)">
`kube-prometheus-stack`, a ServiceMonitor, and the six panels — including predicted-class distribution and confidence, which are the two that make it a model dashboard. Histogram buckets that bracket your actual latency.
</Step>
<Step title="SLO, budget and burn-rate alerts (45 min)">
The SLI as one good/total ratio, the SLO as a sentence with two numbers, the budget in hours, and the four burn-rate alerts. **Add a minimum-traffic condition** or they will page on noise.
</Step>
<Step title="Queue-depth autoscaling (60 min)">
An in-flight gauge, KEDA or the Prometheus Adapter, and a threshold derived from Little's Law using your own load-test knee. Re-run the step and compare against CPU: expect roughly 19 s to first scale and about half the peak p99.
</Step>
<Step title="Runbook and game day (120 min)">
Write it, including §D "the model returns garbage" and §E "input drift". Then run the game day: ten breakages, dashboards and `kubectl` only, alternating roles. **Score how many your monitoring caught at all.**
</Step>
<Step title="Tear down (5 min)">
`kind delete cluster --name nexus`. Three seconds, and the whole week costs nothing.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Ship it

<Card title="This is the part people skip" icon="rocket" horizontal>
A repo with the manifests, plus `RUNBOOK.md` and a scaling report.
</Card>

**`RUNBOOK.md`** is the deliverable the ticket actually asked for, and it is the one that most distinguishes this repo. It must contain:

1. **Owner, escalation path, and links to both dashboards.**
2. **One section per alert**, each a decision procedure — a numbered sequence of checks with a branch at each step, not prose.
3. **§D "the model returns garbage"**, with committed fixture images and the expected label and confidence for each. This section is what makes it an *ML* runbook.
4. **§E "input drift"**, containing the instruction **"do not roll back the model"** and the reason. Rolling back is the trained reflex and it is exactly wrong when the data moved.
5. **Expected recovery times.** "Expected recovery: 2–3 minutes after scaling. If not, escalate." An on-call engineer at 3 a.m. needs to know when to stop waiting.

**The scaling report** must contain:

1. One chart with offered load, replica count and p99 over time.
2. The six lag components with their defaults, the predicted total, and your measured median and p95.
3. Which component is largest for you, and what you changed to test the attribution.
4. The CPU-versus-queue-depth comparison with both time-to-first-scale numbers.
5. **One sentence on whether autoscaling is the right tool for your traffic shape** — and if it is not, what is.

<Tip>
**The most valuable page in this repo is the runbook's §D.** Every deployment portfolio has
manifests. Almost none has a written procedure for "the service is healthy and the answers are
wrong", which is the failure mode unique to ML and the one a hiring manager will recognize
immediately as coming from someone who has operated a model in production.
</Tip>

## Start here — your first 30 minutes

The requirements above are the *finished* picture. Do not try to hold all of it at once. Do these in
order; each step ends somewhere you can stop and still be making progress.

<Steps>
<Step title="Run the starter and expect RED (5 min)">
```bash
cd labs/p3/week-24
make verify TRACK=basic
```
A red result is success here — it means the harness runs and the tests can see your code. If you get
something that is *not* a test failure (a missing command, an import error), fix that first; it is an
environment problem, not a lab problem.
</Step>

<Step title="Read one test before writing any code (5 min)">
The tests are the real specification — everything written above is prose describing them. Where the
prose and a test disagree, **the test wins**. Open the first one and read it properly.
</Step>

<Step title="Make ONE test pass (15 min)">
Implement the smallest thing the first failing test needs. Run `make verify` again. One more test
passing is the whole goal of your first half hour.

That is the loop for the entire lab: one test, one function, run, repeat. Do not write five functions
and then run for the first time — when it fails you will not know which one broke it.
</Step>

<Step title="Finish the basic track">
Work the `TODO`s in the order they appear. They are in dependency order, not alphabetical.
</Step>

<Step title="Only then read the standard requirements">
With `basic` green you have something that works. `standard` extends it; it does not replace it.
</Step>

<Step title="Ship it">

</Step>
</Steps>

## Stuck? Open these in order

Each reveals a little more than the last. Try the level above before opening the next — a hint you
did not need costs you nothing, but an answer taken too early costs you the lesson.

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="Nudge — I don't know where to start" icon="lightbulb">
Find the **first** failing test and implement only what that one test needs. Ignore the rest of the
lab until it is green. If you cannot tell which test fails first, run
`make verify TRACK=basic` and read the top of the output, not the bottom.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Hint — my pod is CrashLoopBackOff" icon="lightbulb">
Kubernetes is restarting a container that keeps exiting. The event log says why and almost nobody reads it first.

**What to do instead:** `kubectl describe pod <name>` then `kubectl logs <name> --previous` — the `--previous` flag shows the run that died, which is the one you need. The five common causes are a bad image, OOMKilled, a failing probe, missing config, and insufficient resources.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Hint — my rolling update drops requests" icon="lightbulb">
The pod stops accepting traffic before it stops receiving it. Kubernetes removes an endpoint and sends SIGTERM concurrently, so in-flight requests land on a shutting-down pod.

**What to do instead:** Add a `preStop` sleep of a few seconds and handle SIGTERM by draining rather than exiting immediately. The week measures 187 dropped requests going to zero with exactly this fix.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Hint — my HPA does not scale, or scales too late" icon="lightbulb">
Scaling lag is the sum of six delays: metrics scrape interval, HPA loop period, stabilization window, scheduling, image pull, and readiness. Each is small and together they are ~30 seconds.

**What to do instead:** Set resource `requests` — the HPA computes utilisation against them and does nothing without them. Then measure the lag end to end rather than assuming it is instant.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="I have a real attempt that fails — show me the full solution" icon="lock">
Open the [reference solution](/solutions/p3/week-24/lab).

Use it properly: read only far enough to get unstuck, then close it and return to your own code.
Afterwards, diff your version against it — the differences are the lesson, and yours is not
automatically the worse one.

If you have not yet written code that fails, do not open it. Reading a solution before struggling
with the problem feels exactly like learning, and is the most reliable way to finish this course
having learned nothing.
</Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## If you get stuck

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="ErrImagePull for an image you loaded with kind load">
`imagePullPolicy` is `Always` — which is also the implicit default for a `:latest` tag. Set `IfNotPresent`, and never tag `:latest`. Confirm the image is there with `docker exec -it nexus-worker crictl images | grep bloodcell`.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Service has no endpoints but the pods are Running">
Two possible causes and `kubectl get endpointslices` distinguishes them: the Service's selector does not match the pod labels, or no pod is Ready. For the second, `kubectl describe pod` shows the readiness-probe failure.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Cannot get the rolling update below about 40 failures">
That is the endpoint-propagation race and it needs a `preStop` hook. Check the error type first: `connection refused` is the race; a 503 from the ingress means the readiness probe is missing or wrong; timeouts mean in-flight requests killed by SIGTERM, which needs graceful shutdown. Module 2's four-row table maps each.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Added preStop and still losing a few requests">
Check the arithmetic: `terminationGracePeriodSeconds` must exceed `preStop` + your server's drain timeout. With `preStop: 5` and a 20 s drain you need at least 30 s of grace; the default is 30, which is uncomfortably tight. Set 45 and write the arithmetic in a comment.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="metrics-server pods are CrashLoopBackOff on kind">
`kind` node kubelets use self-signed certificates. Patch `--kubelet-insecure-tls` into the metrics-server deployment's args. This is the single most common `kind` setup problem and it has nothing to do with your service.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="HPA shows TARGETS as <unknown>">
metrics-server is not returning data for those pods — either it is not running, or the pods have no CPU `requests` (utilization is a percentage of the request, so with no request there is nothing to be a percentage of). `kubectl top pods` distinguishes them.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="p99 is far worse in the cluster than it was on the VM">
CPU throttling. `kubectl exec deploy/bloodcell -- cat /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat` and look at `nr_throttled`. With multiple inference threads under a CPU limit you get periodic stalls of up to 87.5 ms. `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1`, and reconsider the CPU limit entirely (Module 3).
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Prometheus is not scraping your service">
The `kube-prometheus-stack` chart's Prometheus selects ServiceMonitors by a label — usually `release: monitoring`. Without it your ServiceMonitor applies cleanly and is ignored. Check Prometheus's Targets page rather than guessing.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Burn-rate alerts fire overnight when traffic is near zero">
A ratio with a tiny denominator. Three bad requests out of ten is 30%. Add `and sum(rate(bloodcell_requests_total[5m])) > 1` to every ratio-based alert.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="TF Serving says 'No versions of servable found under base path'">
Version directories must be integers. `v4` and `2026-08-30` are both ignored. Rename it `4`, or use a Unix timestamp, which sorts correctly.
</Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
