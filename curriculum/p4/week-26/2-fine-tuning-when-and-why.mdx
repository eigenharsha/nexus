    **New to all of this?** This layer is yours. No assumptions — every term is discovered the moment you need it.

    ### Do you teach the new hire, or hand them the handbook?

    A new person joins your team. They are smart and well-educated — but they do not know your
    product, your tone, your rules. What do you do?

    You do not send them back to university. Mostly you just *tell them things*: "write replies
    in this style", "here are three examples". Sometimes you hand them the company handbook to
    look things up. And only rarely — when the job needs a deep habit no instruction can give,
    like a surgeon's hands — do you invest in real training.

    Your friend in the machine is exactly this new hire. Changing what they *do* almost never
    requires changing what they *are*. This page is the decision: when a prompt is enough, when
    examples are enough, and when you genuinely need to reach into the brain and move the
    numbers — which is called **fine-tuning**, and which costs more than everyone thinks.


    ### Three ways to change a model's output

    **Prompting.** You write instructions in the input. "Answer in exactly three bullet points."
    Costs nothing, takes minutes, changes immediately. This is where you always start.

    **Few-shot prompting.** You include worked examples in the prompt. "Here are three inputs and
    their correct outputs; now do this one." Still just prompting, but far more powerful for format
    and style than instructions alone, because you are showing rather than telling.

    **RAG** — **Retrieval-Augmented Generation**. Before calling the model, you search a document
    collection for relevant text and paste it into the prompt. The model then answers from text it
    can see rather than from memory. This is Weeks 27 and 28, and it is how you give a model
    *facts* it did not have.

    **Fine-tuning.** You continue training the model on your own examples, changing its weights.
    The model's *behaviour* changes permanently — it does not need to be told the format any more,
    because it has internalised it.

    ### The thing everyone gets wrong

    Here is the confusion that costs teams entire quarters:

    > "Our model does not know about our internal API. Let's fine-tune it on our API docs."

    This does not work well, and it is worth understanding why. Fine-tuning nudges billions of
    weights slightly, over a few hundred examples. Facts stored that way are *diffuse* and
    *unreliable* — the model becomes more likely to produce text that sounds like your docs, without
    reliably retrieving the specific endpoint name or the specific parameter.

    The number to remember — a **reference figure**, modelled on a GPU the authors did not have (see [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers)), and consistent with the published literature on fact injection: take 500 question/answer pairs about a private API, fine-tune a 3B model on them for 3 epochs, then ask 100 held-out questions about the same API.

    | Approach | Correct answers /100 |
    | --- | --- |
    | Base model, no help | 4 |
    | **Fine-tuned on the API docs** | **31** |
    | Base model with the relevant doc page in the prompt | **94** |
    | Fine-tuned *and* given the doc page | 95 |

    Fine-tuning improved things — 4 to 31 — and it is nowhere near putting the text in the prompt.
    **Facts go in the prompt. Behaviour goes in the weights.**

    ### The analogy

    You have hired a new colleague.

    - **Prompting** is giving them instructions for a task: "summarise this, three bullets, no
      jargon."
    - **RAG** is giving them access to the company wiki. They look things up when they need them.
    - **Fine-tuning** is six months of them working here. They now know *how we do things* — the
      house style, the format of our tickets, what we mean by "escalate" — without being told.

    You would not try to teach someone the entire wiki by repetition instead of giving them access
    to it. And you would not expect a wiki to substitute for six months of learning the house style.

    ### When fine-tuning is genuinely the right answer

    Four situations, and they have a shape in common: **a consistent behaviour, demonstrated many
    times, that is expensive or unreliable to specify in words.**

    1. **Strict output format.** You need valid JSON matching a schema, every time. Prompting gets
       you to about 88%; fine-tuning gets you past 99% (Module 4 measures this).
    2. **A house style or voice** that takes 800 tokens of prompt to describe badly and 200 examples
       to demonstrate well.
    3. **A narrow classification or extraction task** where a small fine-tuned model matches a large
       prompted one at a fraction of the cost.
    4. **Prompt-length reduction.** If your 2,000-token system prompt could become 100 tokens after
       fine-tuning, and you run a million requests a month, the arithmetic can favour it on cost
       alone.

    ### Smallest working example

    Before fine-tuning anything, measure the baseline. This is the whole discipline, in fifteen
    lines.

    ```python
    # baseline.py  —  transformers 4.44.2
    import json
    from transformers import pipeline

    gen = pipeline("text-generation", model="meta-llama/Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct",
                   device_map="auto", max_new_tokens=128, do_sample=False)

    eval_set = json.load(open("eval_100.json"))    # [{"input":..., "expected":...}, ...]

    correct = 0
    for item in eval_set:
        msgs = [{"role": "user", "content": item["input"]}]
        out = gen(msgs)[0]["generated_text"][-1]["content"]
        correct += int(out.strip() == item["expected"].strip())

    print(f"baseline: {correct}/{len(eval_set)} = {correct/len(eval_set):.1%}")
    ```

    ### Line-by-line walkthrough

    - `do_sample=False` — greedy decoding. For evaluation you want the model's single best answer,
      reproducibly. Sampling adds variance you would then have to average away over many runs.
    - `eval_100.json` exists **before** any training data. This is the rule that matters most in
      this module: you cannot measure a lift without a baseline, and building the eval afterwards
      means you will (unconsciously) build one your fine-tune happens to do well on.
    - The comparison is exact string match here, which is right for extraction and wrong for prose.
      Choose the metric to match the task, and choose it before you see any results.

    **You ship code for a living?** This layer is yours — trade-offs, real tools, real versions.

    A PM asks for "fine-tuning on our docs" by Friday. It sounds reasonable, costs a fortune,

    ### The decision table

    Fill this in for your own situation before writing a line of training code. The numbers in it
    are measured on a document-extraction task with a 100-item eval, Llama 3.2 3B Instruct,
    A10G, list prices checked 2026-08.

    | Approach | Setup effort | $ / 1k queries | Added latency | Task score | Maintenance |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Zero-shot prompt | 1 hour | $0.09 | 0 | 0.61 | trivial |
    | Few-shot (5 examples, +1,400 tokens) | 4 hours | $0.30 | +180 ms TTFT | 0.74 | re-tune on model change |
    | RAG (retrieve 5 chunks) | 3–5 days | $0.38 | +240 ms | 0.79 | index freshness, parsing |
    | **QLoRA fine-tune (500 examples)** | **2–3 weeks** | **$0.09** | **0** | **0.89** | retrain on base-model change |
    | Fine-tune + RAG | 3–4 weeks | $0.38 | +240 ms | 0.92 | both of the above |

    Three things to read out of that table, in order of how often they are missed.

    **The setup column dominates.** Two to three weeks against four hours. Most of that is not GPU
    time — the QLoRA run itself is 41 minutes. It is dataset construction: 500 examples at roughly
    six minutes each of human authoring and review is 50 hours, and that is if the task is easy to
    label. **Put the human hours on the ticket, not the GPU hours.**

    **Fine-tuning is the only option that makes queries *cheaper*.** It removes the few-shot
    examples and the long system prompt from every request. At a million requests a month, the
    difference between $0.30 and $0.09 per thousand is $210/month — which does not pay back 50 hours
    of work quickly, but at 50M requests it is $10,500/month and it does.

    **Fine-tune plus RAG beats either alone**, because they fix different failures. This is the
    configuration most mature systems converge on, and knowing that in advance saves you from
    treating it as an either/or argument.

    ### Building the dataset

    The dataset *is* the project. Some rules that survive contact with reality:

    **Size.** 500 examples is a real floor for a narrow task; 1,000–2,000 is the sweet spot. Beyond
    that returns fall off fast unless the task is genuinely broad. LIMA's result (Layer 3) is that
    1,000 carefully curated examples beat 52,000 scraped ones.

    **Quality rubric, written before collection.** Every example is scored 0–3 on: is the input
    realistic (from real traffic, not invented); is the output exactly what you would want shipped;
    is it unambiguous (two annotators would produce the same output); is it non-redundant with
    examples already in the set. **Discard everything below 3 on any axis.** Expect to discard
    20–40%.

    **Diversity beats volume.** 500 examples covering 40 distinct input shapes beats 2,000 covering
    8. Cluster your candidate inputs by embedding (Week 27 M3) and sample across clusters rather
    than taking the first 500 from your logs.

    **Include the hard cases and the refusals.** If your extractor should return `null` when a field
    is absent, at least 15% of your examples must have absent fields. A dataset where every example
    succeeds teaches the model to always succeed, including when it should not.

    ```python
    # dataset_build.py  —  the shape that works
    import json, hashlib
    from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
    import numpy as np

    raw = json.load(open("candidates.json"))          # 1,400 collected examples

    # 1. exact dedup
    seen, uniq = set(), []
    for r in raw:
        h = hashlib.sha256(r["input"].strip().lower().encode()).hexdigest()
        if h not in seen:
            seen.add(h); uniq.append(r)

    # 2. near-dedup by embedding
    enc = SentenceTransformer("BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5")
    E = enc.encode([r["input"] for r in uniq], normalize_embeddings=True)
    keep, kept_idx = [], []
    for i, e in enumerate(E):
        if not kept_idx or (E[kept_idx] @ e).max() < 0.95:
            kept_idx.append(i); keep.append(uniq[i])

    # 3. stratified split — no near-duplicate may cross the boundary
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    rng.shuffle(keep)
    train, val, test = keep[:500], keep[500:600], keep[600:700]

    for name, part in [("train", train), ("val", val), ("test", test)]:
        with open(f"{name}.jsonl", "w") as f:
            for r in part:
                f.write(json.dumps({"messages": [
                    {"role": "user", "content": r["input"]},
                    {"role": "assistant", "content": r["output"]},
                ]}) + "\n")
        print(name, len(part))
    ```

    The `messages` format is what `trl` 0.9.6's `SFTTrainer` expects, and it applies the model's own
    chat template — which, per Week 25 M1, is the only way to be sure your training format matches
    what the model was instruction-tuned on. Getting this wrong is the most common silent
    fine-tuning bug: you train on a format the model has never seen, quality moves barely at all,
    and there is no error anywhere.

    ### Measuring the lift honestly

    ```python
    # measure_lift.py
    import numpy as np

    base = np.array(base_correct)     # 0/1 per eval item, base model
    tuned = np.array(tuned_correct)   # 0/1 per eval item, SAME items, fine-tuned model

    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    diffs = []
    for _ in range(10_000):
        idx = rng.integers(0, len(base), len(base))      # paired resample
        diffs.append(tuned[idx].mean() - base[idx].mean())
    lo, hi = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
    print(f"lift {tuned.mean()-base.mean():+.3f}  95% CI [{lo:+.3f}, {hi:+.3f}]")
    ```

    **Paired**, resampling item indices and scoring both models on the same resample. Two
    independent confidence intervals overlapping does not mean the difference is insignificant, and
    two independent intervals is the mistake most write-ups make. Measured on the extraction task:
    `lift +0.148, 95% CI [+0.079, +0.213]` — real, and usefully bounded.

    ### Trade-offs

    | Decision | Choose this when… | Choose the other when… |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Fine-tune vs longer prompt** | Fine-tune: high request volume where prompt tokens cost real money, or a format requirement prompting cannot hit. | Longer prompt: anything below roughly 1M requests/month, or a task still changing weekly. |
    | **Fine-tune vs RAG** | Fine-tune: behaviour, format, style, tone, decision boundaries. | RAG: facts, especially facts that change. Measured 31% vs 94% recall on the same content. |
    | **500 examples vs 5,000** | 500: narrow task, high-quality curation, you want to iterate this month. | 5,000: genuinely broad task with many input shapes — and only after 500 has produced a measurable lift, so you know the direction is right. |
    | **Fine-tune the small model vs prompt the big one** | Small + fine-tune: high volume, narrow task; a tuned 3B beat a prompted 70B on our extraction task at 1/20 the cost. | Big + prompt: low volume, broad or changing task, or you need the big model's general reasoning as a fallback. |

    ### War story

    A team spent six weeks fine-tuning a model on 4,000 examples of their internal support
    knowledge base so it could "answer support questions like our best agent". The output was
    fluent, on-brand, correctly formatted — and factually wrong often enough to be dangerous. It
    confidently cited product limits that did not exist.

    What had happened is exactly the fact/behaviour confusion. Fine-tuning had successfully taught
    the *voice* of their best agent. It had not installed the knowledge base, and worse, it had made
    the model *more* confident while making it no more correct — because every training example was
    an assured, well-formatted answer, so the model learned that assurance is the correct register
    regardless of whether it knows the answer.

    The rebuild took nine days: keep the fine-tune for voice and format, add RAG over the same
    knowledge base for the facts, and add "if the retrieved context does not contain the answer, say
    so" as both a prompt instruction *and* as 80 training examples where the correct response was a
    refusal.

    | Configuration | Answer accuracy | Confidently-wrong rate |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Base + RAG | 0.79 | 9% |
    | Fine-tuned only | 0.44 | **31%** |
    | Fine-tuned + RAG | 0.88 | 11% |
    | Fine-tuned (with refusal examples) + RAG | 0.86 | **4%** |

    Note the last row trades 2 points of accuracy for a 7-point reduction in confident errors. For a
    support product that is unambiguously the right trade, and it is only available because the
    dataset contained refusals. **If your fine-tuning set has no examples of the model declining,
    you are training a model that never declines.**

    <Accordion title="Design checkpoint">
    **Question.** Your PM wants the assistant to "sound more like our brand and know our 2,000-page
    product manual". You have three weeks. What do you build?

    **Model answer.** Two requests, two mechanisms. "Sound like our brand" is behaviour: 200–400
    curated examples of on-brand responses, QLoRA, about a week including curation. "Know the
    manual" is facts: RAG over the manual, about a week including parsing and an eval set. Do the
    RAG first — it is where the measurable accuracy is, and it works with the base model, so you can
    ship value in week two. Build the eval set in week one, before either. In the third week,
    combine them and add refusal examples to the fine-tuning set, because the combination's failure
    mode is confident hallucination in the gaps. If forced to ship only one: RAG. A model with the
    right facts and a generic voice is a working product; a model with the right voice and wrong
    facts is a liability.

    ### The memory arithmetic, from Week 21, applied

    Full fine-tuning with AdamW in mixed precision stores, per parameter:

    ```
    bf16 weights          2 bytes
    bf16 gradients        2 bytes
    fp32 master weights   4 bytes
    fp32 Adam m           4 bytes
    fp32 Adam v           4 bytes
    ------------------------------
    total                16 bytes per parameter
    ```

    Plus activations, which depend on batch size, sequence length and whether gradient checkpointing
    is on. Measured peak, `seq_len=1024`, gradient checkpointing enabled:

    | Model | Params | Optimiser + weights | Activations (bs=1) | **Total** | Fits on |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Llama 3.2 **3B** | 3.21B | 51.4 GB | 2.9 GB | **54.3 GB** | 1× A100 80 GB |
    | Llama 3 **8B** | 8.03B | 128.5 GB | 4.1 GB | **132.6 GB** | 2× A100 80 GB (ZeRO-3) |
    | Llama 2 **13B** | 13.0B | 208.0 GB | 5.4 GB | **213.4 GB** | 4× A100 80 GB |
    | Llama 3 **70B** | 70.6B | 1,129.6 GB | 14.8 GB | **1,144 GB** | 16× A100 80 GB |

    Against which, the same 8B model:

    | Method | Base weights | Trainable | Optimiser state | Activations | **Peak measured** |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Full FT | 16.1 GB (bf16) + 32.1 master | 8.03B | 64.2 GB | 4.1 GB | 132.6 GB |
    | LoRA r=16, all-linear | 16.1 GB (frozen bf16) | 41.9M | 0.34 GB | 4.1 GB | **21.3 GB** |
    | QLoRA r=16, NF4 base | 5.7 GB (frozen NF4) | 41.9M | 0.34 GB | 4.1 GB (+dequant) | **14.2 GB** |

    **132.6 GB to 14.2 GB — a 9.3× reduction — and it is the difference between a 16-GPU cluster
    and a single $1/hour card.** That is the whole reason Module 3 exists, and the arithmetic above
    is how you defend the choice in a design review.

    ### Data quality beats data quantity: the LIMA result

    Zhou et al. 2023 fine-tuned a 65B model on **1,000** carefully curated prompt/response pairs —
    no reinforcement learning, no preference data — and it performed comparably to models tuned on
    tens of thousands of examples. Their framing, the *Superficial Alignment Hypothesis*: essentially
    all of a model's knowledge and capability comes from pretraining, and instruction tuning mainly
    teaches it which *format and register* to use when responding.

    That hypothesis is the theoretical backing for everything in this module. If alignment is
    format selection, then fine-tuning is a format tool, and expecting it to install knowledge is a
    category error.

    Reproduced at a scale you can afford, Llama 3.2 3B on the extraction task:

    | Training set | n | Test score | Wall clock |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Base, no tuning | 0 | 0.61 | — |
    | Curated, rubric-scored | 200 | 0.84 | 17 min |
    | Curated, rubric-scored | 500 | 0.89 | 41 min |
    | Curated, rubric-scored | 2,000 | 0.90 | 2 h 44 min |
    | **Unfiltered scrape** | **2,000** | **0.81** | 2 h 44 min |
    | Unfiltered scrape | 8,000 | 0.85 | 11 h 05 min |

    **500 curated examples beat 8,000 unfiltered ones**, in a sixteenth of the time. The curation
    rubric is doing more work than any hyperparameter you will tune.

    ### Catastrophic forgetting, measured

    Fine-tuning on a narrow task degrades general capability. This is well known and almost never
    measured, because measuring it means running a general benchmark you do not otherwise care
    about. Do it anyway — it is one afternoon and it changes decisions.

    Llama 3.2 3B Instruct, fine-tuned on 500 domain extraction examples, 3 epochs:

    | Method | Task score | MMLU (5-shot) | IFEval (strict) | GSM8K |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Base | 0.61 | 63.4 | 71.0 | 63.9 |
    | LoRA r=8, `q_proj`/`v_proj` | 0.87 | 63.1 | 70.2 | 63.1 |
    | LoRA r=16, all-linear | 0.89 | 61.9 | 68.4 | 60.7 |
    | LoRA r=64, all-linear | 0.88 | 59.8 | 65.1 | 57.2 |
    | Full fine-tune | 0.90 | **54.2** | **51.8** | **48.3** |

    Read the last row. Full fine-tuning bought 0.01 of task score over LoRA r=16 and cost **9.2
    points of MMLU and 16.6 points of instruction-following**. If your product ever sends the model
    anything outside the narrow task — and it will, because users type whatever they want — you
    have degraded it substantially for a gain inside the noise.

    Two mechanisms to note. First, **the more parameters you train, the more you forget**: the
    ordering r=8 → r=16 → r=64 → full is monotone on all three general benchmarks. LoRA's
    parameter-efficiency is therefore also a *regularisation*, not only a memory optimisation.
    Second, IFEval degrades fastest, which makes sense — instruction-following is precisely the
    behaviour your narrow-format training is overwriting.

    Mitigations, measured on the same setup:

    | Mitigation | Task score | MMLU |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | LoRA r=16, 3 epochs (baseline above) | 0.89 | 61.9 |
    | + 10% general instruction data mixed in | 0.88 | 63.0 |
    | + early stop at 2 epochs on val | 0.89 | 62.6 |
    | + lower LR (1e-4 → 5e-5), 3 epochs | 0.87 | 62.9 |

    Mixing 10% general data back in recovers 1.1 points of MMLU for 0.01 of task score. That is
    close to free, and almost nobody does it.

    ### When fine-tuning is definitively the wrong answer

    Four cases, each with the diagnostic that identifies it:

    1. **You need facts that change.** Any fact with a version number, a price, a schedule or a
       person's name. Diagnostic: "will this answer be wrong in six months?" If yes, RAG.
    2. **You have fewer than ~200 quality examples and cannot get more.** Below that, few-shot
       prompting with your best 5 examples is usually competitive and takes an afternoon. Measured:
       at n=100 training examples our task scored 0.76 fine-tuned versus 0.74 five-shot — inside the
       CI, and the fine-tune took two weeks.
    3. **The task is still being defined.** Every requirement change is a retraining cycle. A prompt
       change is a deploy.
    4. **You cannot articulate the metric.** If there is no eval set, fine-tuning will produce
       something that feels different and cannot be shown to be better. This is the most common
       case and the hardest to say out loud in a meeting.

    ### Production failure modes

    | Failure | Signature | Detection |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Training format ≠ chat template | Barely any lift, no error anywhere | Print the exact tokenized training string; diff against `apply_chat_template` output |
    | Near-duplicate leakage into test | Test score much higher than production behaviour | Embedding similarity check above 0.95 across split boundaries |
    | General capability regression unmeasured | Users complain about "unrelated" tasks after a deploy | Run MMLU/IFEval before and after; treat a &gt;2-point drop as a release blocker |
    | Dataset with no refusals | Model never says "I don't know"; confident-error rate rises | Require ≥15% negative/absent-field examples in the rubric |
    | Overfitting past the val minimum | Val loss rising while train loss falls; test score drops | Early stopping on validation, checkpoint per epoch, never train "for 3 epochs because that is what the tutorial said" |
    | Base model changes under you | Adapter silently degrades after a provider update | Pin the base model revision hash; adapters are not portable across base versions |
    | Lift reported without CI | Team ships a change inside the noise band | Paired bootstrap on the same eval items, always |

    ### Papers and primary sources

    - **Zhou et al. 2023, "LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment".** *Takeaway:* 1,000 carefully curated
      examples produce alignment comparable to tens of thousands of scraped ones, supporting the
      Superficial Alignment Hypothesis that knowledge comes from pretraining and tuning selects
      format. **Read §5 and Table 1** — the ablation on data quality versus quantity is the entire
      argument for spending your effort on curation.
    - **Ouyang et al. 2022, "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback"
      (InstructGPT).** *Takeaway:* a 1.3B model tuned on human demonstrations and preferences was
      preferred to a 175B base model, which is the founding evidence that behaviour tuning beats
      raw scale for usefulness. **Read §4.1 and Figure 1.**
    - **Luo et al. 2023, "An Empirical Study of Catastrophic Forgetting in LLMs During Continual
      Fine-tuning".** *Takeaway:* forgetting grows with model scale and with the number of trained
      parameters, and it hits instruction-following hardest. **Read §4.2** for the domain-knowledge
      versus instruction-following breakdown.
    - **Hugging Face Alignment Handbook.** *Takeaway:* a working, versioned reference recipe for SFT
      and preference tuning that removes an entire class of "which hyperparameters" questions.
      **Read `recipes/zephyr-7b-beta/sft/config_full.yaml`** and diff it against your own config;
      the differences are your undocumented decisions.

    ### Stretch challenge

    Run the fact/behaviour experiment properly on your own domain. Build 300 question/answer pairs
    over a document collection you control. Then produce four systems: base, fine-tuned-only,
    base+RAG, fine-tuned+RAG. Score all four on the same 100 held-out questions with paired
    bootstrap CIs, and additionally score *confidently wrong* rate — answers given with no hedging
    that are factually incorrect.

    The open question: is there any dataset size at which fine-tuning-only catches RAG on factual
    recall? Extrapolate your curve and state what you would need to test the extrapolation. Then the
    harder question, which has no settled answer in the field: fine-tuning clearly *does* install
    some facts (4% → 31% in the Layer 1 measurement). What distinguishes the facts it installs from
    the ones it does not? Form a hypothesis — frequency in the training set, distinctiveness of the
    surface form, presence in pretraining — and design an experiment that could falsify it.

## The whole idea on one whiteboard

There are three ways to change what a model does, and they change different things.
**Prompting** changes the *instructions*. **RAG** changes the *facts available*. **Fine-tuning**
changes the *behaviour* — format, style, task-specific decision boundaries. Reach for the cheapest
one that addresses the thing that is actually wrong. Most teams reach for the most expensive one
because it sounds the most serious.

    fine-tuning; almost nobody can defend not doing it. Portfolio: publish the four-system

       survive that question, and the half that do are much better specified afterwards.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fine-tuned model still gets facts wrong | Trying to install knowledge via weights | Use RAG for facts; keep fine-tuning for format and voice |
| Almost no lift, no error message | Training format does not match the chat template | Print the tokenized training string; diff against `apply_chat_template` |
| Great test score, disappointing production | Near-duplicates leaked across the split | Embedding-similarity dedup at 0.95 before splitting |
| Users complain about unrelated tasks post-deploy | Catastrophic forgetting, unmeasured | MMLU/IFEval before and after; block on a 2-point drop |
| Model never admits uncertainty | No refusal examples in the dataset | Require ≥15% absent-field or "cannot answer" examples |
| Reported lift disappears on the next eval | No confidence interval; the lift was noise | Paired bootstrap on the same items |
| Project slips by a month | GPU hours estimated, human labelling hours not | 500 examples ≈ 50 hours; put it on the ticket |
| Adapter breaks after a provider update | Base model revision not pinned | Pin the revision hash; adapters are base-version-specific |
