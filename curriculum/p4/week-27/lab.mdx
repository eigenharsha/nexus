## The ticket

> **NEX-4402 · Knowledge platform · P1**
>
> Support handles about 400 tickets a week and answers most of them from a 1,000-document library —
> product manuals, contracts, internal policies, some of it scanned. Agents spend a documented
> 11 minutes per ticket finding the right document.
>
> We tried an off-the-shelf tool last quarter. It demoed well and agents stopped using it within
> three weeks: it confidently returned the reset procedure for the wrong product, and it never
> found anything in the scanned contracts at all. Nobody could tell us why, because nobody could
> tell us how well it was supposed to work.
>
> Build the retrieval layer, and — this is the part we are actually buying — **tell us how good it
> is, per document type, with the failure modes named.** If the honest answer is that it works for
> manuals and not for contracts, we would rather know that now.

**Time box, honestly:** `basic` 4–5 hours. `standard` 16–20 hours, of which the eval set is 6–8.
`hard` a further 10–12 hours. The eval set is the long pole and every attempt to shorten it makes
the rest of the lab meaningless.

**Hardware:** Postgres and pgvector in Docker on any laptop. Embedding 47,300 chunks takes 33 s on
a GPU or ~22 min on an M2 Pro CPU. The `hard` track's 10M-vector index wants 32 GB of RAM for about
3 hours (~$1.20 on an `m6i.2xlarge`); a 1M-vector fallback is documented and scores full marks with
the limitation stated.

    <Steps>
      <Step title="Build the eval set — do this first">

        Column-aware ordering, OCR routing for scanned documents, tables rendered as markdown,
        header/footer stripping, dehyphenation and ligature normalisation. Per-document error

      </Step>
      <Step title="Choose a chunking strategy by measurement">
        Sweep at least 128 / 256 / 512 / 1024 / 2048 with the split-rate column. Compare fixed,
        recursive and structural. Add section-path prefixes and measure the delta separately — it
        should be one of your largest.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Choose an embedding model by measurement">
        At least four candidates, evaluated on your own eval set, **segmented by query type**. Apply
        the correct query instruction prefix and show the measurement with and without it. Report
        Recall@10, NDCG@10, encode throughput, index size and cost.
      </Step>
      <Step title="Index with pgvector HNSW and tune it">
        Build with a sensible `m` and `ef_construction`; measure `index_recall@10` against exact
        search on the same data at several `ef_search` values; pick an operating point. Report build
        time and index size.

  <Tab title="hard">
    **A constraint the `standard` solution fails.**

    Scale to **10M chunks** (synthetically extended by ingesting the corpus with several chunking
    configurations and perturbations, documented) and prove filtered search is correct.

    1. **Report at scale:** index build time, index size, peak memory during build, p50/p95 query
       latency and `index_recall@10` at 10M vectors. The `standard` configuration will not fit in
       32 GB at 768 dimensions — show the failure (or the p95 cliff when the index exceeds
       `shared_buffers`) before showing your fix.
    2. **Fix it,** by one of: dimension reduction via Matryoshka truncation, `halfvec` scalar
       quantization, or partitioning with partial indexes. Measure the quality cost of whichever you
       choose against the 1M baseline.
    3. **Filtered search, proven.** Benchmark five filter selectivities (50%, 10%, 3%, 1%, 0.1%)
       crossed with four strategies (post-filter at default `ef`, post-filter at high `ef`, partial
       index, exact scan over the filtered subset). Twenty cells, filtered recall and p95 in each.
       Identify the crossover where exact scan wins and state the rule you would put in production.
    4. **The constraint:** at 10M chunks you must hold `index_recall@10 ≥ 0.95` and `p95 ≤ 10 ms`
       simultaneously, **and** filtered recall at 1% selectivity must be ≥ 0.95. The `standard`
       solution fails the third of these by a wide margin — include its measured failure in your
       report.

    The filtered-recall requirement is the real gate. It is easy to hit the latency and recall
    numbers unfiltered and to never notice that a `WHERE` clause has quietly destroyed your recall.
    If your report does not contain a filtered-recall number, you have not attempted this track.
    </Warning>

## Stuck? Open these in order

Each reveals a little more than the last. Try the level above before opening the next — a hint you
did not need costs you nothing, but an answer taken too early costs you the lesson.

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="Nudge — I don't know where to start" icon="lightbulb">
Find the **first** failing test and implement only what that one test needs. Ignore the rest of the
lab until it is green. If you cannot tell which test fails first, run
`make verify TRACK=basic` and read the top of the output, not the bottom.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Hint — my retrieval returns irrelevant chunks" icon="lightbulb">
Check parsing before embeddings. In practice most RAG failures are upstream: a PDF parsed into interleaved column text produces chunks that are individually meaningless, and no embedding model recovers from that.

**What to do instead:** Print twenty random chunks and read them. If a human cannot tell what a chunk is about, the retriever cannot either. Fix the parse, then re-evaluate.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Hint — my HNSW index recall is poor" icon="lightbulb">
`ef_search` too low. It controls how many candidates the search keeps, and the default is tuned for speed rather than recall.

**What to do instead:** Measure recall against exact brute-force search on a sample — you cannot tune what you have not measured. Then sweep `ef_search` and plot recall against latency, and pick a point deliberately.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Hint — indexing 1,000 PDFs takes hours" icon="lightbulb">
Embedding one chunk at a time. The model is far more efficient on batches, and the per-call overhead dominates otherwise.

**What to do instead:** Batch to 32-64 chunks per call, and cache embeddings to disk keyed by a content hash so a re-run does not recompute what has not changed.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="I have a real attempt that fails — show me the full solution" icon="lock">

Use it properly: read only far enough to get unstuck, then close it and return to your own code.
Afterwards, diff your version against it — the differences are the lesson, and yours is not
automatically the worse one.

If you have not yet written code that fails, do not open it. Reading a solution before struggling

## Rubric

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Correctness** | tests fail | `basic` passes | `standard` passes: 1,000 docs ingested, eval set built and reviewed, ablation complete | `hard` passes: 10M chunks within recall and latency budget, and filtered recall ≥ 0.95 proven |
| **Code quality** | works | readable | idempotent ingest, per-document error isolation, deterministic chunk IDs, pinned versions | a reviewer would approve unchanged; the eval harness refuses to run against the wrong corpus |
| **Performance** | untested | timed once | build time, index size and p95 measured; `index_recall` measured against exact search | operating point chosen against a stated binding constraint, with the other two axes justified |
| **Explanation** | none | a README | ablation table with deltas | every delta has a paired CI; non-significant rows labelled; failure modes named per document type |

**What a 3/3 on Explanation looks like:** your table contains rows you kept for reasons other than
the primary metric, and it says so. "Parent-child retrieval: ΔRecall@10 +0.00, CI [−0.03, +0.03] —
kept for the +0.03 NDCG gain, which reranking will build on" is a sentence that demonstrates you
understand your own measurement. It is also, in my experience, the rarest sentence in this genre.

## If you get stuck

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Recall is around 0.6 and I cannot find why">
    Run the parse scorecard before anything else. The likely causes in order: scanned documents
    producing empty text and being silently counted as ingested; two-column pages read across the
    columns; the embedding model's query instruction prefix missing. All three are cheap to check
    and one of them is usually most of the gap.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Queries suddenly take 60 ms after building the index">
    The index is not being used. Run `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE)` — you will see `Seq Scan`. The usual causes
    are an operator-class mismatch (`vector_cosine_ops` with `<->`) or a query shape the planner
    cannot push down, such as a wrapping subquery or an extra `ORDER BY` term.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="The HNSW build has been running for two hours">
    `maintenance_work_mem`. At the 64 MB default the build spills to disk and takes about six times
    longer with no warning. `SET maintenance_work_mem = '8GB'` before `CREATE INDEX`.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="My eval Recall@10 is 0.95 and I do not believe it">
    You should not. Read twenty of your eval questions out loud. If they contain section numbers,
    document titles or phrases copied from the passage, you are measuring string overlap. Regenerate
    with the anti-quoting prompt and compare the mean query length against real user queries.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Filtered queries return fewer than k rows">
    That is the post-filter trap and it is the intended discovery. Your options are a partial index
    per hot filter value, an exact scan when the filter is selective enough, or pgvector 0.8's
    iterative scan. Measure all three; the exact scan often wins and is exact.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
