## The story this week tells

You have spent four weeks learning what the machine is and how to give it a library. This week is
about the thing you will actually do every working day: **talking to it.**

It starts with a new colleague who has read everything ever written and knows nothing about your
company, remembers nothing between conversations, and will never say *"I don't understand what you
want."* Everything they do is decided by the note you hand them.

By Friday that note has grown into a discipline. Because a real request is not one instruction —
it is a system prompt, a few examples, a list of tools, five retrieved passages, forty turns of
history and a question, all competing for one finite window on every single call. Deciding what
earns its place there is what the industry now calls **context engineering**, and it is what
"prompt engineer" job titles turned into.

<Card title="This week — saying what you mean, and choosing what it sees" icon="sparkles">
  - **Write the note properly** — audience, length, format, and what to leave out
  - **Teach with examples what no instruction can express** — including the trap case
  - **Send a screenshot when the meaning is in the pixels** — and know when not to
  - **Budget a finite window like money** — and order it for both attention and cost
  - **Put the whole thing under version control** — because it is production logic in English
  - **Diagnose it when it stops obeying** — seven shapes, seven fixes
</Card>

## The through-line

Six modules, one narrative, each answering the question the last one raised:

1. **Talking to the model.** What a prompt actually is, what is in the envelope, and why zero-shot
   comes first.
2. **The techniques that work.** Few-shot, chain of thought, delimiters, contracts, roles,
   chaining — and the table saying which to reach for.
3. **When the input is not text.** Images, scans and audio, and the cost of seeing.
4. **Context engineering.** The desk with one drawer: budgets, dilution, position, and the order
   that serves attention and cost at once.
5. **Prompts are code.** Versioned, templated, tested, revertible.
6. **When it ignores you.** The failure taxonomy, the diagnostic order, and the model-upgrade
   checklist.

| Number | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |

| **20** | Cases in an eval set — enough to catch a regression, small enough to run often |
| **0** | Regressions allowed on a customer-facing prompt change, however many wins it brings |
