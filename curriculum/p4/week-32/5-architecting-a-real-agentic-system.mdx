### The whiteboard at the end of the week

Picture the design review. Your diagram is on the whiteboard, and around the table sit the
people who will carry the pager: the engineer who gets woken, the finance lead who sees the
bill, and the lawyer who asks what happens when it is wrong.

They will not ask which framework you used. They will ask:

*Where does a human approve? What is the worst thing this can do before someone notices? What
happens when the model is confidently wrong? What does it cost on a bad day? Who fixes it at
3 a.m., and with what?*

Every one of those questions is architecture, not code — and none of them is answered by
adding another agent. This page is how to answer them: where approval gates belong (and where
they are only theatre), how to keep the blast radius small, which parts of your system should
never be an agent at all, and what the runbook has to say.

### The words, defined

- **Runbook.** A written procedure a human follows when something breaks: "if checkout latency is above 2 s, check the database connection pool, then check the cache hit rate, then…". Most companies have dozens and they are mostly out of date.
- **Autonomy level.** How much a step is allowed to do by itself. Three useful levels: **suggest** (it writes what it would do, a human does it), **approve** (it does it after a human clicks yes), **auto** (it does it, and tells you).
- **Blast radius.** What is affected if this action is wrong. "Restarts one pod" is small. "Rotates a shared credential" is large.
- **Reversible.** Can be undone. Restarting a service is reversible; deleting a database is not; sending an email is technically reversible and practically is not.
- **Approval boundary.** The line in your architecture that actions must cross with a human's consent.
- **Rollback.** The specific, pre-recorded action that undoes what you just did. Not "we would restore from backup" — an actual command, tested.
- **Audit log.** An append-only record of what happened, who or what caused it, and what the system knew at the time.

### The analogy

A new junior engineer joins your on-call rota. On day one you do not give them production access.
You have them shadow — they say what they would do, and you compare. When they are consistently
right, you let them do things with you watching. Eventually, for the three or four routine actions
they have never got wrong, you let them act alone, and you keep the ability to undo it.

Nobody finds this controversial for a human. **Do exactly this for the agent, for exactly the same
reasons, and say so in those words** — it is the argument that wins the meeting, because it maps
onto a process every engineering manager already trusts.

### Map the runbook to a graph, on paper

Take a real runbook. Here is one, abbreviated:

> **Checkout latency alert.** 1. Check the p99 latency panel and confirm it is real, not a scrape
> gap. 2. Check whether a deploy went out in the last 30 minutes; if so, consider rolling back.
> 3. Check database connection pool saturation. 4. Check cache hit rate. 5. If the pool is
> saturated and no deploy went out, scale the connection pool and observe for 5 minutes.
> 6. If it does not recover, page the database on-call.

Now the graph, drawn before any code:

```
START -> confirm_alert
confirm_alert --(scrape gap)--> close_as_noise -> END
confirm_alert --(real)--> gather_context        [deploy history, pool, cache — in parallel]
gather_context -> diagnose
diagnose --(confident, known remedy)--> propose_action
diagnose --(not confident, attempts < 3)--> gather_context      [bounded cycle]
diagnose --(attempts >= 3)--> escalate_to_human -> END
propose_action -> [APPROVAL BOUNDARY] -> execute_action
execute_action -> verify -> END
verify --(not recovered)--> escalate_to_human
```

Count what the runbook told you and the graph made explicit: two exits that are not "fix it", one
parallel gather, one bounded loop, one approval boundary, and a verify step the runbook mentions
only as "observe for 5 minutes". **Writing the runbook as a graph finds the missing steps.** Every
runbook we have converted had at least one — usually the verify.

### Autonomy, decided per step

Draw the graph, then write a level next to each node. For the graph above:

| Step | Level | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| confirm_alert | auto | Read-only. Wrong answer costs one wasted gather. |
| gather_context | auto | Read-only against three systems. Nothing can be changed. |
| diagnose | auto | Produces text and a confidence number. No effect. |
| propose_action | **suggest** at first | It writes what it would do. A human reads it. This is the shadow phase. |
| execute_action | **approve** | An effect on production. A human clicks yes, seeing the resolved arguments. |
| verify | auto | Read-only. |
| escalate | auto | Sends a message. Worst case: an unnecessary page. |

Five of seven nodes are automatic on day one, because they are read-only. **Most of an agentic
system is read-only, and read-only is where the value is** — the 20 minutes an engineer spends
gathering context is the 20 minutes you are actually saving.

<Check>
    **The answer to the opening question, plainly.** A real agentic system is defined by **its edges, not its cleverness**: where a human approves,
    how small the blast radius is when the model is confidently wrong, what a bad day costs, and
    what the person on call actually does at 3 a.m. Approval gates belong wherever an action is
    expensive, irreversible or public — and nowhere else, because a gate nobody reads is worse
    than none. And the parts that are deterministic, high-volume or legally binding should stay
    ordinary software: an agent is for the steps you genuinely cannot enumerate.
    </Check>

    <Accordion title="Checkpoint — 5 questions before you continue">

1. **What is the difference between suggest, approve and auto?** Who performs the action: the human, the human after the agent asks, or the agent.
2. **Why is most of an agentic system safely automatic?** Because most of it is reading, and reads have almost no blast radius.
3. **What does converting a runbook to a graph reliably reveal?** Missing steps — usually the verification, and usually the "this was not a real alert" exit.
4. **What must an approval prompt show?** The resolved arguments and the blast radius, not the verb.
5. **What is a rollback?** A specific pre-recorded command that undoes the action, which you have tested — not a plan to restore from a backup.

### The full architecture

Three agent roles, two data planes, one approval boundary.

```python
# architecture.py — the shape. Details are in the lab.
#
#   ALERT/TICKET
#        |
#   [triage]          one model call, tool_choice forced -> Severity, is_real
#        |
#   [gather] <-------------+     parallel: RAG over runbooks (Week 28)
#        |                 |               MCP read tools (Week 30 M4)
#   [diagnose] -- cycle ---+               metrics, deploys, logs
#        |  (bounded: attempts < 3, novelty detector on, budgets enforced)
#   [propose]         -> Action(kind, arguments, blast_radius, rollback, evidence_ids)
#        |
#   === interrupt(): human sees kind, arguments, blast radius, evidence ===
#        |
#   [execute]         -> MCP write tool, derived idempotency key, outbox
#        |
#   [verify]          -> re-read the signal that fired; did it recover?
#        |
#   [report]          -> post to the ticket, close or escalate
```

Component by component, with the decision behind each:

**Retrieval over runbooks (Week 28).** Hybrid: BM25 for the exact service and error strings, dense
for the paraphrased symptom, reciprocal-rank fusion, then a cross-encoder re-rank of the top 30 to
the top 5. Measured on 120 labelled alert-to-runbook pairs: BM25 alone recall@5 = 0.71, dense alone
0.68, hybrid 0.84, hybrid plus re-rank **0.91**. The re-rank costs 180 ms and is worth it because a
wrong runbook is not a slightly worse answer — it is a confident, well-evidenced proposal to do the
wrong thing.

**MCP for system access (Week 30 M4).** Read tools and write tools in *separate servers* with
separate credentials. The read server has a read-only Kubernetes role and a read-only database role;
the write server has exactly three verbs and is only reachable from the `execute` node. This is not
paranoia, it is the least-privilege argument from Week 30 M4 made structural: a prompt injection in a
log line reaches the read server's context, and the read server cannot do anything.

**Durability and approval (Module 3).** Postgres checkpointer. `interrupt()` at the approval
boundary means the process can exit while a human sleeps; approval hours later resumes the run in a
new process. `approved_by` is a state field.

**Budgets and detectors (Module 4).** Four budgets, novelty detector, breakers on the three
dependencies, structured escalation. Failing runs route to `escalate`, never to `END`.

### The Action contract

The most important type in the system, because it is what a human approves.

```python
from typing import Literal
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field

class Action(BaseModel):
    kind: Literal["restart_pod", "scale_deployment", "clear_cache",
                  "rollback_deploy", "increase_pool_size"]
    arguments: dict[str, str]                 # resolved. "payments-api", not "the service"
    blast_radius: str = Field(description="Plain English. Who is affected, for how long.")
    reversible: bool
    rollback: str | None = Field(description="The exact command that undoes this, or None")
    evidence_ids: list[str] = Field(min_length=1,
        description="Ids of the evidence that justifies this. Empty is not allowed.")
    runbook_id: str | None = Field(description="The runbook this follows, if any")
    confidence: float = Field(ge=0, le=1)

    def approval_prompt(self) -> str:
        return (f"{self.kind}({', '.join(f'{k}={v}' for k, v in self.arguments.items())})\n"
                f"Blast radius: {self.blast_radius}\n"
                f"Reversible: {'yes — ' + self.rollback if self.reversible else 'NO'}\n"
                f"Confidence: {self.confidence:.0%} · runbook {self.runbook_id or 'none'}\n"
                f"Because: {summarize(self.evidence_ids)}")
```

Four constraints that are doing real work:

1. **`kind` is a closed enum.** The agent cannot propose an action the system does not implement. Adding a new capability is a code change with a review, not a prompt change.
2. **`evidence_ids` cannot be empty.** An action with no evidence is refused by the validator before a human ever sees it. This turns "the agent hallucinated a reason" into a caught error.
3. **`rollback` is required when `reversible` is true**, and the executor *records the rollback command before performing the action*. Recording it afterwards is too late — you may not be able to compute it once the state has changed.
4. **The approval prompt is generated from the object**, so the human sees exactly what will run. There is no path where the displayed text and the executed action differ.

### War story

A team built an incident agent and it worked. Diagnosis quality was good, the approval flow was
clean, engineers liked it. It was switched off after five weeks.

The reason was section 9. Nobody owned the prompts. When a diagnosis was wrong, the on-call engineer
had no way to fix it — they were not the team that built the agent, and that team had moved on to
the next project. Two months of small inaccuracies accumulated with no mechanism to correct them,
trust decayed, and engineers went back to doing it by hand. The system was never *wrong* enough to
kill; it just had no owner and so it slowly stopped being believed.

What the second attempt did differently, and it is entirely organizational: the runbook corpus
became owned by the on-call rota, with a pull-request flow, so fixing the agent meant editing a
runbook — something on-call engineers already do. Prompt changes required an eval-suite pass
(Week 33) so a fix could not silently regress something else. And every escalation carried a
one-click "this diagnosis was wrong" that created a labelled eval case. **The technical architecture
was identical. The second one is still running.**

<Accordion title="Design checkpoint — with model answer">

**Question.** Your incident agent can restart a pod. A director asks why it cannot also roll back a
deploy, since that is "just as reversible". Answer them.

**Model answer.** They are right that both are reversible and wrong that reversibility is the only
axis. Three differences.

*Blast radius.* Restarting one pod affects that pod's in-flight requests — for us, about 30 seconds
of elevated 5xx on one replica of a service that has six. Rolling back a deploy affects every user
of that service and, because our deploys are not always independent, potentially services that
depend on a contract introduced in that deploy. The worst case is an order of magnitude larger.

*Reversibility in practice.* Rolling back a deploy is reversible in the sense that you can roll
forward. But if the deploy included a database migration, rolling back the code without the
migration is not a return to a previous state — it is a *new* state nobody has tested. Reversibility
must be evaluated as "returns to a known-good state", not "there is an inverse command".

*Detection.* We can tell within 90 seconds whether a pod restart helped, from the same signal that
fired. Whether a deploy rollback helped is confounded by everything else the deploy contained, so
the verify step is much weaker.

What I would offer instead: put deploy rollback at **suggest** level immediately — the agent
identifies the suspect deploy and writes the exact rollback command with its evidence, and a human
runs it. That captures most of the time saving (finding the deploy is the slow part) with none of
the risk. Then measure agreement for eight weeks. If the agent's suggested rollback matches what the
human did in more than 90% of cases, we have the data to move it to approve. That is a promise with
a date and a number attached, which is a better answer than "no".

</Accordion>

### Blast-radius analysis, done properly

One row per action kind. This is the table a risk owner signs.

| Action | Worst realistic outcome | What stops it | What detects it | Undo | Undo time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `restart_pod` | Restarts the wrong pod: ~30 s of 5xx on 1 of 6 replicas, ~0.4% of requests in that window | Namespace allow-list; `kind` enum; approval showing the resolved pod name | Error-rate alert on the affected service within 60 s | None needed (it comes back) | 45 s |
| `scale_deployment` | Scales to an absurd replica count: cost, and possible database connection exhaustion | Hard `max_replicas` in the tool, not in the prompt; approval | Replica-count alert; database connection saturation alert | Scale back to the recorded prior value | 30 s |
| `clear_cache` | Clears a hot cache: latency spike for ~4 min while it refills, no data loss | Cache-name allow-list; rate limit of once per 15 min per cache | p99 latency alert | None (it refills) | 4 min |
| `increase_pool_size` | Exhausts database connections for other services | Hard ceiling; the tool checks the database's `max_connections` before acting | Connection-saturation alert | Set back to the recorded prior value | 20 s |
| `rollback_deploy` | Rolls back past a database migration into an untested state | **Not automated.** Suggest only. | — | — | — |
| `rotate_credential` | Breaks 4 dependent services simultaneously | **Not in the system at all.** | — | — | — |

Three things this table forces that prose does not:

1. **"What stops it" must name a mechanism in code, not an instruction in a prompt.** If the only thing preventing a 500-replica scale-up is the system prompt saying to be careful, the cell reads "nothing".
2. **"What detects it" must be an existing alert.** If nothing detects the failure, you are relying on a human noticing, and the row should be at approve level at best.
3. **The last two rows exist.** A blast-radius analysis that only covers what you built is a description. One that names what you deliberately excluded, and why, is an argument.

### Staged autonomy rollout, with the gates stated in advance

The mistake is to launch at approve level and then debate autonomy forever. State the gate first.

| Phase | Duration | What the agent does | Gate to advance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Shadow** | 4 weeks | Runs on every alert, writes its proposal to a channel nobody has to read. Does nothing. | Proposal agrees with what the human did in **more than 70%** of cases, and zero proposals that would have been harmful |
| **Suggest** | 6 weeks | Posts its proposal into the incident thread with a copyable command | Human uses the suggestion in more than 60% of incidents; measured MTTR improvement |
| **Approve** | 8 weeks | Executes after a human clicks approve | Approval rate above 90% for a given action kind, across at least 50 instances of that kind |
| **Auto (per action kind)** | ongoing | Executes automatically, posts what it did, rollback armed | Per kind, not globally. Any incident caused by the agent returns that kind to approve. |

**Our numbers.** Shadow: 1,240 proposals, 71% agreement with the human action, 3 proposals judged
harmful (all three were `scale_deployment` with an absurd target — which is what produced the hard
ceiling in the tool). Suggest: 6 weeks, engineers used the suggestion in 68% of incidents; median
time-to-resolution moved from 41 to 33 minutes, entirely from the gathering phase. Approve: 890
proposals, 94% approved, 6% declined with a reason (52 of those 53 declines became eval cases).
Auto: enabled for **3 of 11 action kinds** — `restart_pod`, `clear_cache`, `increase_pool_size` —
and MTTR is now 26 minutes.

Two honest observations. **The biggest single win came at suggest level**, not at auto: 41 to 33
minutes, from the agent doing the reading. Autonomy over actions bought a further 7 minutes and
carried nearly all of the risk. And **the 6% decline rate is the most valuable output of the approve
phase** — those are labelled failures, arriving free, and they became the seed of the Week 33 eval
set.

### Auditability: what must be recorded

For every run, permanently:

| Field | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Thread id, start and end, final `halt_reason` | The spine of every later query |
| Every node entered, with state delta and duration | "Which step went wrong" |
| Every model call: model id, prompt version, token counts, cost | Attribution when quality changes (Week 34 M2) |
| Every tool call: name, resolved arguments, result digest, latency | The security question from Week 30 M4 |
| Every retrieval: query, document ids returned, scores | "Why did it think that" — usually the answer |
| The `Action` object, verbatim, as approved | What was *actually* sanctioned |
| `approved_by`, timestamp, and the exact text shown to the approver | The compliance question. The last clause matters: showing a different string than you executed is the failure. |
| Rollback command recorded **before** execution | It may be uncomputable afterwards |
| Any `update_state` intervention: actor, field, old, new | The human write path bypasses everything else |

Retention: the audit log outlives the checkpoints. Ours keeps checkpoints for 7 days (Module 3) and
the audit log for 13 months, because the question "what did this system do in March" arrives in
April of the following year, from someone in a different department.

### The organizational reality

The technical architecture is the easy half. Four questions decide whether the system survives, and
all four belong in the design document:

1. **Who owns the prompts and the runbook corpus?** If it is the team that built it, the system dies when that team moves on (the war story). It must be the team that *feels the failures* — the on-call rota — with a normal pull-request flow.
2. **Who is paged when the agent misbehaves?** There must be a name. An agent with no on-call owner is a service with no on-call owner.
3. **What is the mechanism for a human to say "that was wrong"?** One click, from the place they already are, producing a labelled case. If correcting the agent is more work than doing the task by hand, nobody will do it, and the system cannot improve.
4. **What does it cost, monthly, and who sees that number?** Ours: $310 in model spend, roughly 1.5 engineer-days per month of maintenance (prompt fixes, runbook updates, eval triage). The maintenance number is the one that surprises people and the one that must be budgeted, or it comes out of somebody's slack time and then stops happening.

### Papers and primary sources

- **Beyer et al., *Site Reliability Engineering*, chapters on incident response and postmortem culture.** Takeaway: incident response is a *role* structure (incident commander, ops lead, communications) before it is a procedure — an agent slots in as a very fast ops lead and never as the commander, and that framing settles most autonomy arguments.
- **Anthropic, *Building Effective Agents*.** Takeaway: it is explicit that autonomy should be added only where the branching is genuinely unknown and that human checkpoints belong at the boundaries of irreversible actions — the same conclusion this module reaches from the blast-radius side.
- **LangGraph reference architectures and the human-in-the-loop guide (0.2.74).** Takeaway: `interrupt` plus a durable checkpointer is what makes an hours-long approval wait cheap, which is what makes approve-level autonomy operationally viable rather than a nice idea.
- **Woods & Hollnagel, *Joint Cognitive Systems* (2006).** Takeaway: automation does not remove human work, it changes it — usually into the harder work of supervising an opaque process — which is exactly why the "this was wrong" button and the structured escalation are architecture, not polish.

### Stretch challenge

Write the design document and blast-radius analysis for a system you would actually be allowed to
run at your own workplace, and then get it reviewed by someone who would have to sign it.

The document is the artifact. The review is the exercise. Take it to the person who owns the systems
your agent would touch — an SRE lead, a security engineer, a database owner — and ask them to find
the row in your blast-radius table that is wrong. They will. Ours had two: we had classified
`clear_cache` as harmless, and the person who owned that cache pointed out that clearing it during a
traffic peak causes a thundering herd against a database that is already the bottleneck, which turns
a 4-minute latency blip into an outage. That single conversation moved the action from auto to
approve and added the rate limit.

The genuinely open question you will hit: how do you decide when to move the approval boundary left
for an action kind you have only seen 12 times? Fifty instances is a comfortable gate; some action
kinds will never reach fifty in a year. There is a real statistical answer here (Week 12's
confidence intervals on a proportion — 12 successes out of 12 gives a 95% lower bound of about 74%,
which is nowhere near 90%) and a real organizational one, and they disagree. Write down which one
you would use, and what you would tell the person who has to sign it.

## The whole idea on one whiteboard

Autonomy is not a property of a system. It is a property of **each step**, and it is rationed by two
questions asked per step: *how bad is the worst outcome*, and *can we undo it*. The architecture is
then almost mechanical — reversible low-impact steps run automatically, irreversible or high-impact
steps go behind a human, and everything is recorded so the answer to "what happened" is a query
rather than an investigation.

**3. Propose shadow mode, not autonomy.** When the agentic proposal arrives, the intervention that
gets it shipped is "run it in shadow for four weeks and measure agreement". It is unarguable, it
costs nothing, and it produces the data that every later conversation needs.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The project dies quietly after two months | No owner for prompts and runbooks | Ownership by the on-call rota, with a pull-request flow they already use |
| Approval fatigue; humans click yes without reading | The prompt shows the verb, not the resolved arguments and blast radius | Generate the prompt from the `Action` object; include evidence |
| The agent proposes an action the system cannot do | Action space is prose, not a type | Closed `Literal` enum for `kind`; new capabilities are code reviews |
| An action cannot be undone because the prior state is gone | Rollback computed after the action | Record the rollback command before executing |
| "What stops it" is the system prompt | Controls in text rather than code | Hard ceilings and allow-lists in the tool; the prompt is not a control |
| Autonomy debated forever | No numeric gate stated in advance | Shadow/suggest/approve/auto with per-kind gates written before launch |
| Wrong runbook retrieved, confident wrong proposal | Dense-only retrieval | Hybrid plus re-rank; 0.68 to 0.91 recall@5 in our measurement |
| Injected instruction from a log line reaches a write tool | One MCP server for reads and writes | Separate servers, separate credentials, writes reachable only from `execute` |
| Nobody can answer "what did it do in March" | Audit log retained as long as checkpoints | Audit log outlives checkpoints — ours 13 months against 7 days |
| Maintenance cost surprises everyone at quarter end | Only model spend was budgeted | Budget engineer-days too. Ours is 1.5 per month, and it is the number that decides survival. |

## Assessment

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="8 concept questions (with answers)" icon="circle-question">

1. *Is autonomy a property of a system?* No — of each step, rationed by blast radius and reversibility.
2. *Why is most of an agentic system safely automatic?* Most of it reads, and reads have near-zero blast radius. The value is also mostly there.
3. *What must an approval prompt contain?* Resolved arguments, blast radius, reversibility with the rollback, confidence, and the evidence.
4. *What makes "reversible" a trickier property than it looks?* An inverse command is not the same as returning to a known-good state — a code rollback past a migration is a new, untested state.
5. *Where does the biggest time saving come from?* Suggest level: the agent doing the context gathering. Ours was 41 to 33 minutes before any action was automated.
6. *What is the most valuable output of the approve phase?* The declines — labelled failures, free, which become eval cases.
7. *Why must the audit log outlive the checkpoints?* Questions about what the system did arrive months later; checkpoints are pruned in days.
8. *What kills these projects?* Not accuracy. No owner for the prompts, and no cheap way for a human to say "that was wrong".

</Accordion>
