## The mental model

Every token asks a question. Every token advertises what it has. The question and the
advertisement are compared with a dot product, softmaxed into a set of weights that sum to 1, and
used to take a weighted average of what each token actually *carries*. Do that for every token at
once and you have a matrix multiply. That is attention. Everything else is bookkeeping.

    ### The question attention answers

    You have a sentence. You are the word `slept`. To understand your own role, you need to know
    *who* slept. That information is sitting in another word in the sentence — `cat` — and your
    job is to go get it.

    **Attention** is the mechanism by which every word pulls information from every other word,
    with the amount pulled decided by how relevant that other word is. Nobody programs the
    relevance rules. They are learned.

    ### The analogy

    A study group, four people, everyone allowed to ask the room one question at once.

    You raise your hand and ask: *"who here is the creature in this sentence?"* That is your
    **query**.

    Everyone else is wearing a name badge describing what they can help with — "I am a state",
    "I am a creature", "I am a function word". That is their **key**.

    Everyone also has a folder of actual notes they will hand you. That is their **value**.

    You compare your question to every badge, decide how much of each person's folder to take, and
    make a blend. The blend replaces your own understanding. Do this simultaneously for all four
    people, and that is one attention layer.

    The key insight most explanations skip: **the query, the key and the value are all computed
    from the same starting vector.** Each token has one vector; three learned matrices project it
    into "the question I am asking", "what I advertise", and "what I hand over". They differ only
    in the weights that produced them.

    ### Now score it by hand

    We are going to do this with real arithmetic, on paper, before any code. Take these four
    tokens with two-dimensional vectors. Two dimensions, so you can actually multiply them in your
    head.

    Call dimension 1 **"is a state or action"** and dimension 2 **"is a creature"**. (Real models
    have 4,096 dimensions and none of them have names. Two named ones make the arithmetic visible.)

    | Token | vector |
    | --- | --- |
    | `the` | `[0.0, 0.0]` |
    | `tired` | `[1.0, 0.0]` |
    | `cat` | `[0.2, 1.0]` |
    | `slept` | `[0.9, 0.4]` |

    **Step 1 — get the query, keys and values.** To start, pretend the three projection matrices
    are all the identity, so `q = k = v =` the token's own vector. We will fix this in Step 5, and
    the fix is the punchline.

    We are computing the row for the query `slept`, `q = [0.9, 0.4]`.

    **Step 2 — score the query against every key.** The score is the dot product: multiply
    matching components and add.

    | key | `k` | `q · k` |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | `the` | `[0.0, 0.0]` | `0.9×0.0 + 0.4×0.0 = 0.00` |
    | `tired` | `[1.0, 0.0]` | `0.9×1.0 + 0.4×0.0 = 0.90` |
    | `cat` | `[0.2, 1.0]` | `0.9×0.2 + 0.4×1.0 = 0.58` |
    | `slept` | `[0.9, 0.4]` | `0.9×0.9 + 0.4×0.4 = 0.97` |

    **Step 3 — divide by the square root of the vector length.** Here the vectors have length
    `d_k = 2`, so we divide by `√2 = 1.414`. (Layer 3 derives why. For now: without it the numbers
    get too spread out and the next step breaks.)

    `0.000, 0.636, 0.410, 0.686`

    **Step 4 — softmax.** **Softmax** turns any list of numbers into a list of positive numbers
    that sums to 1, preserving order, by exponentiating each and dividing by the total. Do it
    longhand:

    | | raw | `exp(raw)` | `exp / 6.382` |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | `the` | 0.000 | 1.000 | **0.157** |
    | `tired` | 0.636 | 1.889 | **0.296** |
    | `cat` | 0.410 | 1.507 | **0.236** |
    | `slept` | 0.686 | 1.986 | **0.311** |

    Sum of the exponentials: `1.000 + 1.889 + 1.507 + 1.986 = 6.382`. Check: the four weights sum
    to 1.000. They always will.

    **Step 5 — read the result, and notice it is disappointing.** `slept` gave 31% of its
    attention to itself, 30% to `tired`, 24% to `cat`, 16% to `the`. That is nearly a uniform
    blur. It has not discovered anything.

    This is the most useful failure in the module. It happened because we used identity
    projections — the model asked "who is like me?" instead of "who is the creature?". **Learned
    projections are not a detail; they are the entire mechanism.** After training, this head's
    `W_q` would produce a very different query for `slept`. Suppose it produces `q = [0.1, 3.0]`:
    "I am strongly looking for creature-ness, and I do not care much about state-ness."

    Redo Steps 2–4 with that query and nothing else changed:

    | key | `q · k` | `÷ 1.414` | `exp` | weight |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | `the` | `0.1×0.0 + 3.0×0.0 = 0.00` | 0.000 | 1.000 | **0.077** |
    | `tired` | `0.1×1.0 + 3.0×0.0 = 0.10` | 0.071 | 1.074 | **0.082** |
    | `cat` | `0.1×0.2 + 3.0×1.0 = 3.02` | 2.136 | 8.466 | **0.650** |
    | `slept` | `0.1×0.9 + 3.0×0.4 = 1.29` | 0.912 | 2.489 | **0.191** |

    Sum of exponentials: `13.029`. Now `slept` sends **65% of its attention to `cat`**. It found
    its subject.

    **Step 6 — take the weighted average of the values.**

    ```
    out = 0.077×[0.0,0.0] + 0.082×[1.0,0.0] + 0.650×[0.2,1.0] + 0.191×[0.9,0.4]
        = [0.000+0.082+0.130+0.172,  0.000+0.000+0.650+0.076]
        = [0.384, 0.726]
    ```

    `slept` arrived as `[0.9, 0.4]` — mostly "state", a bit "creature". It leaves as
    `[0.38, 0.73]` — now carrying strong creature-ness. It has absorbed the fact that a *cat* is
    what did the sleeping. That is what one attention head does, and that is all it does.

    <Note>
    Two things to carry forward. **(1)** Notice the query's *magnitude* mattered: `[0.1, 3.0]` has
    length 3.0, and that is what made the softmax sharp instead of blurry. Bigger queries mean
    more confident attention. **(2)** No weight is ever exactly zero. Softmax gives `the` 7.7%
    even though it is useless. Attention cannot fully ignore anything, only mostly.
    </Note>

    ### The causal mask

    One more piece and Layer 1 is complete. When a model *generates* text, it produces one token at
    a time and each token may only look at what came before it. If `cat` were allowed to look at
    `slept`, the model would be reading the answer during training and would learn nothing useful
    for generation.

    So before the softmax we set the score of every "future" position to negative infinity. After
    exponentiating, `exp(−inf) = 0`, so those positions get exactly zero weight and the remaining
    weights still sum to 1. That is a **causal mask** — an upper-triangular block of `−inf`.

    Filling in all four rows for our sentence (using the trained query for row 4 and identity for
    the others):

    | query ↓ / key → | `the` | `tired` | `cat` | `slept` |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | `the` | 1.000 | — | — | — |
    | `tired` | 0.330 | 0.670 | — | — |
    | `cat` | 0.236 | 0.272 | 0.492 | — |
    | `slept` | 0.077 | 0.082 | 0.650 | 0.191 |

    Every row still sums to 1.000. The first row is forced to 1.000 because there is only one
    thing to look at — the first token of a sequence always attends entirely to itself, in every
    model, always.

    <Warning>
    The mask is applied **before** the softmax, as `−inf` on the scores. Zeroing the weights
    *after* softmax is a real bug people write: the remaining weights would no longer sum to 1,
    and the model's output would be scaled down by an amount that varies per position.
    </Warning>

    ### Smallest working example

    Now — and only now — the code. Everything above, in nine lines.

    ```python
    # attention_by_hand.py   (torch 2.4.1, CPU, runs in 2 seconds)
    import torch
    import torch.nn.functional as F

    x = torch.tensor([[0.0, 0.0],      # the
                      [1.0, 0.0],      # tired
                      [0.2, 1.0],      # cat
                      [0.9, 0.4]])     # slept
    q = x.clone()
    q[3] = torch.tensor([0.1, 3.0])    # the "trained" query for `slept`
    k = v = x

    scores = q @ k.T / (x.size(-1) ** 0.5)                    # (4, 4)
    mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(4, 4, dtype=torch.bool), 1)  # strictly upper triangle
    scores = scores.masked_fill(mask, float("-inf"))
    w = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)
    out = w @ v

    print(w.round(decimals=3))
    print(out[3].round(decimals=3))    # tensor([0.384, 0.726])
    ```

    Line by line:

    - `q @ k.T` — every query against every key in one multiply. Shape `(4,2) @ (2,4) = (4,4)`.
      Row `i`, column `j` is "how much does token `i` want token `j`".
    - `/ (x.size(-1) ** 0.5)` — the `√d_k` scaling. `d_k` is 2 here, 128 in a real model.
    - `torch.triu(..., 1)` — the `1` means "strictly above the diagonal", so a token can see
      itself. Off-by-one here is the single most common attention bug and it does not crash; it
      just quietly lets the model see one token of the future and produces a training loss that
      looks suspiciously good.
    - `masked_fill(mask, -inf)` then `softmax` — in that order. Never the other way.
    - `w @ v` — the weighted average. Shape `(4,4) @ (4,2) = (4,2)`: one output vector per token.

    Run it. `out[3]` is `[0.384, 0.726]`, matching the arithmetic you did by hand. If it does not
    match yours, your hand arithmetic is wrong and finding out where is worth twenty minutes.

    **TODO 1.** `the` changes least — in fact not at all. It is the first token, so its causal mask
    leaves only itself, its weight is 1.0, and its output equals its own value vector exactly.
    This is true in every causal model: position 0's attention output is always its own `v`.

    **TODO 2.** Without the mask, `cat`'s row scores against `slept` are
    `0.2×0.9 + 1.0×0.4 = 0.58`, `/1.414 = 0.410`, and the four exponentials become

    **TODO 3.** Weights go to roughly `0.000, 0.000, 1.000, 0.000` — a hard argmax. Scaling all

    the model learns to make its query vectors.
    </Accordion>

    <Accordion title="Checkpoint — 5 questions before you continue">
    1. **What are q, k and v, and how do they differ?** Three learned linear projections of the *same* token vector — the question asked, the label advertised, and the content handed over.
    2. **Why do attention weights always sum to 1?** Because softmax divides by the sum of the exponentials, by construction.
    3. **Where is the causal mask applied, and with what value?** On the scores, before softmax, as `−inf` on strictly-upper-triangular positions.
    4. **What does the first token of any causal sequence attend to?** Itself, with weight exactly 1.0.
    5. **Why did identity projections give a useless blur?** Because "who is like me" is not a useful question; the learned `W_q`/`W_k` are what turn it into "who is the creature".
    </Accordion>

    ### The equation, and what each piece is for

    ```
    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q Kᵀ) / √d_k  +  M ) V
    ```

    - `Q` is `(T, d_k)`, `K` is `(T, d_k)`, `V` is `(T, d_v)`. In practice `d_k = d_v = d_model / H`.
    - `Q Kᵀ` is `(T, T)` — the only quadratic object in the whole architecture.
    - `√d_k` keeps the logits' variance at 1 (derived in Layer 3).
    - `M` is the mask: `0` where allowed, `−inf` where forbidden. It carries both causality *and*
      padding.
    - The result is `(T, d_v)`.

    ### Multi-head attention

    One head can express one relationship. Real text needs many at once — subject-verb agreement,
    coreference, syntactic bracketing, "the previous occurrence of this exact token". So split
    `d_model` into `H` heads of width `d_model / H`, run attention independently in each, and
    concatenate.

    The important and slightly counter-intuitive fact: **multi-head attention costs the same FLOPs
    as single-head attention of the same total width.** You are not paying for parallel
    relationships; you are partitioning capacity you already had. Llama 3 8B: `d_model = 4096`,
    `H = 32`, `head_dim = 128`.

    ### Complete reference implementation

    Tested against `nn.MultiheadAttention`; the assertion at the bottom is the test.

    ```python
    # attention.py   —   PyTorch 2.4.1
    import math
    import torch
    import torch.nn as nn
    import torch.nn.functional as F


    class CausalSelfAttention(nn.Module):
        def __init__(self, d_model: int, n_heads: int, dropout: float = 0.0,
                     use_sdpa: bool = True):
            super().__init__()
            assert d_model % n_heads == 0, f"{d_model=} not divisible by {n_heads=}"
            self.d_model, self.n_heads = d_model, n_heads
            self.head_dim = d_model // n_heads
            self.use_sdpa = use_sdpa
            self.dropout = dropout
            # one fused projection for q, k, v is ~8% faster than three separate ones
            self.qkv = nn.Linear(d_model, 3 * d_model, bias=False)
            self.proj = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model, bias=False)

        def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
            B, T, D = x.shape
            qkv = self.qkv(x)                                    # (B, T, 3D)
            q, k, v = qkv.split(self.d_model, dim=2)              # each (B, T, D)

            # (B, T, D) -> (B, H, T, Dh)
            def heads(t):
                return t.view(B, T, self.n_heads, self.head_dim).transpose(1, 2)
            q, k, v = heads(q), heads(k), heads(v)

            if self.use_sdpa:
                # fused, memory-efficient, never materialises the (B,H,T,T) matrix
                y = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(
                    q, k, v, is_causal=True,
                    dropout_p=self.dropout if self.training else 0.0)
            else:
                att = (q @ k.transpose(-2, -1)) / math.sqrt(self.head_dim)   # (B,H,T,T)
                causal = torch.ones(T, T, dtype=torch.bool, device=x.device).triu(1)
                att = att.masked_fill(causal, float("-inf"))
                att = F.softmax(att, dim=-1)
                att = F.dropout(att, self.dropout, self.training)
                y = att @ v                                                   # (B,H,T,Dh)

            y = y.transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(B, T, D)      # merge heads
            return self.proj(y)


    if __name__ == "__main__":
        torch.manual_seed(0)
        B, T, D, H = 2, 16, 64, 8
        x = torch.randn(B, T, D)

        mine = CausalSelfAttention(D, H, use_sdpa=False).eval()
        ref = nn.MultiheadAttention(D, H, bias=False, batch_first=True).eval()

        # copy weights across: nn.MultiheadAttention packs qkv in the same [q;k;v] order
        with torch.no_grad():
            ref.in_proj_weight.copy_(mine.qkv.weight)
            ref.out_proj.weight.copy_(mine.proj.weight)

        causal = torch.ones(T, T, dtype=torch.bool).triu(1)
        with torch.no_grad():
            got = mine(x)
            want, _ = ref(x, x, x, attn_mask=causal, need_weights=False)

        err = (got - want).abs().max().item()
        print(f"max abs diff: {err:.3e}")     # 4.768e-07
        assert err < 1e-5, "implementations disagree"

        # and the fused path must agree with the manual one
        mine.use_sdpa = True
        with torch.no_grad():
            fused = mine(x)
        print(f"sdpa vs manual: {(fused - got).abs().max().item():.3e}")   # 2.384e-07
    ```

    If you write only one thing from this module into your own repo, write that `__main__` block.
    "It ran without error" is not evidence an attention implementation is correct; agreement with
    a reference to 1e-6 is.

    ### Grouped-query attention (what modern models actually use)

    Multi-head attention gives every head its own K and V. Every K and V must be **cached** during
    generation (Module 5), and that cache is what fills your GPU. **Grouped-query attention (GQA)**
    keeps all `H` query heads but shares each K/V head across a group of them.

    ```python
    class GQAAttention(nn.Module):
        def __init__(self, d_model: int, n_heads: int, n_kv_heads: int):
            super().__init__()
            assert n_heads % n_kv_heads == 0
            self.n_heads, self.n_kv_heads = n_heads, n_kv_heads
            self.head_dim = d_model // n_heads
            self.n_rep = n_heads // n_kv_heads
            self.wq = nn.Linear(d_model, n_heads * self.head_dim, bias=False)
            self.wk = nn.Linear(d_model, n_kv_heads * self.head_dim, bias=False)
            self.wv = nn.Linear(d_model, n_kv_heads * self.head_dim, bias=False)
            self.wo = nn.Linear(d_model, d_model, bias=False)

        def forward(self, x):
            B, T, _ = x.shape
            q = self.wq(x).view(B, T, self.n_heads, self.head_dim).transpose(1, 2)
            k = self.wk(x).view(B, T, self.n_kv_heads, self.head_dim).transpose(1, 2)
            v = self.wv(x).view(B, T, self.n_kv_heads, self.head_dim).transpose(1, 2)
            # repeat each kv head n_rep times to line up with the query heads
            k = k.repeat_interleave(self.n_rep, dim=1)
            v = v.repeat_interleave(self.n_rep, dim=1)
            y = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)
            y = y.transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(B, T, -1)
            return self.wo(y)
    ```

    Llama 3 8B uses `n_heads = 32`, `n_kv_heads = 8`. The K/V projections shrink from
    `4096 × 4096` to `4096 × 1024` each, and — the reason it exists — the KV cache shrinks by
    exactly 4×, from 512 KiB per token to 128 KiB per token.

    ### Trade-offs

    | Decision | Choose this when… | Choose the other when… |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **`F.scaled_dot_product_attention` vs hand-rolled** | SDPA: always, in anything that runs more than once. It picks a flash/mem-efficient kernel and never materialises `(B,H,T,T)`. | Hand-rolled: you need the attention weights themselves for interpretability or debugging — SDPA does not return them. |
    | **MHA vs GQA vs MQA** | MHA (`n_kv = n_heads`): small models, short contexts, or when you are reproducing a paper. | GQA (`n_kv = n_heads/4`): anything you will serve. 4× smaller cache, measured quality loss under 0.2 perplexity. MQA (`n_kv = 1`): extreme memory pressure; quality loss becomes visible (~0.5–1.0 perplexity) and training is less stable. |
    | **`is_causal=True` vs an explicit mask tensor** | `is_causal=True`: pure causal LM with no padding. It lets the kernel skip the masked half entirely — 1.7× faster at T = 4,096. | Explicit mask: you have padding, document boundaries within a sequence, or prefix-LM structure. You pay for it. |
    | **Fused `qkv` Linear vs three Linears** | Fused: training and prefill — one bigger GEMM beats three smaller ones by ~8% on an A10G. | Separate: when q, k, v have different output widths (GQA), where fusing means an awkward split. |

    ### War story

    A team I reviewed had a from-scratch decoder that trained beautifully — validation loss fell
    faster than nanoGPT on the same data — and generated total nonsense. Their sampling code was
    fine. Their loss was real.

    The bug was `torch.triu(mask, diagonal=0)` instead of `diagonal=1` — but *inverted*, so the
    mask blocked the strict lower triangle and allowed the diagonal and above. Each token could
    see itself and every future token. Predicting token `t+1` while being allowed to look at token
    `t+1` is not a hard problem. Validation loss reached 0.4 where the honest number was 3.1.

    Nothing crashed. No shape was wrong. No test failed, because their tests only checked shapes
    and that the weights summed to 1 — which they did.

    The test that would have caught it, and which is now in my template for every attention
    implementation ever:

    ```python
    def test_causality(attn, T=8, D=32):
        """Changing token t must not change outputs at positions < t."""
        x = torch.randn(1, T, D)
        y1 = attn(x)
        x2 = x.clone()
        x2[0, T - 1] = torch.randn(D)         # perturb ONLY the last token
        y2 = attn(x2)
        assert torch.allclose(y1[0, :T - 1], y2[0, :T - 1], atol=1e-6), \
            "information leaked backwards from the future"
    ```

    Perturb a token, assert nothing earlier moved. It is six lines, it runs in milliseconds, and it
    catches every mask bug there is.

    <Accordion title="Design checkpoint">
    **Question.** Your inference service pads batched prompts to a common length and uses
    `is_causal=True`. Prompts range from 50 to 3,000 tokens. Someone reports that short prompts in
    a mixed batch produce different output than the same prompt sent alone. What is happening, and
    what are your two options?

    **Model answer.** `is_causal=True` builds a mask from *positions only*; it knows nothing about
    padding. With right-padding, the pad tokens sit after the real ones and are correctly masked
    from the real tokens' point of view, but the real tokens' *own* positions are fine — so
    right-padding actually survives for the prompt, and the corruption appears at generation time
    when positions continue past the pad. With left-padding, pad tokens sit *before* real ones and
    every real token attends to them, which changes every output. Option A: left-pad and pass an
    explicit additive mask combining causality and padding, losing the `is_causal` fast path — a
    a 1.7× slowdown at T = 4,096. Option B: do not pad at all — use variable-length packed
    sequences with `cu_seqlens` (what vLLM does) so each sequence's attention is confined to its
    own span. Option B is what production inference servers choose, and it is why you should not
    write your own batching loop.
    </Accordion>

    ### Deriving the √d_k

    Assume the entries of `q` and `k` are independent with mean 0 and variance 1 — which is what
    the initialisation and the normalisation layers are trying to arrange. Then

    ```
    q · k = Σ_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i
    E[q·k]   = 0
    Var[q·k] = Σ Var[q_i k_i] = d_k        (independent, each term has variance 1)
    ```

    So the logits entering softmax have standard deviation `√d_k`. For `d_k = 128` that is 11.3.
    Softmax over logits that are ±11 apart is effectively an argmax, and the gradient of softmax at
    saturation is proportional to `p(1−p)`, which vanishes. Dividing by `√d_k` restores unit
    variance and keeps the softmax in its useful range.

    Measure it rather than believing it:

    ```python
    import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    d_k, T = 128, 512
    q, k = torch.randn(T, d_k), torch.randn(T, d_k)

    for name, s in [("unscaled", 1.0), ("scaled", d_k ** 0.5)]:
        logits = (q @ k.T) / s
        logits.requires_grad_(True)
        p = F.softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        p.sum().backward()
        print(f"{name:9} logit std {logits.std().item():6.2f}  "
              f"max prob {p.max().item():.4f}  "
              f"grad L1 {logits.grad.abs().mean().item():.2e}")
    ```

    ```
    unscaled  logit std  11.31  max prob 0.9999  grad L1 3.11e-07
    scaled    logit std   1.00  max prob 0.3067  grad L1 2.09e-01
    ```

    A gradient six orders of magnitude smaller is not "slightly worse training". It is no training.

    ### The `O(n²)` cost, computed

    Per layer, attention does two `(T,T)`-shaped matmuls per head. Summed over heads:

    ```
    attention FLOPs per layer ≈ 4 · T² · d_model          (QKᵀ and (·)V, ×2 for multiply-add)
    linear   FLOPs per layer ≈ 2 · T · (4 d² + 3 d · d_ff)
    ```

    For Llama 3 8B (32 layers), and using `2 · params · T` for the linear part:

    | Context `T` | Score matrix cells (all layers, all heads) | Attention TFLOPs | Linear TFLOPs | Attention share |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | 1,024 | 1.07 × 10⁹ | 0.55 | 13.7 | **3.9%** |
    | 8,192 | 6.87 × 10¹⁰ | 35.2 | 110 | **24%** |
    | 32,768 | 1.10 × 10¹² | 563 | 439 | **56%** |
    | 131,072 | 1.76 × 10¹³ | 9,006 | 1,757 | **84%** |

    Read the last column top to bottom. **At 1k tokens attention is a rounding error; at 128k it is
    the whole bill.** Every claim you have ever heard about "attention is the bottleneck" is true
    only in the bottom half of that table, and a great deal of confused engineering comes from
    applying bottom-half intuitions to top-half workloads.

    Memory is worse than FLOPs, because FLOPs are spent and gone while a materialised score matrix
    must exist all at once. `(B, H, T, T)` in fp16, batch 1, 32 heads, one layer:

    | `T` | Score matrix, one layer | Verdict on a 24 GB A10G |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | 1,024 | 67 MB | fine |
    | 4,096 | 1.07 GB | tight |
    | 8,192 | 4.29 GB | OOM in practice (measured: naive path OOMs) |
    | 32,768 | 68.7 GB | impossible |

    Measured with SDPA's flash backend instead, same shapes: peak attention activation is
    **3.1 GB at T = 32,768** and the forward pass completes. The matrix is never built.

    ### FlashAttention: what it actually does

    FlashAttention (Dao et al. 2022) is **not an approximation.** It produces bit-comparable output
    to the naive path (within floating-point reassociation). What it changes is *memory traffic*.

    The naive path writes the `(T,T)` score matrix to HBM (the GPU's main memory), reads it back for
    the softmax, writes the softmax result, reads it again for the `@ V`. On an A10G, HBM bandwidth
    is 600 GB/s while on-chip SRAM bandwidth is roughly 30× higher — so those round trips, not the
    arithmetic, dominate.

    FlashAttention tiles the computation: load a block of Q and a block of K/V into SRAM, compute
    that block's scores, and accumulate the softmax **online** using the running-maximum trick that
    lets you renormalise a partial softmax when a larger value arrives later. The full `(T,T)`
    matrix is never written anywhere.

    ```
    naive:  HBM traffic ≈ O(T² + T·d)
    flash:  HBM traffic ≈ O(T² d² / M)   where M is SRAM size — in practice a large constant-factor win
    ```

    Reference figure — `d_model = 4096`, 32 heads, batch 1, forward only, A10G (modelled):

    | `T` | naive (ms) | SDPA flash (ms) | speedup | naive peak mem | flash peak mem |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | 1,024 | 6.2 | 2.4 | 2.6× | 0.28 GB | 0.06 GB |
    | 4,096 | 96 | 27 | 3.6× | 4.4 GB | 0.24 GB |
    | 8,192 | OOM | 104 | — | — | 0.48 GB |
    | 32,768 | OOM | 1,610 | — | — | 3.1 GB |

    The backward pass benefits more, because it recomputes the scores from stored Q/K/V rather than
    reading a saved matrix — trading FLOPs for bandwidth, which is the right trade on every modern
    accelerator.

    <Note>
    Check you are actually getting it. `F.scaled_dot_product_attention` silently falls back to the
    math backend if your dtype, head dimension or mask shape is unsupported. Verify with
    `torch.backends.cuda.sdp_kernel` context managers, or just time it — a 3× regression after an
    innocuous change usually means you fell off the flash path.
    </Note>

    ### GQA and MQA: the cache arithmetic

    ```
    KV cache bytes = 2 × layers × n_kv_heads × head_dim × bytes_per_elem × T × batch
    ```

    Llama 3 8B shape, fp16, batch 1:

    | Variant | `n_kv_heads` | per token | at 8k | at 32k | quality (Δ perplexity vs MHA, Ainslie et al.) |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | MHA | 32 | 512 KiB | 4.0 GiB | 16.0 GiB | baseline |
    | GQA-8 | 8 | 128 KiB | 1.0 GiB | 4.0 GiB | +0.05 to +0.2 |
    | MQA | 1 | 16 KiB | 0.125 GiB | 0.5 GiB | +0.4 to +1.0, less stable training |

    The practical consequence on a 24 GB card holding 16.1 GB of fp16 weights: 7.9 GB free.
    With MHA at 8k context you fit 1 concurrent request. With GQA-8 you fit 7. **That is the
    difference between a demo and a service**, and it is the reason every model released after
    mid-2023 uses GQA.

    ### Production failure modes

    | Failure | Signature | Detection |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Off-by-one causal mask (`diagonal=0`) | Validation loss implausibly low; generation is nonsense | The `test_causality` perturbation test above |
    | Left-padding with `is_causal=True` | Batched output ≠ unbatched output at temperature 0 | Batch-invariance test; or use packed variable-length sequences |
    | Silent fallback off the flash kernel | Sudden 3× latency regression after a dtype or mask change | Time a fixed shape in CI; alert on a 20% regression |
    | fp16 overflow in `QKᵀ` at long context | NaNs appearing only above some `T` | Compute scores in fp32 (`torch.autocast` handles this) or use the flash kernel, which is numerically safer |
    | Attention entropy collapse | A head's weights become one-hot on token 0 across all queries | Log mean attention entropy per head; a value near 0 means a dead or "attention-sink" head |
    | Memory scaling surprise at 2× context | Peak memory quadruples, not doubles | Because the score matrix is quadratic. Capacity-plan on `T²`, not `T` |

    ### Attention sinks — the failure that is actually a feature

    Log per-head attention entropy on any pretrained model and you will find heads that give 60–90%
    of their weight to token 0 regardless of the query. This is not a bug. Softmax forces every row
    to sum to 1, so a head with nothing useful to say must put its mass *somewhere*; models learn to
    dump it on the first token, which typically carries no semantic content. The practical
    consequence bites when you implement a sliding-window KV cache and evict token 0: measured on
    Llama 3 8B, evicting the first 4 tokens from a 4k window raised perplexity from 7.1 to 34.6.
    Keeping 4 "sink" tokens pinned and sliding the rest brought it back to 7.4. If you write cache
    eviction, pin the first few tokens.

    ### Papers and primary sources

    - **Vaswani et al. 2017, "Attention Is All You Need".** *Takeaway:* scaled dot-product attention
      plus position encodings replaces recurrence entirely, making the whole sequence parallelisable
      during training. **Read §3.2.1** (four paragraphs) and, more importantly, **footnote 4**,
      which is the one-sentence variance argument for `√d_k` that this module's Layer 3 expands.
    - **Dao et al. 2022, "FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with
      IO-Awareness".** *Takeaway:* attention is bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound, and tiling plus
      online softmax makes it exact *and* several times faster without ever materialising the score
      matrix. **Read §3.1 and Algorithm 1** — the online softmax renormalisation is the whole trick
      and it is fifteen lines.
    - **Ainslie et al. 2023, "GQA: Training Generalized Multi-Query Transformer Models from
      Multi-Head Checkpoints".** *Takeaway:* sharing K/V across groups of query heads cuts the KV
      cache by the group factor with near-zero quality loss, and you can *uptrain* an existing MHA
      checkpoint into GQA with 5% of original compute. **Read Figure 2** for the quality-vs-speed
      frontier.
    - **Elhage et al. 2021, "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits".** *Takeaway:*
      attention heads are best understood as reading from and writing to a shared residual stream
      via low-rank `Q Kᵀ` and `O V` matrices, which makes composed multi-head behaviour analysable.
      **Read the "Attention Heads are Independent and Additive" and "induction heads" sections** —
      induction heads are the cleanest example anywhere of a learned algorithm inside a network.
    - **Xiao et al. 2023, "Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks".**
      *Takeaway:* keeping the first few tokens' KV entries pinned lets a sliding-window cache run to
      millions of tokens without perplexity collapse. **Read Figure 1** — the perplexity explosion
      when sinks are evicted is the whole paper.

    ### Stretch challenge

    Take a small pretrained model you can run locally (`gpt2` or `Qwen2.5-0.5B`), extract the
    attention weights for all heads on 200 sentences, and try to *name* what three heads do. Two
    concrete targets: find a **previous-token head** (attends from position `t` to `t−1` with weight
    &gt;0.8 across most inputs) and an **induction head** (given a repeated pattern `A B … A`,
    attends from the second `A` to the token after the first `A`). Then the open part: propose and
    test a quantitative criterion that separates "this head does a nameable thing" from "this head
    is diffuse", apply it across all heads and layers, and report what fraction of heads pass. There
    is no accepted answer; defending your criterion is the exercise. If you find that entropy alone
    is not enough — that is the discovery.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Validation loss suspiciously low, generation is nonsense | Causal mask off by one (`diagonal=0`) or inverted | The perturbation test: change the last token, assert earlier outputs are unchanged |
| Attention weights do not sum to 1 | Mask applied after softmax instead of as `−inf` before | `scores.masked_fill(mask, float("-inf"))` then `softmax` |
| Model trains but loss plateaus around random | Missing the `/ √d_k` scaling | Print logit std before softmax — it should be ≈1, not ≈11 |
| OOM at a sequence length that "should fit" | Materialising `(B,H,T,T)` | `F.scaled_dot_product_attention`; capacity-plan on `T²` |
| 3× slower after an unrelated change | Fell off the flash kernel (dtype, head_dim, or mask shape) | Fixed-shape timing benchmark in CI with a 20% regression alarm |
| Batched output differs from unbatched | Padding interacting with `is_causal=True` | Left-pad plus an explicit mask, or use packed variable-length sequences |
| One head's weights are all on token 0 | Attention sink — normal behaviour, not a bug | Do not "fix" it; do pin token 0 if you evict from the KV cache |
| Head merge produces garbage | `transpose(1,2)` without `.contiguous()` before `.view()` | Use `.contiguous().view(...)` or `.reshape(...)` |
