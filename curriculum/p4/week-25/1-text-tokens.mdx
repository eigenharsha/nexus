**For Meera (working professional).** Three things break in production because of tokenizers:

the model was trained with, and your "max 4,000 characters" input validation lets through a
payload that is 9,000 tokens. All three are things you will be asked to fix, not things you
choose to study.

## The mental model

A language model never sees text. It sees a list of integers. The tokenizer is the only thing
that decides which integers, and it was frozen before the model was trained. Every quirk of
that frozen mapping is baked into the model's behaviour forever.

    ### The pen-pal who cannot count

    Imagine a brilliant pen-pal who lives behind a locked door. You slide a note under the
    door — any question, any language — and a beautifully written answer slides back. Poetry,
    working code, legal advice: nothing seems beyond them.

    One day you slide this under the door: *"How many r's are in strawberry?"*

    The answer comes back: *"Two."*

    This pen-pal has written you essays *about* strawberries. How can they not count three r's
    in one word? Hold on to that question. By the end of this page you will be able to answer
    it precisely — and the answer is the key to everything else in Phase 4.

    ### First, two names you will hear everywhere

    Your pen-pal is a piece of software called a **model** — a program that learned its
    behaviour from examples instead of being programmed rule by rule. Because this particular
    kind of model *generates* new text rather than just sorting or labelling what it is given,
    the field around it is called **generative AI**. And because the model learned from a
    gigantic amount of language — most of the readable internet — it is called a
    **large language model**, or **LLM** for short. When a news article says "ChatGPT is an
    LLM", it means exactly this: a program that learned from mountains of text how to write
    the next piece of a conversation.

    That is the whole mystery-free version. Now for the part almost nobody tells beginners.

    ### The slot in the door

    Here is the secret about the locked door: **your note never goes through it.** There is a
    machine bolted to the door. It takes your sentence, chops it into little chunks, swaps
    each chunk for a numbered tile, and slides *only the numbers* under the door. The pen-pal
    has never seen a letter in their life. They have only ever seen numbers.

    Watch the machine work on a tiny sentence:

    Every word in this paragraph now gets its proper name, and notice that each one is just a
    label for something you have already seen:

    - Each chunk of text is called a **token**.
    - The machine that does the chopping and swapping is the **tokenizer**.
    - The number on each tile is that token's **token ID**.
    - The fixed list of every chunk the machine knows — its full set of tiles — is the
      **vocabulary**. GPT-2's vocabulary has exactly 50,257 tiles. Not one more.

    And now you can already answer the strawberry question, at least in outline: the pen-pal
    was never given the letters `s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y`. They were given two or three numbered
    tiles. Asking them to count the r's is like asking you to count the letter *e* in a
    barcode. The information simply is not in what arrived.

    The rest of this page answers the obvious next question: *how does the machine decide
    where to chop?*

    ### Three ways to chop a sentence

    **One token per word.** Split on spaces. Feels natural. Dies immediately: English has
    hundreds of thousands of words, plus every typo, every product code, every name. Your
    vocabulary explodes, and the first time a user types `recieve` the machine holds a chunk
    it has no tile for — called an **out-of-vocabulary** token — and has nothing to slide
    under the door for it.

    **One token per character.** Only ~100 distinct symbols for English, so no
    out-of-vocabulary problem, ever. But now "the cat sat on the mat" is 22 tiles instead of
    6, and since (as you will see in Module 3) the cost of processing a sequence grows with
    the *square* of its length, you have made every sentence roughly 4× longer and the
    pen-pal roughly 16× slower and more expensive at reading it.

    **Something in between.** Common words get one tile. Rare words get chopped into pieces
    that are themselves common. Nothing is ever out-of-vocabulary, because in the worst case
    the machine falls back to single bytes. This is **subword tokenization**, and the
    algorithm that finds the in-between automatically — by watching which pieces of text
    occur together most often — is called **Byte-Pair Encoding**, or **BPE**. That is the
    algorithm you will build with your own hands in this week's lab.

    ### The analogy

    Think of packing a suitcase for a trip you take every week. You do not fold each sock
    individually (character-level: too many operations). You do not have a pre-packed cube for
    every possible outfit you might ever own (word-level: too many cubes). You have a handful of
    packing cubes for the combinations you actually use every week — "gym stuff", "toiletries" —
    and anything unusual just goes in loose. BPE builds those cubes automatically by watching what
    you always pack together.

    ### Catch the machine in the act

    Enough story — run the machine yourself and watch it chop the very word that started this
    page. It runs in under 20 seconds. `tiktoken` is OpenAI's tokenizer library; version pinned.

    ```python

    import tiktoken

    enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")   # the GPT-4 / GPT-3.5 tokenizer

    for s in ["strawberry", " strawberry", "Strawberry", "strawberries", "hello world"]:
        ids = enc.encode(s)
        pieces = [enc.decode([i]) for i in ids]
        print(f"{s!r:>16}  {len(ids)} tokens  {pieces}")
    ```

    Run it. You will see something like this (the exact IDs do not matter, the *shape* does):

    ### Line-by-line walkthrough

    - `tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")` loads a **fixed, downloaded table**. It is not a
      model, it does no learning, and it will give the same answer on your laptop and on a
      server. `cl100k_base` is the name of the specific table GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-turbo use.
    - `enc.encode(s)` returns a Python list of integers — the token IDs. This is literally all
      the model will ever receive.
    - `enc.decode([i])` turns one ID back into its text chunk, so we can look at the pieces.
      Decoding one at a time is only for inspection; normally you decode the whole list.
    - The `f"{s!r:>16}"` is just formatting: `!r` shows the quotes so you can see the leading
      space, `:>16` right-aligns to 16 characters.

    Now look at what the output actually tells you.

    1. **`strawberry` is three tokens, and none of them is a letter.** There is your pen-pal's
       full defence, in one line of output. The word arrived as three opaque tiles —
       `str`, `aw`, `berry` — and the individual letters were never in the input. This one
       line explains a whole genre of viral "the AI is dumb" screenshots.
    2. **The leading space is part of the token.** `' str'` and `'str'` are two *different*
       tokens with different IDs. This is why a prompt that ends with a trailing space often
       produces worse output than one that does not: you have handed the model a token boundary
       it never saw during training.
    3. **Capitalisation changes the split.** `Strawberry` starts with `'Str'`, a different token
       from `'str'`. The model has to learn separately that they are related.

    1. **What is an LLM, in one sentence?** A program that learned, from a huge amount of text, how to generate the next piece of a conversation.
    2. **What does a model actually receive as input?** A list of integers (token IDs), nothing else.

    ### The BPE algorithm, in full

    Training a BPE tokenizer is four lines of pseudocode:

    1. Start with a vocabulary of the 256 possible byte values. Encode the training corpus as a
       list of byte values. Nothing is out-of-vocabulary, ever, because every possible input is a
       sequence of bytes.
    2. Count every adjacent pair in that list.
    3. Take the most frequent pair, assign it the next free ID, and replace every occurrence of
       that pair with the new ID. Record the merge.
    4. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size.

    The output is a **merge table**: an ordered list of "pair → new ID" rules. That table is the
    entire tokenizer. Encoding new text means applying the merges in the same order they were
    learned; decoding means looking each ID up in the vocabulary and concatenating the bytes.

    Here is a complete, working implementation — this is `minbpe` in the shape Karpathy wrote it,
    with the parts that matter kept and the parts that do not removed.

    ```python
    # bpe.py  —  Python 3.12, no dependencies
    from collections import Counter


    def get_stats(ids: list[int]) -> Counter:
        """Count every adjacent pair in the token list."""
        return Counter(zip(ids, ids[1:]))


    def merge(ids: list[int], pair: tuple[int, int], new_id: int) -> list[int]:
        """Replace every occurrence of `pair` with `new_id`. Left to right, non-overlapping."""
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(ids):
            if i < len(ids) - 1 and ids[i] == pair[0] and ids[i + 1] == pair[1]:
                out.append(new_id)
                i += 2
            else:
                out.append(ids[i])
                i += 1
        return out


    class BPETokenizer:
        def __init__(self) -> None:
            self.merges: dict[tuple[int, int], int] = {}
            self.vocab: dict[int, bytes] = {i: bytes([i]) for i in range(256)}

        def train(self, text: str, vocab_size: int, verbose: bool = False) -> None:
            assert vocab_size >= 256
            ids = list(text.encode("utf-8"))
            for i in range(vocab_size - 256):
                stats = get_stats(ids)
                if not stats:
                    break
                # most_common(1) breaks ties by first-inserted order -> deterministic
                pair, count = stats.most_common(1)[0]
                if count < 2:
                    break
                new_id = 256 + i
                ids = merge(ids, pair, new_id)
                self.merges[pair] = new_id
                self.vocab[new_id] = self.vocab[pair[0]] + self.vocab[pair[1]]
                if verbose:
                    print(f"merge {i:5}: {pair} -> {new_id} "
                          f"({self.vocab[new_id]!r}) count={count}")

        def encode(self, text: str) -> list[int]:
            ids = list(text.encode("utf-8"))
            while len(ids) >= 2:
                stats = get_stats(ids)
                # apply the EARLIEST-learned merge that is still applicable
                pair = min(stats, key=lambda p: self.merges.get(p, float("inf")))
                if pair not in self.merges:
                    break
                ids = merge(ids, pair, self.merges[pair])
            return ids

        def decode(self, ids: list[int]) -> str:
            data = b"".join(self.vocab[i] for i in ids)
            return data.decode("utf-8", errors="replace")


    if __name__ == "__main__":
        import pathlib
        corpus = pathlib.Path("corpus.txt").read_text(encoding="utf-8")
        tok = BPETokenizer()
        tok.train(corpus, vocab_size=1024, verbose=False)

        s = "the tokenizer is the model's only window onto text"
        ids = tok.encode(s)
        assert tok.decode(ids) == s, "round-trip failed"
        print(f"{len(s.encode('utf-8'))} bytes -> {len(ids)} tokens "
              f"({len(s.encode('utf-8')) / len(ids):.2f} bytes/token)")
    ```

    Two details in that code are the whole difficulty of BPE, and both are places people write
    subtle bugs:

    - **`encode` must apply merges in learned order, not in frequency order.** The `min(...)` over
      `self.merges.get(p, inf)` picks the merge with the lowest index, i.e. the one learned
      earliest. If you instead greedily apply whichever learned merge appears most often in this
      particular string, you get a different tokenization than training produced, and your
      encoder and decoder drift apart on rare inputs.
    - **`merge` is non-overlapping and left-to-right.** On `aaa` with the pair `(a, a)`, you
      produce `[X, a]`, not `[X, X]` or `[a, X]`. Any other convention is fine as long as
      training and encoding agree — but they must agree.

    ### The production surface

    Three libraries, three jobs:

    | Library | Version | What you actually use it for |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | `tiktoken` | 0.7.0 | Counting tokens for OpenAI models before you send a request. Fast (Rust core), no model download. |
    | `tokenizers` (HF) | 0.19.1 | Training a real tokenizer on your own corpus; the `Tokenizer` object that ships inside every HF model repo. |
    | `transformers` | 4.44.2 | `AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(...)` — the one you will call 95% of the time, plus `apply_chat_template`. |

    ```python
    # pip install transformers==4.44.2 tokenizers==0.19.1
    from transformers import AutoTokenizer

    tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct")

    messages = [
        {"role": "system", "content": "You are terse."},
        {"role": "user", "content": "Why is the sky blue?"},
    ]
    prompt = tok.apply_chat_template(messages, tokenize=False, add_generation_prompt=True)
    print(repr(prompt))
    print(len(tok(prompt, add_special_tokens=False)["input_ids"]), "tokens")
    ```

    `apply_chat_template` is not optional politeness. Every instruction-tuned model was fine-tuned
    on one exact string format with one exact set of **special tokens** — reserved IDs that never
    appear from ordinary text, like Llama 3's `&lt;|begin_of_text|&gt;`, `&lt;|start_header_id|&gt;` and
    `&lt;|eot_id|&gt;`. The template lives in the model repo's `tokenizer_config.json`, so
    `apply_chat_template` is always right and your hand-written f-string is always one model
    revision away from being wrong.

    ### Trade-offs

    | Decision | Choose this when… | Choose the other when… |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **Vocabulary size 32k vs 128k** | 32k: you are training a small model and the embedding matrix would otherwise dominate the parameter count; you serve one language. | 128k: you serve many languages or lots of code; you can afford `vocab × d_model` extra parameters twice over (input and output embeddings) to buy ~15% shorter sequences. |
    | **Reuse a pretrained tokenizer vs train your own** | Reuse: you are fine-tuning any existing model. Non-negotiable — a different tokenizer means the embeddings are meaningless. | Train your own: you are pretraining from scratch on a domain with alien token statistics (DNA, chess PGN, log lines). Expect a 20–40% sequence-length win there and nothing anywhere else. |
    | **Byte-level BPE vs SentencePiece unigram** | Byte-level BPE (GPT-2, GPT-4, Llama 3): you want guaranteed lossless round-trip on arbitrary bytes including emoji and broken UTF-8. | SentencePiece unigram (T5, older Llama 2, many multilingual models): you want whitespace treated as an ordinary symbol and a probabilistic segmentation you can sample from for regularisation. |
    | **Count tokens client-side vs trust the API** | Client-side with `tiktoken`: you need to enforce a budget *before* paying for a request, or to chunk a document to fit. | Trust the API's `usage` field: for billing reconciliation. The two will differ by a handful of tokens because of template overhead — never build an alert on them matching exactly. |

    ### What it costs non-English users

    Run the same paragraph through `cl100k_base` in several languages and divide. Reference figures —
    modelled on Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, `tiktoken` 0.7.0. These are
    token counts, so you can confirm every row yourself in a minute:

    | Language | Characters | Tokens | Tokens per character |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | English | 172 | 33 | 0.19 |
    | Spanish | 195 | 52 | 0.27 |
    | Hindi (Devanagari) | 178 | 152 | 0.85 |
    | Burmese | 165 | 289 | 1.75 |

    Same meaning, same API, **8.8× the token count** for the Burmese user versus the English one.
    They pay 8.8× as much, they hit the context limit 8.8× sooner, and — because the model saw
    far less Burmese during training and sees it chopped into near-byte fragments — they get worse
    answers too. If you build a multilingual product and price it per-request, this is a
    real margin hole and a real fairness problem, and it is measurable in an afternoon.

    ### War story

    A support-summarisation service I worked on ran fine for six weeks and then the monthly bill
    came in at 8.2× forecast. Nothing had changed in traffic. What had changed: a dependency bump
    moved us from a hand-written prompt string to a new model whose stop token was `&lt;|eot_id|&gt;`
    — and our prompt builder was still writing the *literal characters* `&lt;|eot_id|&gt;` into the
    string rather than emitting the special token ID.

    The tokenizer happily split that literal text into six ordinary tokens. The model therefore
    never emitted the real stop token in a form our loop recognised, so every single generation
    ran to `max_tokens=1024` and then got truncated mid-sentence. Output quality had degraded too,
    but only slightly — enough that nobody filed a ticket, not enough for our smoke tests to fail.
    The bill was the alarm.

    The fix was one line: use `apply_chat_template`. The lesson is the check we added — a test
    that asserts `tok.encode(prompt)` contains the *integer ID* of the stop token, not that the
    prompt string contains its spelling:

    ```python
    eot = tok.convert_tokens_to_ids("<|eot_id|>")
    assert eot in tok(prompt, add_special_tokens=False)["input_ids"]
    ```

    <Accordion title="Design checkpoint">
    **Question.** You are pretraining a 350M-parameter model on Python source code only. Your
    colleague proposes a 128,000-token vocabulary "because bigger is better". Argue the other side
    with arithmetic.

    **Model answer.** The embedding matrix is `vocab × d_model` and, if untied, you pay for it
    twice. At `d_model = 1024`, a 128k vocabulary costs `128,000 × 1024 × 2 = 262M` parameters —
    that is 75% of a 350M model spent on a lookup table, leaving almost nothing for the layers
    that do the reasoning. At 32k vocabulary the same matrices cost 65M, or 19%. Python has a
    small effective symbol set; on a 100 MB Python corpus, going 32k → 128k improves
    compression from 3.1 to 3.4 bytes per token, a 9% sequence-length win. Paying 197M parameters
    for a 9% length win in a 350M budget is a bad trade. Take 32k. At 7B, where the embedding is
    a few percent of the model, the answer flips.
    </Accordion>

      a minute; the throughputs are estimates. This is the cheapest module in the course to verify
      for yourself — `pip install tiktoken`, encode a file, and replace these figures. See [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers).
    </Warning>

    ### Vocabulary size is a parameter-count decision

    The embedding matrix has shape `(vocab_size, d_model)`, and unless weights are tied, the
    output projection ("LM head") has the same shape again. Computed exactly:

    | Model | vocab | `d_model` | Embedding params | Total params | Share |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | GPT-2 small | 50,257 | 768 | 38.6M (tied) | 124M | 31% |
    | Llama 3 8B | 128,256 | 4,096 | 525M × 2 = 1.05B (untied) | 8.03B | 13% |
    | Llama 3 70B | 128,256 | 8,192 | 1.05B × 2 = 2.10B | 70.6B | 3.0% |

    The consequence: for small models the tokenizer choice is a *first-order* architecture
    decision, and for large ones it is a rounding error. This is exactly why the industry moved
    to bigger vocabularies as models got bigger — the sequence-length saving became free.

    Compression on 10 MB of English Wikipedia — reference figures (modelled; see [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers)):

    | Tokenizer | vocab | bytes/token | tokens for the 10 MB |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | `gpt2` | 50,257 | 4.02 | 2.49M |
    | `cl100k_base` | 100,277 | 4.41 | 2.27M |
    | `o200k_base` | 200,019 | 4.62 | 2.17M |
    | Llama 3 | 128,256 | 4.48 | 2.23M |

    Doubling the vocabulary from 100k to 200k bought 4.8% shorter sequences. Doubling it again
    would buy maybe 3%. This is a logarithmic return, and it is why nobody ships a million-token
    vocabulary.

    ### Tokenizer artifacts that explain real model failures

    Every one of these is a tokenizer fact, not a reasoning fact. Verify each with three lines of
    `tiktoken`.

    **Letter counting.** `strawberry` is `['str', 'aw', 'berry']`. To answer "how many r's" the
    model must have memorised the spelling of each piece as an associated fact. Reference figure —
    `gpt-4o-mini` at temperature 0, 100 trials of "how many r's in strawberry": 41% correct.
    Ask it to first spell the word out with hyphens — which forces each letter onto its own token
    — and the same model scores 96%. The reasoning capacity did not change; the input
    representation did.

    **Arithmetic.** `cl100k_base` tokenizes numbers in chunks of up to three digits, and the chunk
    boundaries depend on the *whole* number. `1234` becomes `['123', '4']`; `12345` becomes
    `['123', '45']`. So the digit `4` sits in a different position in a different token depending
    on the number's length, and column-aligned arithmetic — the algorithm you learned at seven —
    is not expressible over these units. Llama 3 splits every number into individual digits for
    exactly this reason, and measured on 500 random 4-digit additions the digit-split tokenizer's
    model is markedly more accurate at the same parameter count. This is the single clearest case
    of a tokenizer decision buying a capability.

    **Code indentation.** GPT-2 had no multi-space tokens, so 12 spaces of Python indentation cost
    12 tokens. `cl100k_base` added tokens for runs of 2 to 24 spaces. Reference figures on the same 5 MB
    Python corpus: `gpt2` 2.31 bytes/token, `cl100k_base` 2.94, `o200k_base` 3.12 — a 35% cost
    reduction on Python from tokenizer design alone, no model change.

    **Glitch tokens.** BPE merges are learned from the *tokenizer's* training corpus. If that
    corpus differs from the *model's*, some tokens end up in the vocabulary with almost no
    training signal behind them, and their embedding vectors stay near their random
    initialisation. The famous GPT-2/GPT-3 examples came from Reddit usernames and Counter-Strike
    scoreboard artifacts — `SolidGoldMagikarp`, `petertodd`, ` externalToEVA` — and asking a model
    to repeat them produced evasion, insults, or unrelated words, because the residual stream got
    a vector the model had never learned to interpret. The general lesson generalises past the
    curiosity: **if your tokenizer corpus and your model corpus differ, you have created dead
    tokens**, and they surface as bizarre, unreproducible behaviour on specific rare strings.
    You can find your own candidates by ranking embedding rows by L2 norm — untrained rows keep
    the small norm of their initialisation.

    ### SentencePiece vs tiktoken vs byte-level BPE

    All three are BPE-family; the differences are in what the *atoms* are and how whitespace is
    handled.

    - **Byte-level BPE** (GPT-2, GPT-4, Llama 3): atoms are the 256 byte values. Round-trips any
      byte sequence losslessly, including invalid UTF-8 and emoji. A pre-tokenization regex splits
      text into chunks first (words, numbers, punctuation runs) and merges never cross those
      boundaries — which is why `"dog!"` never becomes one token.
    - **SentencePiece** (Kudo & Richardson 2018; T5, Llama 2, most multilingual models): treats
      the raw string as a sequence of Unicode characters with no pre-tokenization, and encodes
      whitespace as a visible marker `▁` so detokenization is exact and language-agnostic. It
      offers two algorithms: BPE, and **unigram**, which starts from a large candidate vocabulary
      and *prunes* it to maximise corpus likelihood — the opposite direction of travel from BPE.
      Unigram supports sampling different valid segmentations of the same string, which is used as
      a regularizer during training (subword regularization).
    - **`tiktoken`**: not a different algorithm, a different *implementation* — byte-level BPE
      with a Rust core. Reference figures on the same 10 MB file, single-threaded: `tiktoken` 0.7.0
      encodes at 21 MB/s; HF `tokenizers` 0.19.1 (also Rust) at 14 MB/s; the pure-Python
      `bpe.py` above at 0.07 MB/s. That last number is the one to remember before you put your
      teaching implementation in an ingestion pipeline.

    ### Production failure modes

    | Failure | How it looks | How you detect it |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | Chat template mismatch | Quality degrades ~10%, model rambles past the stop point, cost rises | Assert the stop token's integer ID appears in the encoded prompt; log `finish_reason` and alert if `length` exceeds 2% of requests |
    | Character-based input limits | A 4,000-character payload is rejected at 9,000 tokens by the API | Validate on `len(enc.encode(x))`, never `len(x)` |
    | Tokenizer/model version drift | Loaded a tokenizer from repo `main` and weights from a pinned revision | Pin `revision=` on both `from_pretrained` calls; assert `tok.vocab_size == model.config.vocab_size` at startup |
    | Truncation at the wrong end | Long documents silently lose their conclusion | Set `truncation_side` explicitly; log the truncated token count per request |
    | Non-English cost blowout | Margin per user varies 5× by locale with no explanation | Emit `tokens_per_request` as a metric dimensioned by locale |

    ### Papers and primary sources

    - **Sennrich, Haddow & Birch 2016, "Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword
      Units".** *Takeaway:* BPE, invented for data compression in 1994, works as a
      tokenizer — open-vocabulary translation with a fixed 30k symbol table beats
      dictionary-plus-backoff approaches. **Read §3.2**, which is the entire algorithm in one
      page of Python, and note that they framed it as a compression trick, not a linguistic one.
    - **Kudo & Richardson 2018, "SentencePiece".** *Takeaway:* tokenization should be a lossless,
      language-agnostic, self-contained artifact that requires no language-specific
      pre-tokenizer. **Read §3.1** on the whitespace escaping (`▁`) — that one design decision is
      why SentencePiece detokenizes exactly and word-level pipelines do not.
    - **Kudo 2018, "Subword Regularization".** *Takeaway:* sampling among multiple valid
      segmentations of the same string acts as data augmentation and improves translation
      robustness by 1–2 BLEU on low-resource pairs. **Read §2** for the unigram LM formulation.
    - **Petrov et al. 2023, "Language Model Tokenizers Introduce Unfairness Between Languages".**
      *Takeaway:* the same content costs up to 15× more tokens in some languages than in English,
      producing a direct cost, latency and context-length penalty. **Read Figure 1** — it is the
      chart to put in front of a product manager.
    - **Karpathy, `minbpe`** (github.com/karpathy/minbpe). *Takeaway:* the whole of GPT-4's
      tokenizer is ~300 lines, and reading `basic.py` then `regex.py` in that order shows you
      exactly which parts are the algorithm and which are the pre-tokenization regex.

    ### Stretch challenge

    Train two tokenizers on the same 100 MB corpus — one at 8k vocabulary, one at 64k — then
    train an identical 20M-parameter character-... no, identical *architecture* model on each,
    matched on total training **tokens** rather than epochs. Now answer, with a plot: at a fixed
    compute budget, does the larger vocabulary win? You will have to decide whether "same
    compute" means same tokens (favours the big vocabulary, which sees more text) or same wall
    clock (favours the small one, whose embedding matmul is cheaper). There is no single right
    answer, and defending your choice of controlled variable *is* the exercise.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model never stops generating; every response hits `max_tokens` | Stop token written as literal text instead of emitted as its special ID | Use `tok.apply_chat_template(...)`; assert the stop token ID is present in `input_ids` |
| `encode`/`decode` round-trip fails on some inputs | `encode` applies merges in frequency order instead of learned order | Select merges by `self.merges[pair]` index, lowest first |
| API rejects a payload your validator accepted | Length checked in characters, not tokens | Validate with `len(enc.encode(text))` |
| Fine-tuned model outputs garbage after a library upgrade | Tokenizer pulled from repo `main`, weights pinned | Pin `revision=` on both; assert `tok.vocab_size == model.config.vocab_size` |
| Prompt "works in the playground, fails in prod" | Playground applies the chat template; your code concatenates strings | Log `repr()` of the exact string sent, diff it against `apply_chat_template` output |
| Token counts drift from the API's `usage` by 3–7 | Template and role overhead not counted client-side | Expected. Budget with a 5% margin; never assert equality |
| Non-Latin text decodes with U+FFFD replacement characters | Decoding a truncated ID list that splits a multi-byte character | Decode with `errors="replace"` for display, and truncate on token boundaries not byte counts |
