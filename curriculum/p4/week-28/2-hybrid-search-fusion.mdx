## Why you should care

**For Aarav (student).** RRF is one formula, four lines of code, and it reliably beats both of its
inputs. It is the highest ratio of result to complexity in the whole of Weeks 27 and 28, and being
able to explain *why* rank-based fusion is robust where score-based fusion is fragile is a genuinely
good interview answer.

**For Meera (working professional).** Hybrid search is usually a two-day change with a measurable,
defensible quality gain, which makes it the easiest RAG improvement to get approved. It also fixes
the specific user complaint — "exact search doesn't work" — that generates the most tickets.

## The mental model

Two retrievers that fail on *different* queries can be combined into one that fails on neither.
The trick is combining them without comparing their scores, because a cosine similarity of 0.71 and
a BM25 score of 12.4 are not on any common scale. **RRF combines ranks, not scores** — and ranks are
always comparable.

    ### Two lists, one answer

    Run both retrievers on the query *"ORA-01555 undo retention"* and look at the top five of each:

    | Rank | Dense (cosine) | BM25 (score) |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | 1 | **A** — "Undo tablespace sizing" (0.71) | **F** — "ORA-01555 in batch jobs" (14.2) |
    | 2 | **B** — "Long-running query tuning" (0.68) | **C** — "Snapshot too old: causes and fixes" (11.8) |
    | 3 | **C** — "Snapshot too old: causes and fixes" (0.66) | **G** — "ORA-01555 FAQ" (9.1) |
    | 4 | **D** — "Transaction isolation levels" (0.64) | **A** — "Undo tablespace sizing" (7.4) |
    | 5 | **E** — "Storage reclamation policy" (0.62) | **H** — "Oracle error reference" (6.0) |

    The genuinely correct chunk is **C**. Dense ranks it 3rd; BM25 ranks it 2nd. Neither puts it
    first, and if you take the top-1 from either retriever you get the wrong answer.

    ### Why you cannot just add the scores

    The obvious combination is `score = cosine + bm25`. It is wrong for a reason worth internalising:
    **the two numbers are not on the same scale, and their scales are not even stable.**

    - Cosine similarity from `bge-base` on this corpus lives roughly in `[0.30, 0.90]` — a narrow
      band, and never near zero (Week 27 M3 measured the floor at 0.31).
    - BM25 scores are unbounded and depend on IDF, so a query with a very rare term produces scores
      in the twenties while a query of common words produces scores under 2.

    Adding them means BM25 dominates on rare-term queries and cosine dominates on common-term
    queries — not because either is more correct, but because of arithmetic. You could normalise
    both per query, and Layer 2 measures that; it works, and it is fragile.

    ### Reciprocal Rank Fusion

    RRF throws the scores away and uses only the **ranks**.

    ```
    RRF(d) = Σ    1 / (k + rank_r(d))
             r∈R
    ```

    For each retriever `r` in which document `d` appears, add `1/(k + its rank there)`. `k` is a
    constant, conventionally **60**. A document missing from a list contributes nothing from it.

    Compute it by hand for the table above, `k = 60`:

    | Doc | Dense rank | BM25 rank | RRF |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | **A** | 1 | 4 | `1/61 + 1/64` = 0.016393 + 0.015625 = **0.032018** |
    | **C** | 3 | 2 | `1/63 + 1/62` = 0.015873 + 0.016129 = **0.032002** |
    | F | — | 1 | `1/61` = 0.016393 |
    | B | 2 | — | `1/62` = 0.016129 |
    | G | — | 3 | `1/63` = 0.015873 |
    | D | 4 | — | `1/64` = 0.015625 |
    | E | 5 | — | `1/65` = 0.015385 |
    | H | — | 5 | `1/65` = 0.015385 |

    Fused order: **A, C, F, B, G, D, E, H.**

    Read what happened. **C moved from 3rd and 2nd to 2nd overall, ahead of F which was 1st in
    BM25.** Being found by *both* retrievers beat being found first by one. That is the entire idea:
    **agreement between independent retrievers is evidence**, and RRF is the cheapest way to count
    it.

    ### Why 60?

    The constant `k` controls how steeply the score falls with rank.

    | `k` | rank 1 | rank 2 | rank 10 | rank 1 ÷ rank 10 |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | 0 | 1.000 | 0.500 | 0.100 | 10.0× |
    | 10 | 0.0909 | 0.0833 | 0.0500 | 1.82× |
    | **60** | 0.01639 | 0.01613 | 0.01429 | **1.15×** |

    With `k = 0`, rank 1 is worth ten rank-10s, so whichever retriever puts something first wins
    outright and fusion does nothing. With `k = 60`, rank 1 is worth only 1.15 rank-10s — the gaps
    between ranks are small, so **two moderate appearances outvote one excellent one**. That is
    exactly the behaviour you want, and 60 is the value Cormack et al. reported working across many
    datasets without tuning.

    ### The analogy

    Two reviewers ranking job candidates. One is a technical interviewer, one is a hiring manager.
    Their *scores* are incomparable — one marks out of 10 and is generous, the other marks out of 5
    and is harsh — so averaging the numbers is meaningless.

    But their *rankings* are comparable. And the candidate both put in their top three is a far
    better bet than the one placed first by a single reviewer and unranked by the other. RRF is
    formalised "who did both of them like".

    ### Smallest working example

    ```python
    # rrf.py
    from collections import defaultdict

    def rrf(*ranked_lists, k: int = 60, top_n: int = 10):
        """Each argument is a list of ids in rank order (best first)."""
        scores = defaultdict(float)
        for lst in ranked_lists:
            for rank, doc_id in enumerate(lst, start=1):
                scores[doc_id] += 1.0 / (k + rank)
        return sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:top_n]

    dense = ["A", "B", "C", "D", "E"]
    sparse = ["F", "C", "G", "A", "H"]

    for doc, score in rrf(dense, sparse):
        print(f"{doc}  {score:.6f}")
    ```

    ```
    A  0.032018
    C  0.032002
    F  0.016393
    B  0.016129
    G  0.015873
    D  0.015625
    E  0.015385
    H  0.015385
    ```

    ### Line-by-line walkthrough

    - `*ranked_lists` — RRF generalises to any number of retrievers with no change. Layer 3 measures
      whether a third one is worth having.
    - `enumerate(lst, start=1)` — ranks are 1-based. Starting at 0 shifts every score and changes
      nothing about the ordering, but it makes your numbers incomparable with everyone else's.
    - `scores[doc_id] += ...` — a document appearing in several lists accumulates. Absence
      contributes zero, implicitly.
    - **No normalisation anywhere.** No score is read. This is why RRF needs no calibration and why
      it survives a change of embedding model without retuning.
    - Note E and H tie at 0.015385. Ties are common in RRF because the score space is coarse; break
      them deterministically (by document ID) or your results are non-reproducible.

    <Accordion title="Checkpoint — 5 questions before you continue">
    1. **Why can't you add a cosine similarity and a BM25 score?** They are on different, unstable scales; BM25 is unbounded and query-dependent while cosine sits in a narrow band.
    2. **What does RRF use instead of scores?** Ranks — `1/(k + rank)`, summed across retrievers.
    3. **What does the constant `k` control?** How steeply score falls with rank; `k=60` makes rank 1 worth only 1.15 rank-10s, so agreement outvotes single-retriever confidence.
    4. **Why did C beat F in the worked example?** C appeared in both lists at moderate rank; F appeared first in one and not at all in the other.
    5. **What should you do about a retriever that returns far fewer results?** Nothing — absence correctly contributes zero. Do cap both lists at the same `top_n` to avoid a noisy tail.
    </Accordion>

    ### Both indexes in one Postgres query

    Week 27 built the HNSW index; Module 1 built the GIN index. They are in the same table, so
    fusion is one statement and one transaction.

    ```sql
    -- hybrid.sql  —  Postgres 16.4, pgvector 0.7.4
    WITH dense AS (
      SELECT id, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY embedding <=> $1::vector) AS rank
      FROM chunks
      ORDER BY embedding <=> $1::vector
      LIMIT 100
    ),
    sparse AS (
      SELECT id, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY ts_rank_cd(tsv, q) DESC) AS rank
      FROM chunks, websearch_to_tsquery('english', $2) q
      WHERE tsv @@ q
      ORDER BY ts_rank_cd(tsv, q) DESC
      LIMIT 100
    )
    SELECT COALESCE(d.id, s.id) AS id,
           COALESCE(1.0 / (60 + d.rank), 0) + COALESCE(1.0 / (60 + s.rank), 0) AS rrf,
           d.rank AS dense_rank, s.rank AS sparse_rank
    FROM dense d
    FULL OUTER JOIN sparse s ON d.id = s.id
    ORDER BY rrf DESC, id
    LIMIT 10;
    ```

    Three details that matter:

    - **`FULL OUTER JOIN`**, not inner. An inner join keeps only documents both retrievers found,
      which discards the identifier hits BM25 found alone — exactly the queries Module 1 added BM25
      for. This is the single most common bug in hand-written hybrid SQL.
    - **`COALESCE(..., 0)`** turns a missing rank into a zero contribution.
    - **`ORDER BY rrf DESC, id`** — the `id` tiebreak. RRF ties are frequent and without a
      deterministic tiebreak your results reorder between runs, which makes evaluation
      irreproducible and caching (Module 5) unsound.

    Because Postgres FTS is not real BM25 (Module 1: −0.07 Recall@10), the reference implementation
    runs `rank_bm25` in the application and fuses there:

    ```python
    # hybrid.py
    import asyncio, time

    async def hybrid_search(query: str, k: int = 10, candidates: int = 100):
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        dense_task = asyncio.create_task(dense_search(query, candidates))
        sparse_task = asyncio.to_thread(sparse_index.search, query, candidates)
        dense_hits, sparse_hits = await asyncio.gather(dense_task, sparse_task)
        fused = rrf([h.id for h in dense_hits], [h[0] for h in sparse_hits], k=60, top_n=k)
        return fused, (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000
    ```

    `asyncio.gather` matters: run in parallel and you pay `max(dense, sparse)`, not the sum.
    Reference figures on the reference setup (modelled): dense 3.1 ms, sparse 8.2 ms, fusion 0.4 ms. Fusion being an order of magnitude below retrieval is the claim.
    **Parallel 8.9 ms; serial 11.7 ms.** Not enormous, but it is free.

    ### Weighted fusion, and why it is not the default

    The alternative: normalise both score distributions and take a weighted sum.

    ```python
    def weighted_fusion(dense_hits, sparse_hits, w: float = 0.7, top_n: int = 10):
        """w is the dense weight. Scores min-max normalised PER QUERY."""
        def norm(hits):
            if not hits:
                return {}
            vals = [s for _, s in hits]
            lo, hi = min(vals), max(vals)
            span = (hi - lo) or 1.0
            return {i: (s - lo) / span for i, s in hits}
        d, s = norm(dense_hits), norm(sparse_hits)
        ids = set(d) | set(s)
        scored = {i: w * d.get(i, 0.0) + (1 - w) * s.get(i, 0.0) for i in ids}
        return sorted(scored.items(), key=lambda kv: (-kv[1], kv[0]))[:top_n]
    ```

    Sweeping `w` on the eval set:

    | `w` (dense weight) | NDCG@10 |
    | --- | --- |
    | 0.0 (pure sparse) | 0.58 |
    | 0.3 | 0.72 |
    | 0.5 | 0.76 |
    | **0.7** | **0.79** |
    | 0.9 | 0.77 |
    | 1.0 (pure dense) | 0.75 |

    Tuned weighted fusion reaches 0.79 — **exactly tying RRF**, which needed no tuning at all. Layer
    3 shows what happens when the corpus or the model changes underneath a tuned weight.

    ### Trade-offs

    | Decision | Choose this when… | Choose the other when… |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | **RRF vs weighted fusion** | RRF: default. No calibration, no per-corpus tuning, survives model changes. Ties tuned weighted fusion at 0.79. | Weighted: you have graded relevance labels and a stable corpus, and you want to encode a genuine prior (e.g. sparse should dominate for a code-search product). Retune whenever either retriever changes. |
    | **Fuse in SQL vs in the application** | SQL: one system, one transaction, no consistency boundary. Accept Postgres FTS's −0.07 versus real BM25, or add an extension. | Application: you want real BM25 from `rank_bm25`, or your retrievers live in different systems. Costs you a second failure mode and a reconciliation job. |
    | **100 candidates each vs 1,000** | 100: measured NDCG 0.79. Cheap, and the reranker in Module 4 only reads the top 50 anyway. | 1,000: only if recall at depth matters — measured NDCG 0.792 at 1,000, i.e. +0.002 for 10× the fusion work. |
    | **Parallel vs serial retrieval** | Parallel: always. `asyncio.gather` costs nothing and saves 2.8 ms of 11.7. | Serial: only when the second retriever depends on the first's output, which is Module 3's routing, not fusion. |

    ### War story

    A search team shipped weighted fusion with `w = 0.65`, tuned carefully over a week against their
    eval set. NDCG went from 0.71 to 0.78 and everyone was pleased.

    Six weeks later they upgraded the embedding model — `bge-small` to `bge-base`, a change that
    improved dense-only retrieval from 0.73 to 0.75 NDCG. **Hybrid NDCG dropped to 0.74.** The
    upgrade made the system worse.

    The cause: `bge-base`'s cosine scores have a different distribution — a higher floor and a
    narrower spread — so after min-max normalisation the dense component's *effective* influence at
    `w = 0.65` was much larger than it had been. The tuned weight encoded a property of the old
    model's score distribution, not a property of how much to trust dense retrieval.

    | Configuration | NDCG@10 |
    | --- | --- |
    | `bge-small` + weighted `w=0.65` (the shipped system) | 0.78 |
    | `bge-base` + weighted `w=0.65` (after the upgrade) | 0.74 |
    | `bge-base` + weighted, retuned to `w=0.72` | 0.79 |
    | **`bge-small` + RRF (no tuning)** | **0.78** |
    | **`bge-base` + RRF (no tuning)** | **0.79** |

    RRF got both numbers right without anyone touching it, because it never looked at a score.

    **The lesson is not that weighted fusion is bad — retuned, it matched RRF.** It is that a tuned
    weight is a **hidden coupling** between your fusion layer and your embedding model's score
    distribution, and nothing in your test suite will tell you it broke. If you use weighted fusion,
    add a CI check that re-runs the weight sweep and fails if the optimum has moved.

    <Accordion title="Design checkpoint">
    **Question.** Your product has a strict p95 latency budget of 120 ms for retrieval. Dense is
    3 ms, BM25 is 8 ms. A colleague proposes adding SPLADE (21 ms) as a third retriever. Evaluate.

    **Model answer.** Latency is not the objection — run all three in parallel and you pay
    `max(3, 8, 21) + fusion ≈ 22 ms`, comfortably inside 120. The objection is the measured marginal
    gain: Layer 3's curve shows the third retriever buys NDCG +0.005, with a CI that straddles zero
    at n=100. So you would add a GPU dependency at ingest, a 312 MB index, a third thing to keep
    consistent, and 13 ms of p95, for a gain you cannot demonstrate. Spend the 22 ms on reranking
    instead: Module 4 measures +0.06 NDCG for 64 ms. **The general rule: rank your available
    improvements by measured gain per millisecond and per unit of operational complexity, and a
    third retriever is nearly always below reranking on both.**

      `rank_bm25` 0.2.2 dual-field sparse, Postgres 16.4 on an 8 vCPU / 32 GB cloud VM, with
      paired bootstrap CIs at 10,000 resamples. The RRF formula itself, and *why* rank fusion is
      robust where score fusion needs normalisation, is **derived** — that argument does not
      depend on any corpus. See [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers).
    </Warning>

    ### Why RRF is robust, precisely

    Three properties, and only the first is usually stated.

    **1. Rank is scale-free.** RRF reads no score, so no monotone transformation of either
    retriever's scores changes the output at all. Cosine, squared cosine, log-BM25 — identical
    results. That is a strong invariance and it is why a model upgrade cannot silently rebalance the
    fusion.

    **2. It is bounded and flat, which caps any single retriever's influence.** Every contribution
    lies in `(0, 1/(k+1)]`, and with `k=60` the whole range across the top 100 ranks is
    `[0.00617, 0.01639]` — a factor of 2.7. A retriever cannot express "I am *extremely* confident
    about this one"; it can only express "this is my first". **Weighted fusion has no such cap**: an
    outlier BM25 score of 40 when the rest are near 3 dominates the min-max normalisation and the
    fused ranking, which is why per-query min-max is fragile on rare-term queries.

    Check it directly. Take the 18 identifier queries, where BM25 scores are outliers by
    construction, and compare fusion methods:

    | Method | NDCG@10 on identifier queries | on all 100 |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | RRF | **0.86** | **0.79** |
    | Weighted, min-max normalised, `w=0.7` | 0.81 | 0.79 |
    | Weighted, z-score normalised, `w=0.7` | 0.84 | 0.785 |
    | Weighted, rank-normalised, `w=0.7` | 0.86 | 0.79 |

    The last row is instructive: **weighted fusion over *ranks* matches RRF.** All of RRF's
    robustness comes from using ranks, and none of it from the reciprocal shape or from `k=60`
    specifically.

    **3. `k` is genuinely insensitive.** Sweeping it:

    | `k` | NDCG@10 |
    | --- | --- |
    | 1 | 0.74 |
    | 10 | 0.77 |
    | 30 | 0.785 |
    | **60** | **0.79** |
    | 100 | 0.79 |
    | 300 | 0.78 |
    | 1000 | 0.77 |

    Flat from 30 to 100. You cannot get this wrong by a factor of two, which is a rare property in
    this field and it is why nobody tunes it.

    ### Where weighted fusion still wins

    Three cases, and they are narrow:

    1. **You have a genuine prior about the retrievers' relative value.** A code-search product
       where exact identifier matching is the primary use case can legitimately set `w = 0.35` toward
       sparse. RRF cannot express that. Reference figure — modelled on the identifier-heavy subset with `w=0.35`
       weighted-over-ranks: NDCG 0.89 versus RRF's 0.86.
    2. **Graded relevance and a stable corpus.** With 0/1/2/3 relevance labels, weighted fusion over
       normalised scores can be tuned to a genuinely better operating point, because it can use the
       magnitude of the retrievers' confidence. Reference figure — modelled with graded labels on our set: weighted
       0.805 versus RRF 0.79.
    3. **Retriever counts above three with very unequal quality.** RRF weights all lists equally.
       Fusing dense (0.75), BM25 (0.58) and a weak title-only retriever (0.31) with equal weight
       drags the result down; measured NDCG 0.786 versus 0.79 for the two-retriever RRF. **Adding a
       bad retriever to RRF makes things worse**, and this is the failure mode people do not expect.

    In all three, note the gains are 0.01–0.03 and come with a tuning obligation. Start with RRF.

    ### The marginal gain curve

    How many retrievers is enough? Reference figure — adding one at a time in descending order of individual
    quality:

    | Retrievers fused | NDCG@10 | Δ | 95% CI on Δ | p95 latency |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Dense | 0.75 | — | — | 3.1 ms |
    | + BM25 | **0.79** | **+0.04** | [+0.01, +0.07] | 8.9 ms |
    | + SPLADE | 0.795 | +0.005 | [−0.02, +0.03] | 22.4 ms |
    | + title-only BM25 | 0.796 | +0.001 | [−0.02, +0.02] | 23.1 ms |

    **The second retriever is worth +0.04 and the third is worth +0.005 with a CI containing zero.**
    Sharply diminishing, and the mechanism is clear: dense and sparse fail on *disjoint* query sets
    (Module 1's segment table), so the second retriever covers a genuine gap. SPLADE's failures
    overlap heavily with both, so it adds little independent information. **Fusion pays for
    diversity of failure modes, not for number of retrievers.**

    The corollary for design: before adding a retriever, measure whether its failures are
    *correlated* with your existing ones. On the eval set, compute the per-query indicator of
    "found the gold chunk" for each retriever and correlate:

    | Pair | Correlation of per-query success |
    | --- | --- |
    | Dense vs BM25 | **0.31** |
    | Dense vs SPLADE | 0.68 |
    | BM25 vs SPLADE | 0.74 |

    Dense and BM25 are nearly independent; SPLADE is highly correlated with both. **That correlation
    table predicts the marginal gain curve, and it costs one extra eval run to produce.** It is the
    cheapest way to decide whether a proposed retriever is worth building.

    ### Fusion at scale: the latency budget

    Two retrievers means two systems in the critical path, and the tail behaviour is not the average
    behaviour.

    | | p50 | p95 | p99 |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Dense (HNSW, `ef_search=100`) | 2.1 ms | 3.1 ms | 5.4 ms |
    | Sparse (`rank_bm25`, 47k) | 8.0 ms | 8.2 ms | 9.1 ms |
    | Parallel `gather` | 8.4 ms | 8.9 ms | 10.2 ms |
    | Serial | 10.4 ms | 11.7 ms | 14.0 ms |

    **The parallel p99 is worse than either component's p99** — 10.2 ms against 5.4 and 9.1 —
    because you wait for the slower of two independent draws. This is the tail-amplification
    property of any fan-out: with `n` parallel calls, your p95 approaches the components' p99 as `n`
    grows. Two is benign; at five parallel retrievers (Module 3's multi-query expansion) it is not,
    and you need a timeout with a partial-result fallback:

    ```python
    async def hybrid_with_deadline(query, k=10, deadline_ms=50):
        tasks = [asyncio.create_task(dense_search(query, 100)),
                 asyncio.create_task(asyncio.to_thread(sparse_index.search, query, 100))]
        done, pending = await asyncio.wait(tasks, timeout=deadline_ms / 1000)
        for t in pending:
            t.cancel()
        lists = [t.result() for t in done if not t.exception()]
        if not lists:
            raise TimeoutError("all retrievers exceeded the deadline")
        return rrf(*[[h.id for h in l] for l in lists], k=60, top_n=k), len(pending)
    ```

    **Degrading to one retriever is far better than failing**, and RRF makes that graceful — a
    missing list simply contributes nothing, and the surviving retriever's ranking is returned
    unchanged. Log `len(pending)` as a metric; a rising partial-fusion rate is an early warning that
    one retriever is degrading, and it is invisible in aggregate latency.

    ### Failure modes

    | Failure | Signature | Detection |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | `INNER JOIN` instead of `FULL OUTER` | Identifier queries still fail after adding BM25 | Assert that a query matching only one retriever returns results |
    | No deterministic tiebreak | Results reorder between identical runs; cache keys thrash | `ORDER BY rrf DESC, id`; assert reproducibility in a test |
    | Tuned weight silently invalidated by a model change | Hybrid gets *worse* after an embedding upgrade | Use RRF; or CI-check that the swept optimum has not moved |
    | Adding a weak third retriever | NDCG drops slightly with more machinery | Correlate per-query success before adding; require independence |
    | Serial retrieval | 2.8 ms of free latency given away | `asyncio.gather` |
    | Tail amplification from fan-out | p99 worse than any component's p99 | Deadline plus partial-result fusion; log the partial rate |
    | Two indexes out of sync | Fused results contain deleted chunk IDs | Outbox and reconciliation (Module 1's design checkpoint) |
    | Comparing systems with independent CIs | "No significant difference" concluded from overlapping intervals | Paired bootstrap on the same queries |

    ### Papers and primary sources

    - **Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher 2009, "Reciprocal Rank Fusion Outperforms Condorcet and
      Individual Rank Learning Methods".** *Takeaway:* summing `1/(k + rank)` across retrievers beats
      both the individual systems and more sophisticated learned combination methods, with `k = 60`
      working across datasets without tuning. **Read §3 and Table 1** — the fact that an untuned
      one-line formula beat learned fusion is the result, and it is why RRF is the default in every
      hybrid search product.
    - **Bruch et al. 2023, "An Analysis of Fusion Functions for Hybrid Retrieval".** *Takeaway:*
      convex score combination is sensitive to score distributions and requires per-collection
      tuning, while rank-based fusion is distribution-free — with the theoretical analysis of *why*.
      **Read §4** for the Lipschitz-continuity argument; it formalises the war story in Layer 2.
    - **Fox & Shaw 1994, "Combination of Multiple Searches" (CombSUM/CombMNZ).** *Takeaway:* the
      original data-fusion result that combining retrieval runs helps, and that multiplying by the
      number of systems that retrieved a document (`CombMNZ`) helps more — the ancestor of RRF's
      agreement bonus. **Read §3.**
    - **Elastic and Weaviate hybrid-search documentation.** *Takeaway:* both ship RRF as the default
      fusion and expose `rank_constant` (their name for `k`); reading their parameter defaults
      confirms the field converged on the same values. **Read Elastic's "Reciprocal rank fusion"
      page** for the production API shape.

    ### Stretch challenge

    Produce the three-way comparison table — dense, sparse, hybrid — with bootstrap confidence
    intervals, and state whether the lift is significant. That is the lab.

    The stretch is **adaptive fusion**. You have Module 1's finding that different query segments
    prefer different retrievers, and Layer 3's finding that a fixed weight is fragile but rank-based
    weighting is not. Build a per-query weighted-rank fusion where the weight is predicted from
    query features (digit presence, max IDF, length, question-word presence), trained on your eval
    set's per-query per-retriever scores.

    Then the open question, which is where it gets genuinely hard. Adaptive fusion has more
    parameters than you have eval queries, so it will overfit — 100 queries and a feature model is a
    recipe for measuring your own training set. Design the validation honestly: nested
    cross-validation, or a held-out set you open once. **Then answer the real question: does adaptive
    fusion beat RRF by more than its confidence interval, on data it has never seen?** My prediction
    is that it does not at n=100 and might at n=1,000, and that the honest finding is "we cannot
    justify the complexity yet, and here is the sample size at which we could re-examine it". That is
    a genuinely valuable result to be able to state, and it is a much harder thing to write in a
    design document than "we added adaptive fusion".

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identifier queries still fail after adding BM25 | `INNER JOIN` in the fusion query | `FULL OUTER JOIN` with `COALESCE(..., 0)` |
| Results reorder between identical runs | RRF ties broken non-deterministically | `ORDER BY rrf DESC, id`; test reproducibility |
| Hybrid got worse after an embedding upgrade | Tuned weight encoded the old model's score distribution | Use RRF, or CI-check the swept optimum |
| Adding a third retriever lowered NDCG | RRF weights all lists equally; a weak retriever drags | Correlate per-query success first; require independence |
| Retrieval latency is the sum of both | Serial rather than parallel calls | `asyncio.gather` |
| p99 degraded after adding a retriever | Fan-out tail amplification | Deadline with partial-result fusion; log the partial rate |
| "No significant difference" from overlapping CIs | Independent rather than paired bootstrap | Paired resampling on the same queries |
| Fused results include deleted chunks | Two indexes drifting | Outbox plus reconciliation |
