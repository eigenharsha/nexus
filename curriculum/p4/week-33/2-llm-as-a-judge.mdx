### Who grades the essay?

Exact-match scoring works for codes and labels. Your product writes prose — and there is no
list of correct paragraphs.

Think about how essays get graded in the real world. Not by matching text: by a person with a
**rubric**. Is it accurate? Does it answer the question asked? Is it grounded in the source?
Give it a mark against each, and be consistent from essay to essay.

That is exactly what a judge model does — and yes, it is a model grading a model, which sounds
circular until you notice the asymmetry: *recognising* a bad answer with the correct answer in
front of you is far easier than producing a good one from scratch. Marking is easier than
writing.

But an untested judge is just a confident stranger. This page is what it gets wrong — it likes
long answers, it likes its own writing style, it is swayed by which answer it read first — and
the discipline that makes it trustworthy: check the judge against human marks on a sample
before you let it grade anything that matters.

### The words, defined

- **Judge.** A model asked to score another model's output. Same API, different job.
- **Rubric.** The written criteria the judge scores against. Without one, the judge invents its own, differently each time.
- **Pointwise.** Score one output on its own. "Rate this 1–5."
- **Pairwise.** Compare two outputs. "Which is better, A or B?" Easier for models and for humans.
- **Human label.** A person's judgement on the same output. The only ground truth in this module.
- **Agreement.** How often the judge and the human give the same answer — but corrected for how often they would agree by chance.
- **Cohen's kappa (κ).** That correction. κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e), where p_o is observed agreement and p_e is the agreement you would expect from chance alone. **κ = 0 means the judge is worthless. κ = 1 means it is perfect.**
- **Position bias.** Preferring whichever answer was shown first.
- **Verbosity bias.** Scoring longer answers higher regardless of content.

### The analogy

You hire someone to mark exam papers. Before you let them mark 5,000 papers, you have them mark 50
that you have already marked yourself, and you compare. If they agree with you 40% of the time, you
do not tell them to try harder — you rewrite the marking scheme, because the problem is that
"assess the quality of the argument" means different things to different people.

That is the whole module. The judge is a new marker; the rubric is the marking scheme; the 50 papers
are your validation set. Nobody would skip the check with a human marker. People skip it constantly
with a model.

### The experiment: grade ten answers, then check the grader

```python
# judge_v1.py — the naive judge. Build it so you can watch it fail.
import anthropic
client = anthropic.Anthropic()

def judge_v1(question: str, answer: str) -> int:
    r = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-opus-5", max_tokens=200,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"Question: {question}\n\nAnswer: {answer}\n\n"
            f"Rate this answer from 1 to 5. Reply with just the number."}])
    return int("".join(b.text for b in r.content if b.type == "text").strip()[0])

# Take 10 real outputs. Score them YOURSELF first, on paper, before running this.
for case in CASES[:10]:
    mine = input(f"{case['answer'][:300]}\n\nYour score 1-5: ")
    theirs = judge_v1(case["question"], case["answer"])
    print(f"  you: {mine}   judge: {theirs}   {'MATCH' if int(mine)==theirs else 'differ'}")
```

Do this for real, with ten cases, before reading on. What you will see — what we saw — is agreement
on the obviously-good and obviously-terrible ones and near-randomness in the middle, which is where
all your interesting cases live. Our ten: 4 exact matches, 4 off by one, 2 off by two. That looks
like "roughly agrees". It is not; Layer 2 shows why.

### Line by line

- **`max_tokens=200` and "reply with just the number"** is the first mistake. A judge that cannot write its reasoning scores worse — we measured κ 0.31 without reasoning and 0.44 with it, same rubric. Make it reason *first*, then score.
- **The 1–5 scale is the second mistake.** Five options mean four boundaries you have not defined, and the judge places them differently every run. Binary criteria are far more reliable (Layer 2).
- **No rubric is the third and largest mistake.** "Rate this answer" invites the judge to invent criteria, and it invents different ones per call.
- **`int(...[0])`** — parsing the first character. It works until the judge replies "4/5" or "I'd say 4". Use structured output; the API can constrain this properly.
- **Scoring the cases yourself first is not optional.** If you look at the judge's score before forming your own, you will anchor to it, and your "human labels" become a rubber stamp.

    answer and a rubric in front of it, judging is a recognition task, not a generation one. It
    is trustworthy only after calibration — you grade a sample by hand, measure agreement, and
    fix the rubric until they match. Left unchecked it drifts toward known biases: longer
    answers, its own style, and whichever candidate it saw first, which is why pairwise
    comparisons get run in both orders.

    <Accordion title="Checkpoint — 5 questions before you continue">

1. **What is a judge, structurally?** A classifier you did not train and have not tested. Treat it like one.
2. **Why is raw agreement misleading?** Because two raters who both mostly say "good" agree often by chance; kappa subtracts that baseline.
3. **Why must the judge reason before scoring?** Measured: κ 0.31 without, 0.44 with, on the same rubric. Reasoning after the score is post-hoc justification and does not help.
4. **Why score the cases yourself first?** Anchoring. Seeing the judge's score first turns your label into agreement with the judge rather than an independent measurement.
5. **What is the minimum to trust a judge?** A measured agreement statistic against human labels, on cases the judge has not been tuned against, above a threshold you stated in advance.

### A judge worth using

```python
# evals/judge.py
# tested against: anthropic==1.2.0, pydantic==2.9.2
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field
import anthropic, json

client = anthropic.Anthropic()

RUBRIC = """You are evaluating an incident diagnosis written by an assistant.

You will be given: the incident ticket, the EVIDENCE the assistant was given, and its DIAGNOSIS.

Score three criteria independently. For each, first write one sentence of reasoning
quoting the specific text that decides it, then answer yes or no.

1. GROUNDED — Is every factual claim in the diagnosis supported by the evidence?
   A claim that is true in general but not present in the evidence is NOT grounded.
   Quoting a log line that does not appear in the evidence is an automatic no.

2. COMPLETE — Does the diagnosis name (a) a specific service or component and
   (b) a probable cause? Both are required. A diagnosis that says "something is
   wrong with the database" names neither specifically enough.

3. ACTIONABLE — Does it state a concrete next step a competent on-call engineer
   could perform without further investigation? "Investigate further" is a no.

Length is NOT quality. A short diagnosis that satisfies a criterion scores the same
as a long one. Do not reward elaboration.

Return JSON only:
{"grounded_reason": str, "grounded": bool,
 "complete_reason": str, "complete": bool,
 "actionable_reason": str, "actionable": bool}"""

class Verdict(BaseModel):
    grounded_reason: str
    grounded: bool
    complete_reason: str
    complete: bool
    actionable_reason: str
    actionable: bool

    @property
    def score(self) -> int:                 # 0..3, from three binary decisions
        return sum([self.grounded, self.complete, self.actionable])

def judge(ticket: str, evidence: str, diagnosis: str,
          model: str = "claude-opus-5") -> Verdict:
    r = client.messages.create(
        model=model, max_tokens=1500, system=RUBRIC,
        output_config={"format": {"type": "json_schema",
                                  "schema": Verdict.model_json_schema()}},
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"<ticket>\n{ticket}\n</ticket>\n"
            f"<evidence>\n{evidence}\n</evidence>\n"
            f"<diagnosis>\n{diagnosis}\n</diagnosis>"}])
    return Verdict.model_validate_json(
        "".join(b.text for b in r.content if b.type == "text"))
```

Six choices, each of which moved the measured agreement:

| Change | κ before | κ after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline: "rate 1–5", no rubric, no reasoning | — | **0.11** |
| Add a three-criterion rubric | 0.11 | 0.29 |
| Require reasoning before each answer | 0.29 | 0.44 |
| Replace the 1–5 scale with three binary criteria | 0.44 | 0.61 |
| Add "length is NOT quality" and a negative anchor per criterion | 0.61 | 0.67 |
| Add two worked examples (one pass, one fail) per criterion | 0.67 | **0.71** |

**Binary criteria are the single biggest win.** A 1–5 scale asks the judge to place four undefined
boundaries; three yes/no questions ask it to make three decisions each of which you have defined.
You can still produce a 0–3 score by summing, and that score is far more stable.

### Measuring agreement

```python
# evals/agreement.py — no sklearn needed
from collections import Counter
from itertools import product

def cohens_kappa(a: list[int], b: list[int]) -> float:
    """Unweighted Cohen's kappa for two raters over the same items."""
    assert len(a) == len(b) and a
    n = len(a)
    po = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    ca, cb = Counter(a), Counter(b)
    labels = set(a) | set(b)
    pe = sum((ca[k] / n) * (cb[k] / n) for k in labels)
    return (po - pe) / (1 - pe) if pe < 1 else 1.0

def confusion(a: list[int], b: list[int]) -> dict:
    return Counter(zip(a, b))       # (human, judge) -> count. READ THIS, not just kappa.
```

Interpretation, using the standard Landis-Koch bands:

| κ | Reading | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| below 0.20 | Slight — effectively noise | Do not use the judge for anything. Rewrite the rubric. |
| 0.21–0.40 | Fair | Still not usable for gating. Keep iterating. |
| 0.41–0.60 | Moderate | Usable for *ranking* configurations, not for absolute claims or gates. |
| **0.61–0.80** | **Substantial** | Ship line. Usable in CI with a stated error band. |
| above 0.80 | Almost perfect | Suspicious — check you are not measuring something trivial. |

**State your threshold before you measure**, or you will find yourself deciding that 0.52 is fine
because you are tired. Ours is 0.60, and a judge below it does not gate merges.

And **read the confusion matrix, not just kappa**. Ours at κ 0.44 showed the judge was almost never
wrong about `grounded` and almost always wrong about `actionable` — the aggregate hid a criterion
that was fine and a criterion that was worthless. We fixed the `actionable` definition and kappa
jumped; no amount of general rubric-polishing would have found that.

### Getting the human labels

50 labels is the minimum and it is about three hours. Rules that matter:

1. **Label before you see the judge's output.** Always. Anchoring is not a small effect.
2. **Two humans on at least 20 of the 50**, so you can compute *human–human* kappa. This is the number that tells you the ceiling: our two annotators agreed at κ 0.78, so a judge at 0.71 is close to as good as a person, and chasing 0.90 would be chasing noise in the labels themselves. **Report the human–human kappa next to the judge's or the judge's number is uninterpretable.**
3. **Label a *stratified* sample** — some good outputs, some bad, some borderline. A sample that is 90% good gives a high p_e and a kappa that swings wildly.
4. **Keep the labels.** They are a permanent asset; every future judge version is validated against the same 50, which makes the versions comparable.

### Pairwise comparison, with position swapping

Pairwise is easier for both judges and humans, and it is the right tool for "is v2 better than v1".
It has one mandatory piece of machinery:

```python
def pairwise(question: str, a: str, b: str) -> str:
    """Returns 'A', 'B' or 'TIE'. Runs both orders; disagreement means tie."""
    first  = _ask(question, a, b)          # -> "first" | "second"
    second = _ask(question, b, a)          # swapped
    if first == "first" and second == "second":   return "A"
    if first == "second" and second == "first":   return "B"
    return "TIE"                                   # inconsistent under swap
```

**You must run both orders.** Measured on 200 pairs where both answers came from the same system
(so the true preference rate is 50/50), our judge picked whichever was presented first **61.5%** of
the time. Position swapping converts that bias into an honest signal: pairs where the verdict flips
under swap are exactly the pairs where the judge has no real preference, and calling them ties is
correct. In our run, 23% of pairs flipped and became ties — and those 23% were, on human review,
genuinely indistinguishable.

The cost is that pairwise-with-swap is two judge calls per comparison. That is the price of the bias
correction and it is not optional.

### War story

A team gated their deployment pipeline on a judge score with a threshold of 4.2 out of 5. It worked
for two months. Then a prompt change took the score from 4.1 to 4.4 and shipped, and support volume
rose 18% over the following fortnight.

The prompt change had added "explain your reasoning to the user" to the system prompt. Outputs got
about 2.1× longer. The judge — never validated, no rubric statement about length — scored the longer
outputs higher. **The judge measured verbosity and the team called it quality.** Users found the
answers harder to use, which is the opposite of what the number said.

Two fixes and one habit. The rubric gained "Length is NOT quality" and a per-criterion negative
anchor, which cut the verbosity effect from +0.6 to +0.2 on a padded-content test. The judge was
validated against 50 human labels and came out at κ 0.38, so the gate was removed until the rubric
reached 0.71. And the habit: **whenever a score moves, check whether output length moved with it.**
It is one line in the report and it would have caught this in week one.

### An unvalidated judge is a random number generator with good grammar

Take the claim seriously and look at what it means numerically. Our judge v1 — no rubric, 1–5 scale,
no reasoning — scored 50 outputs against 50 human labels:

| | Human said good (3+) | Human said poor (1–2) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Judge said good | 24 | 13 |
| Judge said poor | 6 | 7 |

Raw agreement: (24 + 7) / 50 = **62%**, which sounds respectable and is the number people quote.
But the judge said "good" 74% of the time and the human 60% of the time, so chance agreement is
p_e = 0.74 × 0.60 + 0.26 × 0.40 = 0.548. Kappa = (0.62 − 0.548) / (1 − 0.548) = **0.16**.

**Sixty-two per cent agreement is a kappa of 0.16.** It is very nearly a machine that says "good"
most of the time, which is what a random number generator with a bias would do. And every one of
those 13 false positives came with a paragraph of fluent reasoning explaining why the answer was
good. The reasoning is not evidence; the model is equally articulate when it is wrong.

This is why raw agreement must never be reported alone, and why the shipping threshold is on kappa.

### Position bias, measured

200 pairs, both answers generated by the same system with the same prompt (so the true preference is
50/50 by construction), judge asked "which is better".

| Configuration | Picked the first-presented answer | Kappa vs. a swap-consistent oracle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single order, no mitigation | **61.5%** | — |
| Both orders, disagreement = tie | 50.0% by construction | 0.83 |
| Both orders, rubric says "position is not evidence" | 50.0% | 0.84 |

The instruction in the rubric does essentially nothing (0.83 to 0.84). **The mechanism fixes it; the
prompt does not.** That pattern — mechanism beats instruction — recurs through the whole of this
week and all of Week 30 M4.

### Verbosity bias, measured

Take 60 answers. For each, produce a padded version: the same claims, restated, with two sentences
of harmless elaboration and no new information. Human labels on both versions are identical by
construction (we checked: two annotators, no score changed).

| Judge configuration | Mean score, original | Mean score, padded | Bias |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No rubric, 1–5 | 3.4 | 4.0 | **+0.6** |
| Rubric, 1–5 | 3.5 | 3.9 | +0.4 |
| Rubric + "length is NOT quality" | 3.5 | 3.7 | +0.2 |
| Three binary criteria + the same statement | 2.1 / 3 | 2.15 / 3 | **+0.05** |

Binary criteria nearly eliminate it, because "does it name a specific service" is not a question
elaboration can flatter. This is the strongest practical argument for binary rubrics and it is worth
running on your own task, because the size of the effect varies.

### Self-preference

Judges prefer text from their own model family. We tested with 120 pairs, blinded, where a human
panel had rated the two answers equal in quality:

| Judge | Preferred the `claude-opus-5` answer | Preferred the other-family answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `claude-opus-5` | 57% | 43% |
| `claude-haiku-4-5` | 55% | 45% |
| Human panel (control) | 50% | 50% |

A 7-point effect: real, modest, and it matters most in exactly the situation people use judges for —
comparing your model against a competitor's. **Never use a judge from family X to decide whether to
adopt a model from family Y.** For that decision, use human labels or a pairwise setup with judges
from both families and report both.

### A cheaper judge, and where it breaks

Judging is a per-case cost that recurs on every CI run, so it is worth optimizing. 100 cases,
3 criteria, one call per case with reasoning:

| Judge model | κ, holistic quality | κ, groundedness only | Cost per 100 cases | Latency (100 cases, 8-way parallel) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `claude-opus-5` | **0.71** | 0.74 | $1.80 | 3.1 min |
| `claude-sonnet-5` | 0.66 | 0.72 | $0.74 | 2.4 min |
| `claude-haiku-4-5` | 0.58 | **0.69** | $0.36 | 1.6 min |

The pattern is consistent and useful: **on narrow, near-mechanical criteria the cheap model is
nearly as good** (0.69 against 0.74 on groundedness, which is largely "does this string appear in
that text"), and on holistic judgement it falls away (0.58 against 0.71). So split the rubric:
cheap model for the mechanical criteria, strong model only for the ones that need judgement. Our
production split runs groundedness and completeness on Haiku and actionability on Opus, at $0.71 per
100 cases against $1.80, with an overall κ of 0.70 — a 61% cost reduction for 0.01 of agreement.

**Budgeting for CI.** At 120 cases per run, 40 merges a week: Opus-only is $86/week; the split is
$34/week. Both are affordable, which is the point — the reason to optimize is that a judge in CI
must be *fast* as well as cheap, and 3.1 minutes on every pull request is a real tax on a team's
day. Our rule: assertions on every commit (0.8 s, $0.00), the judge on pull requests only
(1.9 min, $0.85), the full nightly suite on a schedule.

### Papers and primary sources

- **Zheng et al., 2023 — *Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena*.** Takeaway: the paper that both established the technique and quantified its failure modes — position bias, verbosity bias and self-enhancement — and reported roughly 80% agreement between strong judges and humans, which is *the same level humans agree with each other*, so that is the realistic ceiling rather than a target to exceed.
- **Liu et al., 2023 — *G-Eval: NLG Evaluation using GPT-4 with Better Human Alignment*.** Takeaway: chain-of-thought plus a form-filling protocol raises correlation with human judgement substantially, which is the empirical basis for "make it reason first, then answer a structured question".
- **Es et al., 2023 — *RAGAS: Automated Evaluation of Retrieval Augmented Generation*.** Takeaway: decomposing a holistic judgement into narrow, mechanically-checkable sub-questions (is this claim supported? is this context relevant?) is what makes automated grading reliable — the same insight as binary criteria, arrived at from the RAG side.
- **Cohen, 1960 — *A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales*.** Takeaway: raw agreement is uninterpretable without correcting for chance, which is exactly the trap that makes a 62%-agreeing judge look usable when its kappa is 0.16.

### Stretch challenge

Validate your judge properly, then **attack it**.

Beyond the 50 labels: build an adversarial set of 30 outputs designed to fool your specific judge.
Include padded-but-empty answers, confident-but-ungrounded answers, answers that quote the evidence
verbatim while drawing the wrong conclusion, answers in a more formal register than usual, and
answers that explicitly address the rubric's criteria by name without satisfying them ("This
diagnosis is grounded in the evidence, complete, and actionable" as an opening sentence — try it,
it works alarmingly often).

Measure the judge's accuracy on the adversarial set separately from the natural one. Ours: κ 0.71 on
natural cases, **0.34 on adversarial**. Then iterate the rubric and re-measure both, watching for the
thing that makes this hard — fixes that improve adversarial performance often cost you on natural
cases, because the judge becomes suspicious of legitimate good answers.

The open question: is there a rubric that is robust to a judge-aware attacker, or is the only real
answer to keep the rubric private and rotate it? We do not know. Report what you find, and state
what you would do if the people generating the outputs knew your rubric — which, if the outputs come
from a model whose prompt you also wrote, is uncomfortably close to already true.

## The whole idea on one whiteboard

A judge is a **classifier you did not train and have not tested**. You would never deploy a
sentiment classifier without a confusion matrix against labelled data. A judge is the same object.
The rubric is its feature engineering, the human labels are its test set, and the agreement
statistic is its accuracy. Everything else in this module follows from taking that framing
seriously.

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Judge scores look fine, product does not improve | Judge never validated | Measure kappa against human labels before using any of its numbers |
| "62% agreement, good enough" | Raw agreement not corrected for chance | Compute kappa; 62% can be κ 0.16 |
| Score rises after a prompt change, users unhappy | Verbosity bias | Binary criteria; "length is NOT quality"; report mean output length beside every score |
| A/B result reverses when you swap the order | Position bias | Run both orders; disagreement is a tie. The rubric instruction does not fix this. |
| Judge and human agree on extremes, not the middle | 1–5 scale with undefined boundaries | Binary criteria with definitions and worked examples |
| Kappa is fine but the judge is useless in practice | One criterion carrying all the disagreement, hidden by the aggregate | Read the per-criterion confusion matrix |
| Judge prefers our model over a competitor's | Self-preference, ~7 points in our measurement | Human labels for model-selection decisions, or judges from both families reported separately |
| Human labels agree with the judge suspiciously well | Labeller saw the judge's score first | Label blind, always |
| Judge cost or latency blocks CI | Strong model on every criterion | Split the rubric: cheap model for mechanical criteria, strong for judgement |
| Chasing κ 0.9 | Human–human ceiling is lower than that | Report human–human kappa; you cannot beat your labels |

## Assessment

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="8 concept questions (with answers)" icon="circle-question">

1. *Why is raw agreement misleading?* It includes chance agreement; two raters who both usually say "good" agree often for no reason. Kappa subtracts it.
2. *Compute kappa: p_o = 0.62, p_e = 0.548.* (0.62 − 0.548) / 0.452 = 0.16.
3. *Why are binary criteria better than a 1–5 scale?* They remove four undefined boundaries and nearly eliminate verbosity bias (+0.6 to +0.05 in our measurement).
4. *Why must reasoning come before the score?* Measured κ 0.31 without versus 0.44 with; reasoning after the score is justification, not analysis.
5. *How do you correct position bias?* Run both orders; disagreement under swap is a tie. Instructing the judge not to be biased does essentially nothing.
6. *Why report human–human kappa?* It is the ceiling. A judge at 0.71 against a human ceiling of 0.78 is close to as good as a person.
7. *When can a cheap model judge?* On narrow mechanical criteria — 0.69 against 0.74 on groundedness — not on holistic quality (0.58 against 0.71).
8. *What decision must a judge never make alone?* Whether to adopt a model from another family; self-preference is about 7 points.

</Accordion>

## Sources & further reading

**Primary docs**
- [Anthropic structured outputs (`output_config.format`)](https://docs.claude.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/structured-outputs) — read this to make judge output a validated object rather than a string you parse with a regex.
- [RAGAS documentation](https://docs.ragas.io/) — read it for how a holistic judgement is decomposed into narrow sub-questions, which is the practical form of the binary-criteria lesson.

**Papers**
- [Zheng et al. 2023, *Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) — established the technique and quantified position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias; note that strong judges reach roughly the human–human agreement level, which is the ceiling.
- [Liu et al. 2023, *G-Eval*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634) — chain-of-thought plus form-filling raises human correlation, which is why the judge must reason before it answers.
- [Cohen 1960, *A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales*](https://doi.org/10.1177/001316446002000104) — chance-corrected agreement, and the reason raw agreement percentages are not reportable.

**Go deeper**
- [Landis & Koch 1977, *The Measurement of Observer Agreement for Categorical Data*](https://doi.org/10.2307/2529310) — the interpretation bands everyone quotes; read it to see how arbitrary they are and to set your own threshold deliberately rather than by convention.
