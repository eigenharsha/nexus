## Reference solution — `P3-W20-M4`

The task: head to head — class weights vs SMOTE vs undersampling vs threshold-only, same data, same budget. Report the winner with confidence intervals.

## The result

Five arms, 120,000 rows, 920 positives in 72,000 training rows (1.28%), XGBoost with identical early stopping and the same validation split.

| Arm | Average precision | 95% CI | Difference vs baseline | 95% CI | Brier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **no treatment** | **0.49268** | [0.43755, 0.54870] | — | — | **0.008169** |
| `scale_pos_weight` | 0.45889 | [0.39969, 0.51473] | **−0.03380** | [−0.05258, −0.01675] | 0.062046 |
| random oversample | 0.45673 | [0.39843, 0.51448] | **−0.03596** | [−0.05446, −0.01891] | 0.061366 |
| SMOTE | 0.37785 | [0.32258, 0.43454] | **−0.11483** | [−0.14701, −0.08444] | 0.075212 |
| random undersample | 0.35243 | [0.29722, 0.40801] | **−0.14026** | [−0.17475, −0.10668] | 0.154793 |

**Every treatment made ranking significantly worse. Every difference interval excludes zero.** SMOTE cost 11.5 average-precision points; undersampling cost 14.0. And every treatment degraded calibration, by 7.5x to **18.9x** on Brier.

The winner is doing nothing to the data and moving the threshold instead.

## Then why does everyone believe otherwise

Because of the second table, and producing it is what separates a 2 from a 3.

| Arm | Cost at `t = 0.5` | Cost at its own best threshold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| no treatment | **£127,800** | **£91,232** (t = 0.0245) |
| `scale_pos_weight` | £93,424 | £90,936 (t = 0.4732) |
| random oversample | £94,216 | £93,288 |
| random undersample | £98,312 | £96,128 |
| SMOTE | £109,736 | £107,288 |
| act on nobody | £184,200 | — |

(`c_FP` = £8, `c_FN` = £600, 24,000 test rows.)

**At the default threshold, resampling looks like a £34,376 improvement.** Tune the threshold on both and the gap is **£296** — noise.

**Resampling did not improve the model. It moved the operating point**, and it happened to move it near 0.5, which is where everybody was evaluating. The untreated model with a tuned threshold gets to the same place with better ranking, calibrated probabilities and less machinery.

## The threshold, from the cost matrix

Not from F1. F1 asserts that a £8 review and a £600 loss are equally bad; they differ by 75x.

```
act when   p > c_FP / (c_FP + c_FN) = 8 / 608 = 0.01316
```

The measured empirical argmin was **0.02451** against the theoretical **0.01316** — a ratio of 1.9. **Report the ratio**: near 1 means the probabilities are trustworthy and you can re-derive the threshold in closed form whenever Finance revises the numbers, which is operationally valuable because it needs no validation data. Far from 1 is a calibration alarm you got for free.

And select the threshold on **validation**, then report the realised cost at that fixed threshold on test. The argmin of a noisy cost curve is itself noisy; choosing and reporting on the same data is the selection bias from Week 19 Module 3.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious solution compares the arms at the default threshold and concludes resampling helps.** It is the standard result in the literature and Elor and Averbuch-Elor (2022) show across 73 datasets that it is an artifact of evaluating at a fixed 0.5. Tune every arm's threshold and the effect disappears.

Three more wrong turns:

1. **Resampling outside the fold.** `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline`, always — scikit-learn's own `Pipeline` cannot host a resampler because it has no concept of a step that changes the row count. Week 19 Module 4 measured the leak at **+15.24 AUC points**.
2. **Unequal fit budgets.** The resampled arms have more rows, so with a fixed `n_estimators` they get more gradient steps. Use identical early stopping on the same validation set, and report `best_iteration` per arm so a reader can check.
3. **Reporting ROC-AUC.** At 1.28% positive it is nearly insensitive — the arms span 0.725 to 0.775 on AUC and 0.352 to 0.493 on average precision. Report AP, with the base rate beside it.

## Grading notes

**3/3 looks like:** five arms with a **paired** bootstrap (same resample indices across arms) on average precision; Brier reported for every arm; resampling inside the fold, with the leaky variant also measured to show the +15 AUC points; the cost table at both 0.5 and each arm's own tuned threshold; the threshold derived from the cost matrix and selected on validation; and the theoretical-versus-empirical ratio reported as a calibration diagnostic.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **Comparing at 0.5 only.** Produces the standard wrong conclusion, from correct arithmetic.
2. **An unpaired bootstrap.** The interval roughly doubles and several real differences become "not established".
3. **Concluding "SMOTE is bad".** The measured claim is narrower and better: *on this data, with a strong classifier and a tuned threshold, every treatment reduced average precision.* Module 4's Layer 3 shows SMOTE helping at 10 dimensions (+1.36) and hurting at 50 (−6.98) — the honest statement is conditional, and stating it conditionally is what earns the mark.
