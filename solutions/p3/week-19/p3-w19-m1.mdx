## Reference solution — `P3-W19-M1`

The task: given a business scenario with stated costs of a false positive and a false negative, derive the optimal threshold and the expected cost — then show what the default and the F1 maximiser would have cost instead.

## The derivation

For a row with **calibrated** predicted probability `p`, compare the two expected costs:

```
act (predict positive)     : expected cost = (1 - p) * c_FP     you pay c_FP if it is negative
do not act                 : expected cost =      p * c_FN      you pay c_FN if it is positive

act when   (1 - p) c_FP  <  p c_FN
           c_FP - p c_FP <  p c_FN
           c_FP          <  p (c_FN + c_FP)

           p  >  c_FP / (c_FP + c_FN)
```

Three properties worth stating in the write-up, because each is a marking point:

- **It does not depend on prevalence.** Only on the ratio of costs. A rare positive class does not change the threshold; it changes how many rows clear it.
- **It requires calibration.** The derivation applies arithmetic to `p`. If `p` is a ranking score rather than a probability, the formula is being applied to a fiction.
- **0.5 is the special case `c_FP == c_FN`.** The library default is an assertion about your business, and it is almost never the right one.

## The table that is the deliverable

Every submission must produce this shape — five thresholds, their realised cost on held-out data, and the excess over the best:

| Threshold | How it was chosen | Cost | Excess |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | the library default | **£22,480** | £12,300 |
| 0.06934 | maximising F1 | £13,080 | £2,900 |
| 0.0104 | the closed form `c_FP/(c_FP+c_FN)` | — | — |
| **0.02214** | empirical argmin of realised cost | **£10,180** | **£0** |
| — | act on nobody / act on everybody | the two trivial bounds | — |

**The default costs 2.2x the optimum. The F1 maximiser costs 28% more than doing the arithmetic.** Those two sentences are the finding; the rest of the report supports them.

Include the two trivial bounds. A reviewer needs to know that the model beats "do nothing" — occasionally, on a badly imbalanced problem with an expensive intervention, it does not, and that is worth discovering before launch rather than after.

## Why the theoretical and empirical thresholds differ

The closed form gives 0.0104 and the empirical argmin lands at 0.0221 — a factor of 2.1. That gap is **not** an error in either calculation. It is a calibration diagnostic, and it is free.

The closed form is exactly optimal for a calibrated model. This logistic regression is under-confident at the low end of the probability range, so the value the formula wants is not the value that actually minimises realised cost. **Report both numbers and their ratio.** A ratio near 1 means your probabilities are trustworthy and you can re-derive the threshold in closed form whenever the costs change — which is the operationally valuable state, because it needs no validation data. A ratio above about 2 means calibrate first.

That last point is the recommendation the write-up should end on: **calibrate, then use the closed form.** An empirically-tuned threshold has to be re-tuned on fresh data every time Finance revises a cost; a calibrated model plus a formula does not.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious solution sweeps thresholds, picks the argmin, and reports one number.** It gets the right threshold and teaches nobody anything. The comparison table is the deliverable because the audience is a stakeholder who currently believes 0.5 is a neutral choice, and the only thing that changes their mind is seeing what it costs.

Two wrong turns worth knowing about:

1. **Optimising the threshold on the test set.** The argmin of a noisy cost curve is itself noisy, so picking it on the data you then report is the same selection bias as tuning on the test set (Module 3 measured that optimism at +1.96 points on pure noise). Choose the threshold on validation data, then report the realised cost at that **fixed** threshold on the test set. Most submissions get this wrong and it costs a correctness point.
2. **Reporting cost per row rather than total.** A per-row average hides the scale of the decision and makes the comparison unpersuasive. Report the total for a stated volume — "per 48,000 transactions" — and the annualised figure if you can defend the extrapolation.

## Grading notes

**3/3 looks like:** the derivation written out; the five-row table with realised costs on a **held-out** set at thresholds fixed on validation; both trivial bounds included; the theoretical/empirical ratio reported and named as a calibration diagnostic; and a closing recommendation that says calibrate-then-formula rather than re-tune-forever.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **Choosing and reporting the threshold on the same data.** The number is optimistic and the amount is not small.
2. **Omitting the F1 row.** It is the row that does the persuading, because F1 is what the reader would otherwise have used and it costs 28% more here.
3. **Not stating the calibration caveat.** A cost-optimal threshold derived from an uncalibrated model is a plausible-looking number with no theoretical support, and a reviewer who knows that will not trust the rest of the report.
