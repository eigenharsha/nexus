## Reference solution — `P3-W18-M1`

The task: implement CART from scratch and match scikit-learn's predictions on a small dataset.

## The answer to the question the task actually asks

**You can match exactly at depths 1 and 2 on all three datasets, and you cannot match exactly below that — and the reason is not your code.** A submission that reports 100% agreement at every depth has either overfitted its implementation to one seed or is not measuring what it thinks.

Measured, fraction of identical test predictions against `DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=d, random_state=0)`:

| dataset | `max_depth=2` | `max_depth=3` | `max_depth=None` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| iris | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 0.9778 |
| wine | 1.0000 | 0.9259 | 0.8704 |
| breast_cancer | 1.0000 | 0.9942 | 0.9883 |

Now the measurement that explains it — scikit-learn against **itself**, 20 random seeds, same data, `splitter="best"`:

| dataset | depth | distinct root splits | mean agreement with seed 0 | **distinct prediction vectors** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| wine | 3 | 1 | 0.9667 | **10** |
| wine | None | 1 | 0.9204 | **15** |
| breast cancer | 3 | 1 | 0.9789 | 7 |
| breast cancer | None | 1 | 0.9719 | **19** |

Twenty seeds, nineteen different fully-grown trees on breast cancer. The root split is identical every time; everything below it moves. scikit-learn permutes the feature order before scanning at each node — `random_state` is used even with `splitter="best"` — so any tie in the impurity gain is broken by that permutation, and ties are common in the small, nearly-pure nodes near the bottom.

Our 0.9259 on `wine` at depth 3 is only slightly below scikit-learn's own 0.9667 mean self-agreement. **That is what "matching" means here**, and saying so is the deliverable.

## The implementation detail that matters

Naive best-split search recomputes the class counts for every candidate threshold: `O(n)` per candidate, `O(n^2)` per feature. The fix is to sort once and move one row at a time from the right child to the left:

```python
order = np.argsort(X[:, j], kind="mergesort")     # STABLE sort — deterministic ties
xs, ys = X[order, j], y[order]
left = np.zeros(n_classes); right = parent.copy()
for i in range(n - 1):
    left[ys[i]] += 1                              # O(1) update
    right[ys[i]] -= 1
    if xs[i] == xs[i + 1]:                        # cannot cut between equal values
        continue
    ...
```

`kind="mergesort"` is not decoration. It is the only stable sort NumPy offers by that name, and stability is what makes *your* tie-breaking deterministic — which is the precondition for being able to say anything about how your result differs from scikit-learn's.

Per node the cost becomes `O(p n log n)` for the sorts plus `O(p n)` for the scans. That is what scikit-learn's Cython `BestSplitter` does.

## Instability, and the number to report

```
single tree, flip 1 of 398 labels, 30 trials:  mean 3.78% of test predictions change, max 6.43%
200-tree random forest, same perturbation   :  mean 0.37%
```

A 10.2x reduction, and it is the reason Module 2 exists. Two details worth reporting alongside: the root split was unchanged in **30 out of 30** trials, and the maximum was nearly double the mean — the damage is not uniformly distributed across which label you flip.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious solution recurses on boolean masks of the original array and looks clean.** It is fine at these sizes. It is `O(n)` allocation per node, so a fully-grown tree on 200,000 rows allocates gigabytes of intermediate arrays. scikit-learn instead permutes a single index array in place and passes `(start, end)` offsets. If you want the from-scratch implementation to be more than a teaching exercise, that is the change to make — and it is a good second pass once the first version is correct.

Two wrong turns worth knowing about:

1. **Splitting at a data value rather than a midpoint.** Using `xs[i]` as the threshold with a `<=` test puts a boundary exactly on a training point. It works, and it disagrees with scikit-learn on every dataset, because scikit-learn uses `(xs[i] + xs[i+1]) / 2`. You will spend an hour on this if you do not notice.
2. **Forgetting the `xs[i] == xs[i+1]` guard.** Without it you generate candidate splits between two rows with the identical feature value, which produces a "split" that puts identical rows on opposite sides. It does not crash; it silently produces trees that cannot be reproduced by any real threshold.

## Grading notes

**3/3 looks like:** exact match at depths 1 and 2 on all three datasets; the incremental `O(1)` count update with a stable sort; the scikit-learn-against-itself experiment run and reported; the instability measurement with both mean and max; and a written acceptance criterion that is stated in terms of agreement *relative to scikit-learn's self-agreement*, not in absolute terms.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **Claiming a bug where there is none.** Reporting 0.87 agreement at unlimited depth as a failure, and then rewriting a correct splitter three times. The seed experiment costs four lines and prevents the whole detour.
2. **`O(n^2)` split search.** Passes the correctness tests, takes 40 minutes on breast cancer at unlimited depth. The incremental update is the entire performance content of this module.
3. **Not reporting the max alongside the mean instability.** 3.78% mean sounds tolerable; 6.43% worst case on a single flipped label is the number that motivates ensembling.
