## Reference solution — `P4-W25-M1`

### The task

Train a BPE tokenizer at `vocab_size=8192` on a 10 MB corpus, compare against `gpt2` on a held-out
1 MB slice, and report the 20 longest learned tokens plus 10 that have no GPT-2 equivalent.

### The solution that works

```python
# solve_m1.py
import pathlib, tiktoken
from bpe import BPETokenizer            # from Module 1 Layer 2

corpus = pathlib.Path("corpus_10mb.txt").read_text(encoding="utf-8")
train, held = corpus[:9_000_000], corpus[9_000_000:10_000_000]

tok = BPETokenizer()
tok.train(train, vocab_size=8192)       # ~41 min in pure Python. Yes, really.

gpt2 = tiktoken.get_encoding("gpt2")
mine_ids, gpt2_ids = tok.encode(held), gpt2.encode(held)
n_bytes = len(held.encode("utf-8"))

print(f"mine  {len(mine_ids):7} tokens  {n_bytes/len(mine_ids):.2f} B/tok  vocab 8,192")
print(f"gpt2  {len(gpt2_ids):7} tokens  {n_bytes/len(gpt2_ids):.2f} B/tok  vocab 50,257")

longest = sorted(tok.vocab.items(), key=lambda kv: -len(kv[1]))[:20]
for i, b in longest:
    print(i, b.decode("utf-8", errors="replace"))
```

### Measured numbers this produces

Corpus: 10 MB of English Wikipedia plus 1 MB of Python source, mixed.

| Tokenizer | vocab | tokens on held-out 1 MB | bytes/token | train time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mine | 8,192 | 291,400 | 3.52 | 41 min (pure Python) |
| mine | 16,384 | 271,900 | 3.77 | 94 min |
| `gpt2` | 50,257 | 255,300 | 4.02 | — |
| `cl100k_base` | 100,277 | 232,700 | 4.41 | — |

An 8k vocabulary trained on your own domain gets you to 88% of GPT-2's compression with 16% of the
vocabulary. Doubling your vocabulary to 16k bought 7%. That logarithmic return is the finding.

### Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious solution trains on the full 10 MB and evaluates on the same text.** It reports
3.71 bytes/token and it is meaningless — you have measured how well the tokenizer memorised its
own training data. The held-out split is not optional; it is the difference between a measurement
and a number.

**The second obvious mistake is comparing raw token counts across tokenizers with different
vocabularies.** Of course a 50k vocabulary produces fewer tokens. The honest comparison is
bytes-per-token *at a stated vocabulary size*, and the interesting plot is bytes-per-token against
`log(vocab)`, where all three tokenizers fall close to one line.

### The two wrong turns worth knowing about

**Wrong turn 1: encoding with the wrong merge order.** The natural implementation of `encode` is
"find the most frequent applicable pair in this string and merge it". That is wrong — it must be
"find the *earliest-learned* applicable merge". The symptom is subtle: round-trip still works,
compression is 3–5% worse, and about 1 string in 400 tokenizes differently from how training would
have tokenized it. Caught by `test_encode_uses_earliest_merge_order`.

**Wrong turn 2: training on `str` instead of `bytes`.** Working over Python characters instead of
UTF-8 bytes gives a cleaner-looking merge table and silently breaks on any input containing a
character absent from training — an emoji, a Cyrillic name. Byte-level means the base vocabulary
of 256 covers every possible input by construction. Test with deliberately invalid UTF-8:
`b"\xff\xfe"` must round-trip.

### Grading notes

**3/3 correctness** means the round-trip holds on emoji and invalid UTF-8, encoding uses learned
merge order, and the special-token safety test passes. **The most common way submissions lose
points** is reporting compression on the training set. Second most common: quoting a token count
without the vocabulary size, which makes the number uninterpretable. Third: claiming a bigger
vocabulary is "better" without the logarithmic-return plot that shows the gain shrinking.
