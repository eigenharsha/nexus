## Reference solution — `P4-W32-M4`

### Setting limits from your own distribution

Do not copy our numbers. Take your Week 30 benchmark, plot each resource for *successful* runs, and
set each limit near p99:

```python
import numpy as np
for name, values in [("nodes", node_counts), ("tokens", token_counts),
                     ("usd", costs), ("seconds", durations)]:
    p50, p99 = np.percentile(values, [50, 99])
    print(f"{name:8s} p50={p50:8.1f}  p99={p99:8.1f}  suggested limit={p99 * 1.25:8.1f}")
```

Then check the limit's cost: what fraction of historical successful runs would it have killed?
Above 1% is too tight. Ours stopped 0.28–0.52% depending on the resource, which is the range where
a limit is doing work without destroying good runs.

### The detector, and the number that matters

| Window | Recall | False positives (4,200 runs) | Mean detection step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 98.0% | 41 (0.98%) | 3.1 |
| **3** | **92.2%** | **3 (0.07%)** | **4.2** |
| 4 | 76.5% | 1 (0.02%) | 5.4 |

Window 2 has the best recall and is the wrong choice. A false positive kills a run that was about to
succeed, and 41 of those is far more damaging than the 3 extra livelocks window 3 misses. **Report
the false-positive rate whenever you report a detector**, in this module and in every module of
Week 33.

The paraphrase weakness is real and you should measure it: a tool returning "no results (attempt 4)"
defeats content hashing. Normalizing (stripping digits and timestamps) caught 8 of 9 injected
paraphrase cases; embedding-space novelty with a 0.95 cosine threshold caught 9 of 9 at 3 ms and
$0.00002 per step with a local bge-m3.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Obvious:** a step limit is enough. Steps have wildly different costs; the runaway in Week 30's war
story terminated every run correctly and still cost $310 because 340 runs each burned their full
allowance. Four budgets, plus a fleet-level guard, because the per-run cap did not save the team in
this module's war story either.

**Obvious:** detect loops by comparing tool calls. That catches the identical-call case and misses
the two-node alternation, which is the common shape. Measure *information*, not actions.

**Obvious:** treat `recursion_limit` as a handled failure. It is always a bug — 11 hits in 4,200
runs, every one a genuine defect. Page on it.

## The two wrong turns worth knowing about

1. **A breaker that never opened during a real outage**, because the dependency returned 200 with an empty body. Tools must validate their own responses; a schema-invalid success is a failure and must count toward the breaker.
2. **An escalation nobody read.** "The agent gave up" wastes a human. The five fields — established, ruled out, tried-and-failed, suggested next, resume command — took handling time from 14 minutes to 5.
