## Reference solution — `P4-W29-M1`

### The measurement, not the code

The code is in the module. What people get wrong is the measurement, so that is what this page is.

**Deriving per-step reliability.** You cannot label individual steps — nobody can tell you whether
step 4 of a six-step run "succeeded". You can count runs and steps. So invert:

```python
import math
success_rate = 22 / 30          # 0.7333
mean_steps   = 6.2              # mean over SUCCESSFUL runs
per_step = success_rate ** (1 / mean_steps)     # 0.9512
print(f"per-step reliability ≈ {per_step:.3%}")

# What n would you need for 90% end-to-end at this p?
n = math.log(0.90) / math.log(per_step)          # 2.10 steps
print(f"at p={per_step:.3f}, 90% end-to-end needs n ≤ {n:.1f} steps")
```

That last line is the punchline of the module and almost nobody computes it: at 95.1% per step you
get 90% end-to-end only if the task takes **two steps**. Not six. That is what "constrained
workflows beat open agency" means arithmetically.

**Use the mean over successful runs, not all runs.** Failed runs are truncated by `max_steps` and
drag the mean toward the cap, which inflates the derived per-step reliability. We got 95.1% using
successful runs and 93.4% using all runs; the second is wrong in a direction that flatters you.

**The independence assumption is false, and it matters in your favour.** Steps are correlated —
a run that is going well tends to keep going well. So `p ** n` is a *lower* bound on real
end-to-end success, and the derived per-step figure is a *lower* bound on real per-step reliability.
Say so when you present it. An interviewer who knows this will be pleased you said it first.

### Reference numbers

| Metric | Value | Reproduce by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| End-to-end success | 22/30 = 73.3% | `make bench` |
| Mean steps (successful runs) | 6.2 | `bench/results.md` |
| Derived per-step | 95.1% | the inversion above |
| Cost per run | $0.176 | 25,500 in + 1,920 out at $5/$25 per M |
| Cost per successful task | $0.239 | total spend / successes |
| After converting the two dominant task types to chains | 26/30, $0.031/run | `make bench PROFILE=chain` |

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Obvious:** raise `max_steps` so fewer runs get truncated. This makes things worse. Runs that hit
the cap are usually looping, and raising the cap buys more loop, not more answer. In our data,
raising `max_steps` from 6 to 12 moved success from 22/30 to 23/30 while raising cost per run from
$0.176 to $0.291 — cost per successful task went *up*, from $0.239 to $0.379.

**Obvious:** improve the prompt. Worth about 2 points here and unbounded in time spent. The
arithmetic says the exponent is the lever: cutting mean steps from 6.2 to 3 at the same per-step
reliability takes end-to-end from 73% to 86% with no prompt work at all.

**Obvious:** count exceptions as the failure metric. This is the error the war story punishes.
`stop_reason` is the metric. A run that terminates cleanly having achieved nothing is your most
common failure and the only one that will not page you.

## The two wrong turns worth knowing about

1. **Scoring after seeing the output.** If you write the acceptance criterion once you have read the answer, your success rate will be 8–15 points higher and entirely fictional. Write all 30 criteria first, in a file, and commit it before the first run.
2. **Averaging cost per call.** Some steps are 2,000 input tokens and some are 9,000. Reporting a mean per-call cost and multiplying by steps gave us $0.14 where the real figure was $0.176 — a 20% understatement, because the expensive steps are the late ones and late steps are more common in failing runs.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Correctness** looks like: four distinct `stop_reason` values observed in the benchmark run,
the `no_progress` detector demonstrably firing at least once, and budgets enforced in dollars as well
as steps.

**3/3 on Explanation** requires the sentence "steps are not independent, so p^n is a lower bound"
somewhere in the write-up, and a stated decision about which two task types you converted to chains
and why those two.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**
- Success rate reported with no step count, so per-step reliability cannot be derived. Half a result.
- Cost reported per call rather than per successful task. This is the metric the whole of Week 32 turns on; getting it wrong here compounds.
- A `max_steps` guard with no dollar budget. The war story exists precisely because that combination looks complete and is not.
