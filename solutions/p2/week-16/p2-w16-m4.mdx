## Reference solution — `P2-W16-M4`

A two-page EDA memo, and the two measurements that make it defensible.

### Measurement 1: correlation is blind

| Relationship | Pearson r | Spearman ρ | Mutual information |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `y = 2x + noise` | +0.9606 | +0.9632 | 1.2168 |
| `y = x² + noise` | **−0.0218** | −0.0145 | **0.9824** |
| `y = sin(3x) + noise` | **+0.2403** | +0.2445 | **0.9104** |
| `y = noise` | +0.0154 | +0.0148 | **0.0106** |

**A perfect parabola has a Pearson r of −0.02, indistinguishable from the pure-noise row.** Filtering
features by correlation would have dropped it. Spearman does not save you — a parabola is not
monotonic, so the two halves cancel. **Mutual information catches all of them.**

Guidance: MI to *screen*, then plot the survivors. MI gives no direction and no shape, and its values
are only comparable within a dataset.

### Measurement 2: the KS test's sample-size problem

Detection rate for a shift of **0.01 standard deviations** — far too small to affect any model:

| Samples per side | Flagged at p &lt; 0.01 |
| --- | --- |
| 1,000 | 0% |
| 10,000 | 4% |
| 100,000 | **28%** |
| 1,000,000 | **100%** |

The false-positive rate on genuinely identical distributions was 0–1% throughout — **the test is
correctly calibrated and doing exactly what it is designed to do.** It answers "are these samples
from *exactly* the same distribution?", and at large n it answers that confidently for differences of
no practical consequence.

Power on shifts that *do* matter:

| True shift | n=1,000 | n=10,000 | n=100,000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.05 SD | 4.5% | 65.5% | 100% |
| 0.10 SD | 23.5% | 100% | 100% |
| 0.25 SD | 99.0% | 100% | 100% |

**At n = 1,000 you cannot reliably detect a 0.1 SD shift. At n = 100,000 you detect everything,
including what you should ignore.** The useful window is narrow and it depends on sample size —
which is the argument for **fixing** the sample size in a monitor rather than letting it grow with
traffic.

```python
drifted = (p_value < 0.01) and (ks_statistic > 0.1)     # BOTH gates
```

The war story: a team raised their threshold to `p < 1e-10`, it still fired because the p-values were
`0.0` to floating-point precision, so **they disabled the check** — and four months later missed a
genuine KS statistic of ~0.4. **The dangerous outcome of a noisy alert is not the noise; it is the
disabling.**

### Measurement 3: multiple comparisons

40 features of pure noise, 1,000 trials:

```
mean number significant at p<0.05: 2.00   (expected 40 * 0.05 = 2.0)
at least one significant:  88% of trials
three or more significant: 32% of trials
median smallest p-value:   0.0178
```

**On forty noise features you find at least one "significant" result 88% of the time.** And EDA is
worse than this, because you do not run 40 tests — you look at plots, notice patterns, and test the
promising ones. **The number of implicit comparisons is unbounded and unrecorded.**

The practical defence: **treat EDA output as hypotheses, not findings.** Write "amount appears
bimodal, which suggests two populations; worth testing" rather than "there are two populations". You
will be right more often and trusted more.

### The memo

Two pages, for someone who will not open your code:

1. **The recommendation, first.** "This dataset can support a churn model, with three caveats." Not
   "the dataset has 47 columns" — the reader is deciding something.
2. **The three findings that change what someone does.** Not everything you found.
3. **The risks, naming specific columns.** "`days_to_followup` is populated after discharge and must
   not be used" beats a paragraph about data quality.
4. **What the data cannot tell them.** The most valuable paragraph, and the one people omit because
   it feels like admitting failure. It is the opposite — it is the part only someone who understood
   the data could write.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Why the leakage check comes before the interesting analysis.** Single-feature AUC across every
column, step 7 of the checklist, before any modelling. If something scores 0.98, everything you do
afterwards is about that column, and you need to know before you spend the afternoon.

**Why a fixed checklist rather than free exploration.** Free exploration finds what you were already
thinking about. A checklist finds what you were not — and it makes the EDA reproducible, comparable
across datasets, and automatable.

**Why the ECDF.** No bin-width choice, so it cannot mislead through binning, and you can read any
quantile off it. Use the ECDF when you need to be careful and the histogram when you need to
communicate — and **redraw any histogram with a different bin count before concluding anything from
its shape.**

**Why a domain classifier for drift, beyond per-column tests.** AUC is a bounded, interpretable
effect size — 0.5 identical, 1.0 trivially separable — so **no threshold re-tuning as the sample
grows**, which is precisely the war story's failure. It also catches multivariate drift that
univariate tests cannot: if `age` and `income` keep their marginals but their correlation reverses,
every per-column test passes.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Correctness** — MI and correlation both reported with the disagreements flagged; the drift
gate on both significance and effect size, with a test proving a 0.01 SD shift at large n is **not**
reported.

**3/3 on Explanation** — the memo opens with a recommendation, names specific columns as risks, and
states what the data cannot answer.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **Drift gated on p-value alone.** Fires on everything at scale; the module measured 100% on a
   0.01 SD shift at a million rows.
2. **Only Pearson correlation.** Blind to the parabola at r = −0.02, MI = 0.98.
3. **A memo that lists everything found.** The reader wanted a decision, not an inventory.

**A fourth:** reporting accuracy on an imbalanced target. At a 1% positive rate "always negative"
scores 99%. Precision-recall AUC, and always report the base rate alongside any metric.

**On the stretch challenge (calibrated drift detector).** The genuinely original contribution is
deriving the threshold **from measured model degradation** rather than from convention: inject shifts
of increasing magnitude, record where the downstream model's metric drops by more than 1%, and use
that as the alert threshold. A detector with a published detection surface and a threshold justified
that way is exactly what week 34 needs, and almost every team ships the version that gets muted.
