## Reference solution — `P2-W16-M5`

Rebuild a misleading chart honestly and write the critique.

### The critique that gets full marks

Not "it's misleading". A specific mechanism with a computed magnitude:

> **The original.** A bar chart of model accuracy: 94.2%, 94.6%, 95.1%, 95.4%. The y-axis runs from
> 94 to 95.5.
>
> **The mechanism.** Bars encode value as *length*. With the axis starting at 94, the drawn lengths
> are proportional to `value − 94` rather than to `value`, so the bars are 0.2, 0.6, 1.1 and 1.4
> units long — the last bar is **7× the first**. The values differ by **1.2 percentage points**.
> Tufte's lie factor — the ratio of the visual effect to the data effect — is approximately
> **(1.4/0.2) ÷ (95.4/94.2) = 7.0 ÷ 1.013 ≈ 6.9**.
>
> **What was not falsified.** The axis was labelled, the values were correct, and no number was
> altered. The chart was made in good faith by someone who wanted a small difference to be visible.
>
> **The consequence.** The next quarter's chart, drawn identically, showed 95.4% → 95.5% — a nearly
> flat bar. The visual story went from "rapid progress" to "we have plateaued" while the *rate of
> progress was unchanged*, and the credibility cost landed on the team that drew the honest chart.
>
> **The fix.** Bars from zero, values printed on the bars, and a title stating the magnitude —
> "range: 1.2 percentage points" — rather than the conclusion.

**Compute the lie factor.** It converts "this feels overstated" into a number, and the number is what
makes the critique a finding.

### The overplotting measurement

Same data, three methods:

| Points | Method | Time | PNG size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100,000 | scatter | 0.03 s | **75 KB** |
| 1,000,000 | scatter | 0.08 s | **61 KB** |
| 1,000,000 | hexbin | 0.07 s | 87 KB |
| 10,000,000 | scatter | — | *unusable* |
| 10,000,000 | **hexbin** | **0.66 s** | 93 KB |
| 10,000,000 | hist2d, log colour | 1.05 s | 41 KB |

**The interesting number is that the 1,000,000-point scatter PNG is *smaller* than the
100,000-point one.** The plot became a solid black region, which compresses well. **A file that
shrinks as you add data is a mechanical signal that your chart has stopped showing anything** — and
it is a genuinely useful diagnostic.

Note also that alpha blending only fixes occlusion, not saturation: with `alpha=0.1`, ten overlapping
points reach full opacity, so any region with more than ten points looks identical to one with ten
thousand. **Alpha has a hard ceiling of `1/alpha` points.**

### The audit questions

| Ask | Because |
| --- | --- |
| Does the y-axis start at zero? | required for bars and areas; optional for lines and scatters |
| Would a different axis range change the conclusion? | if yes, the conclusion is about your axis |
| Is the aspect ratio doing work? | tall and narrow exaggerates slope; wide and flat suppresses it |
| Are both axes labelled with units? | "accuracy" and "accuracy (%)" are different claims |
| Is the sample size shown? | a difference between n=8 and n=8,000 is a different finding |
| Would this survive greyscale? | if not, meaning is in colour alone — ~8% of men cannot read it |
| **Does the title state a fact or an interpretation?** | the title is the most persuasive and least auditable element |

**The rule about zero follows from the encoding, not from etiquette.** Ask what visual property
carries the meaning: **length or area → the scale must start at zero. Position → it need not.**
Insisting a latency line chart start at zero milliseconds is pedantry that destroys the chart, and
being wrong about the exception makes people stop listening to the rule.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Why change only one thing when rebuilding.** If you also switch from bars to a dot plot you have
changed two variables and the comparison is muddied. Change the dishonest choice, hold everything
else fixed, and the difference is attributable.

**Why print the values on the bars.** It makes axis manipulation self-defeating: the reader sees both
the visual and the number, and they cannot disagree. It is the cheapest structural defence available
and it belongs in a shared plotting module rather than in a style guide, because **a module changes
behaviour where a guide does not.**

**Why test figure metadata.** Most people do not test plots at all, and testing pixels is not worth
it. But **an unlabelled axis or an unmarked log scale is a correctness bug** — the reader draws a
wrong conclusion and no downstream check catches it. Asserting `ax.get_xlabel()` is non-empty, and
that a log scale says "log scale" in its label, is three lines and it covers the part that determines
whether the chart tells the truth.

**Why a dot plot is usually the honest answer for comparing values.** Cleveland and McGill measured
that people judge **position on a common scale** more accurately than **length**. A dot plot uses
position, so it does not require a zero baseline — which means it handles values in a narrow range
honestly, exactly the case the war story's bar chart got wrong. It is the chart people reach for
least and should reach for most.

**Why `viridis` and not `jet`.** `jet` is not lightness-monotonic: its bright cyan and yellow bands
make the eye perceive sharp boundaries where the data is smooth. Borkin et al. (2011) found switching
to a perceptually-uniform map **measurably improved diagnostic accuracy** in a clinical task — which
makes colormap choice a correctness issue rather than a style one.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Explanation** — the mechanism named specifically, the lie factor computed, and the
consequence traced through to what happens to the *next* chart.

**3/3 on Correctness** — the rebuild changes only the dishonest choice; the same numbers appear in
both versions.

**3/3 on Code quality** — a reusable plotting module with rcParams, a colorblind-safe palette, and
figure-metadata tests.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **"It's misleading" without a mechanism.** Which choice, and by how much? The lie factor is one
   division.
2. **Rebuilding with a different chart type.** Two variables changed; the comparison proves nothing.
3. **Not auditing one of their own charts.** The exercise that changes behaviour is the uncomfortable
   one, and it is the last step for a reason.

**A fourth:** applying the zero rule to a line chart. A submission that "corrects" a latency chart to
start at zero has learned the rule and not the reason. **Length and area need zero; position does
not.**

**On the stretch challenge (chart auditor).** The genuinely novel part is not the linter — it is
measuring **which violations actually mislead readers**. Cleveland and McGill's methodology is
directly reusable: show people the same data as an honest and a misleading chart and measure the
difference in their estimated effect size. A linter tells you a rule was broken; that experiment
tells you whether the rule matters, and which rules should be hard failures rather than advisory
notes.
