## Reference solution — `P2-W12-M5`

### The automated reversal check

```python
def simpson_check(df, group_col, outcome_col, segment_cols):
    agg = df.groupby(group_col)[outcome_col].mean()
    agg_dir = np.sign(agg.iloc[1] - agg.iloc[0])
    findings = []
    for seg in segment_cols:
        dirs, weights = [], []
        for _, sub in df.groupby(seg):
            if sub[group_col].nunique() < 2:
                continue
            m = sub.groupby(group_col)[outcome_col].mean()
            dirs.append(np.sign(m.iloc[1] - m.iloc[0]))
            weights.append(len(sub))
        dirs, weights = np.array(dirs), np.array(weights)
        reversed_share = weights[dirs == -agg_dir].sum() / weights.sum()
        if reversed_share > 0.5:
            findings.append((seg, reversed_share, len(dirs)))
    return sorted(findings, key=lambda t: -t[1])
```

Weighting by segment size rather than counting segments is deliberate: a reversal in two tiny segments
is not the same finding as a reversal covering most of your data.

### The correct Berkeley interpretation

> Aggregated across six departments, men were admitted at 44.5% and women at 30.4% — a 14.2-point gap
> with `chi2 = 91.6`, `p = 1.06e-21`. However, women had the **higher** admission rate in four of the
> six departments, and in the two where they did not the gap was under 4 points.
>
> The aggregate reverses because department is a confounder: the correlation between a department's
> overall admission rate and its share of women applicants is **−0.786**. Women applied
> disproportionately to competitive departments (C, E and F, with admission rates of 35%, 25% and 6%)
> while men applied to the less competitive ones (A and B, at 64% and 63%).
>
> The departmental data does not support the claim that admissions committees treated applicants
> differently by gender. It does raise a different and substantive question — why applicants of
> different genders applied to systematically different departments — which points at a completely
> different intervention.

### The wrong interpretation, written out so you can recognise it

> Berkeley admitted 44.5% of male applicants and 30.4% of female applicants (`p = 1e-21`). This is
> overwhelming statistical evidence of gender bias in graduate admissions. The admissions committees
> should be audited.

Every number in that paragraph is correct. The conclusion does not follow from them.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Reversals are weighted by data volume, not counted.** A reversal in 3 of 50 segments covering 2% of
the data is noise; a reversal covering 70% of the data is the finding.

**Spearman is the default in the correlation report.** Measured: one outlier in 501 points took Pearson
from 0.895 to 0.087 while Spearman held at 0.885. On real data with any chance of outliers, Pearson
alone is not safe.

**The expected number of spurious hits is reported alongside the correlation matrix.** For `k` features
there are `k(k−1)/2` pairs and about 5% of them will be "significant" on pure noise — measured, 257 of
4,950 at k = 100. Printing that expectation next to the results is what stops someone mining the
heatmap.

**Wrong turns worth knowing about.**

1. *Concluding causation from the aggregate.* The whole module.
2. *Concluding "no bias" from the departmental data.* Also wrong. The departmental data rules out one
   mechanism; it says nothing about why the application patterns differ, which may itself reflect
   structural factors.
3. *Controlling for everything available.* Colliders and mediators must not be controlled for.
   Controlling for a collider **creates** an association that was not there — measured: two independent
   variables go from `r = +0.001` to `r = −0.677` among the selected.
4. *Reading feature importance as an intervention list.* SHAP describes the model, not the world.

## Measured numbers this solution produces

**Berkeley 1973** (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, *Science* 187, 1975):

| Dept | Men rate | Women rate | Gap | Dept overall rate | % women applicants |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 62.1% | 82.4% | +20.3% | 64.4% | 11.6% |
| B | 63.0% | 68.0% | +5.0% | 63.2% | 4.3% |
| C | 36.9% | 34.1% | −2.9% | 35.1% | 64.6% |
| D | 33.1% | 34.9% | +1.8% | 34.0% | 47.3% |
| E | 27.7% | 23.9% | −3.8% | 25.2% | 67.3% |
| F | 5.9% | 7.0% | +1.1% | 6.4% | 47.8% |

Aggregate: 44.5% vs 30.4%, gap +14.2%, `chi2 = 91.6`, `p = 1.06e-21`.
Correlation(department admit rate, share of women applicants) = **−0.786**.

**Pearson vs Spearman:**

| Case | Pearson | Spearman |
|---|---|---|
| exponential `y = e^(x/2)` | 0.856 | 0.993 |
| parabola `y = x²` | −0.069 | −0.058 |
| linear + one outlier (501 points) | **0.087** | 0.885 |
| the same, outlier removed | 0.895 | — |

**Spurious correlations:** `P(|r| > 0.5)` on independent noise is 0.1386 at n=10, 0.0244 at n=20,
0.0001 at n=50.

**Multiple comparisons on pure noise:** 100 variables → 4,950 pairs → **257 with p &lt; 0.05** (5.19%),
0 surviving Bonferroni.

**Berkson:** two independent variables, `r = +0.0011` in the population, `r = −0.6771` among the 14.5%
selected on their sum.

**Confounded treatment example:** aggregate treated 58.6% vs control 80.0%; within strata, treated
93.0% vs 90.0% (mild) and 50.0% vs 40.0% (severe) — better in both.

## Grading notes

**What 3/3 looks like**

- *Correctness* — `simpson_check` flags department on the Berkeley data and returns nothing on data with
  no reversal; the Berkson demo reproduces a strong negative correlation from independent inputs.
- *Code quality* — the reversal check is generic over any group/outcome/segment columns, not hard-coded
  to this dataset.
- *Performance* — the correlation report applies Benjamini-Hochberg and prints the expected spurious
  count.
- *Explanation* — **both** interpretations written out, correct and incorrect, so the reader can see the
  shape of the plausible error.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points**

1. **Only the correct interpretation written.** Writing the wrong one deliberately is what makes the
   error recognisable later, and it is explicitly asked for.
2. **The confounder identified but not quantified.** "Department is a confounder" is weaker than
   "department admit rate correlates −0.786 with the share of women applicants".
3. **Overcorrecting to "there was no bias".** The departmental data rules out one mechanism. It does not
   establish that nothing was wrong, and a submission that claims it has swapped one unsupported
   conclusion for another.
