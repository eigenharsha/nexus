## Reference solution — `P2-W14-M4`

### The measurement, and why it is smaller than the textbook implies

1,200 × 1,200 grid (1,440,000 cells), 20% randomly blocked, corner to corner, 4-directional
movement, unit cost, Manhattan heuristic:

| Algorithm | Path cost | Expansions | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dijkstra (`h = 0`) | 2,398 | 1,177,499 | 1.04 s |
| A* with Manhattan `h` | 2,398 | **792,399** | **0.87 s** |
| | *identical* | **1.49× fewer** | 1.19× faster |

**1.49×, not 10×.** The reasons, and a strong write-up states all three:

1. **The goal is in the far corner**, so almost the whole grid lies "between" start and goal in
   Manhattan terms. A* prunes the region *beyond* the goal and the strips outside the bounding
   rectangle — a minority of this grid. **The benefit depends on the geometry of the query.**
2. **20% obstacles weaken the heuristic.** Manhattan distance ignores walls, so in cluttered regions
   the true remaining cost far exceeds `h`, and a heuristic well below the truth gives little
   guidance.
3. **Ties are everywhere.** Unit costs mean vast numbers of nodes share an `f`. The default
   tie-break fans the search across the whole equal-`f` plateau.

**The time ratio (1.19×) is smaller than the expansion ratio (1.49×)** because A* computes `h` for
every neighbour and pushes 3-tuples. Even a two-subtraction heuristic eats 20% of the win — which is
the argument for measuring expansions as the primary metric.

### Getting to 3× (the `hard` track)

Plain Manhattan gives 1.49%, so the requirement is genuinely not met by the obvious solution. Three
legitimate routes, and **they are not equivalent**:

| Route | Reduction | Keeps optimality? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tie-break toward larger `g` — push `(f, -g, node_id)` | substantial on plateau-heavy grids | **yes**, and it is free |
| ALT: landmarks + triangle inequality | 5–20× typical | **yes** |
| Weighted A*, `f = g + w·h`, `w ≈ 1.5–2.0` | largest | **no** — bounded `w`× suboptimal |

**Combining the first two is the intended solution**, because it reaches the target without changing
the problem. Weighted A* is a legitimate engineering choice and **must be labelled as a different
guarantee**, with the *measured* suboptimality reported alongside the theoretical bound. In routing
the measured value is usually far better than `w`, and quantifying that gap is worth marks.

The ALT proof, which is two paragraphs:

> For any landmark `L`, the triangle inequality gives `d(L, goal) ≤ d(L, n) + d(n, goal)`.
> Rearranging, `d(n, goal) ≥ d(L, goal) − d(L, n)`. The same holds with the roles reversed, so
> `d(n, goal) ≥ |d(L, goal) − d(L, n)|`. Taking the maximum over all landmarks preserves the bound,
> therefore `h(n) = max_L |d(L, goal) − d(L, n)| ≤ d(n, goal)`, and `h` is admissible. ∎

### The admissibility test nobody writes

```python
def test_heuristic_is_admissible_on_every_sampled_node(net):
    """Admissibility is a property of your heuristic AGAINST THE ACTUAL GRAPH.
    You cannot assert it by reading the formula."""
    true_cost = dijkstra_all(net, goal)          # ground truth
    for n in random.sample(list(net.adj), 1000):
        if math.isinf(true_cost[n]):
            continue
        assert net.heuristic(n, goal) <= true_cost[n] + 1e-9, (
            f"INADMISSIBLE at {n}: h={net.heuristic(n, goal):.4f} "
            f"> true={true_cost[n]:.4f}")
```

**This test would have prevented the module's war story**, where a heuristic of
`haversine_km / average_speed` overestimated by 37% on motorway segments and A* silently returned
routes 15–20 minutes too long for four months. A unit mismatch, a speed constant that is an average
rather than a maximum, or a one-way road that makes the straight line unreachable all break
admissibility, and none are visible in the formula.

**Divide by the *maximum* speed anywhere in the network, not the average.** Admissibility is a
statement about the worst case over the whole graph.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Why lazy deletion rather than an indexed heap.** Textbook Dijkstra uses `decrease_key`, which needs
a `node → heap position` map maintained across every swap — two structures kept consistent, which is
a bug surface. Lazy deletion pushes a new entry and skips stale ones with `if d > dist[node]:
continue`. More heap entries, far less code, and it is what almost every real implementation does.

**Why early-exit at the goal.** When `goal` is popped its distance is final, by the same argument
that makes Dijkstra correct at all. Continuing only computes distances to nodes nobody asked about.
On a large graph this is a large, free saving.

**Why expansions rather than wall time as the primary metric.** Expansions are deterministic and
reproduce exactly on any machine; wall time does not. Report both, lead with expansions.

**Why average over 100 origin/destination pairs.** The expansion ratio varies enormously with
geometry — a short route gives an enormous ratio, corner-to-corner gives 1.49×. **A single-pair
result is noise**, and a `hard` submission claiming 3× from one pair has measured nothing.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Correctness** — A* and Dijkstra return identical costs, asserted; the unreachable case
returns `(inf, [])` rather than raising; heuristic admissibility tested against ground truth on
≥1,000 sampled nodes.

**3/3 on Performance** — expansions and time across ≥100 random pairs, with the distribution rather
than a single number.

**3/3 on Explanation** — the admissibility proof sketch, and — if weighted A* was used — the measured
suboptimality distribution compared against the theoretical `w` bound.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **A unit mismatch in the heuristic** — distance where the cost is time. Silently returns
   suboptimal paths with no error. The admissibility test catches it in one run.
2. **Claiming 3× from a single origin/destination pair.** Average over 100.
3. **Weighted A* presented as if it were optimal.** It is a different guarantee. Say so, and report
   the measured suboptimality.

**On the bidirectional A* stretch.** The naive termination condition — stop when the searches meet —
returns suboptimal paths, and the bug is invisible without a ground-truth comparison. You must
continue until the best forward-plus-backward distance is no worse than the sum of the two
frontiers' minimums. **A submission that found this bug by comparing against Dijkstra and then fixed
it has done the single best exercise in the week.**
