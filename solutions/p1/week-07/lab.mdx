### The seed that makes the analytics meaningful

The single most important design decision in the whole lab is the shape of the generated data. A
seed where every customer has exactly one order produces a retention matrix of zeros and a
gaps-and-islands query that returns nothing — and half of every cohort's submissions have exactly
that problem.

```sql
-- realistic repeat-purchase behaviour: a geometric-ish order count per customer,
-- with 12-52 day gaps between orders
CREATE TABLE orders AS
WITH cust AS (
  SELECT g AS customer_id,
         date '2024-01-01' + (random() * 300)::int      AS signup,
         1 + (-4.0 * ln(1 - random()))::int             AS n_orders    -- mean ~5
  FROM generate_series(1, 150000) g)
SELECT row_number() OVER () AS id,
       c.customer_id,
       (c.signup + (s.k * (12 + (random() * 40)::int)))::timestamptz AS placed_at,
       (ARRAY['paid','paid','paid','refunded','cancelled'])[1 + (random() * 4)::int] AS status,
       (1000 + random() * 250000)::bigint AS total_cents
FROM   cust c, LATERAL generate_series(0, c.n_orders - 1) AS s(k);
```

Measured: 749,741 orders across 150,000 customers in **1,989 ms**, index build 1,066 ms,
`ANALYZE` 224 ms. The resulting retention matrix has a real shape — m1 63–67%, m2 47–50%,
m3 38–39%, m6 17–18% — which is what makes every downstream query worth writing.

`make check-grain` parses those headers, runs each query, and asserts the row count and any declared

are trustworthy.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Wrong turn 1: seeding with an ORM.** Measured, 100,000 rows took 83.8 s as auto-committed inserts
against 0.295 s with `COPY` — 284×. At the lab's million rows that is roughly 14 minutes against
3 seconds, and at `SCALE=10` it is over two hours against 30 seconds.

**Wrong turn 2: a seed with no repeat purchases.** The cohort query returns zeros and looks broken.
Diagnosing it wastes an hour, and the fix is in the data generator, not the SQL.

**Wrong turn 3: joining to `products.price_cents` for historical revenue.** Every price change
rewrites history. `unit_cents` captured on the order line is not denormalization — it is the price
agreed at sale time, which is a different fact.

**Wrong turn 4: offset pagination for the order history page.** It passes at page 1 and fails the
depth test. Measured: `OFFSET 500000 LIMIT 20` took **693.9 ms** even with a matching index (it
produced and discarded 500,000 rows), while keyset pagination at the same depth took **2.1 ms** —
408×, and flat rather than linear.

**Wrong turn 5: speculative indexes.** `make verify` fails on any index with `idx_scan = 0` after
the benchmark suite. Every index costs write throughput (2.02× measured for five) and disk
(13 MB against 8.2 MB of heap). An index you cannot attach to a named query is pure cost.

**Wrong turn 6: no grain declaration.** At least one of the twenty queries will have a fan-out bug.
Measured on the seeded data: 5,002 orders became 15,000 rows after joining items, and `SUM` went
from 507,507,500 to 1,522,492,500 — a silent 3.0×. The header convention plus `make check-grain`
catches it mechanically.

## Grading notes

**A 3/3 on correctness**: every query's grain declared and asserted; the retention invariant tested;
NULL semantics handled (`NOT EXISTS`, `count(col)`); and the schema rejects adversarial data —
duplicate emails, refunds exceeding payments, orders with no items, ties in every ranking.

**A 3/3 on code quality**: constraints express the business rules so the wrong data cannot be
stored; queries are CTE-structured and commented with their grain; the seed is reproducible from a
fixed random seed.

**A 3/3 on performance**: every `hard`-track target met, `INDEXES.md` complete with before/after and
write cost, and `PERFORMANCE.md` includes something that did not work.

**A 3/3 on explanation**: defends one denormalization with its stated cost, and explains the
keyset-versus-offset choice with the measured numbers.

**The six most common ways submissions lose points**

1. Seeding with `INSERT`, so `SCALE=10` never finishes.
2. A seed with no repeat purchases, making half the analytics meaningless.
3. At least one fan-out bug, undeclared and undetected.
4. Offset pagination on the history page.
5. Indexes with no named query — write cost with no read benefit.
6. `EXPLAIN` output committed with no commentary. The plan is evidence; the analysis is the deliverable.
