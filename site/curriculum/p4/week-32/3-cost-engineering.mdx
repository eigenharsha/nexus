## The mental model

Every cost decision is a point on a **cost/quality frontier**, and the only honest axis is money per
completed job. Some levers are free — caching, trimming, routing mechanical steps — and move you
straight down. Others buy quality with money, or money with quality, and those you must measure.
The frontier is your system's, not the industry's, and plotting it is the work.

### The words, defined

- **Token.** The unit of billing. Roughly 3–4 characters of English.
- **Input tokens.** Everything you send: system prompt, tool schemas, conversation, retrieved documents. Usually 90%+ of the tokens.
- **Output tokens.** What the model generates. Priced 5× higher than input on every model in this course.
- **Prompt caching.** Paying about 0.1× for a prefix you have sent before, and about 1.25× the first time to write it. Week 29 M3.
- **Semantic cache.** Returning a stored answer for a question that is *similar* to one you have answered. Week 28.
- **Routing.** Sending easy work to a cheap model and hard work to an expensive one.
- **Cost per run.** Total spend divided by runs. The number that is easy to compute and easy to be fooled by.
- **Cost per successful task.** Total spend divided by *runs that met their acceptance criterion*. The number that matters.

### The analogy

A courier charges by the parcel. One firm is £3 a parcel and delivers 95% of the time; another is
£2 and delivers 70%. The cheap firm looks 33% cheaper. But a failed delivery means you send it
again, so your real cost per *delivered* parcel is £3.16 against £2.86 — closer than it looked — and
that is before the customer complaint, the refund and the reputation.

**Price per attempt is the sticker. Price per outcome is the bill.**

### Price one run by hand

Do this once, manually, before you automate anything. Take a real run's token counts from your
traces (Module 1) and a price table.

| Model | Input $/1M | Output $/1M |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `claude-opus-5` | $5.00 | $25.00 |
| `claude-sonnet-5` | $2.00 | $10.00 |
| `claude-haiku-4-5` | $1.00 | $5.00 |

One run of the incident agent, five model-calling nodes:

| Node | Model | Input tokens | Output tokens | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| triage | opus-5 | 2,100 | 180 | 2,100 × $5/1M + 180 × $25/1M = **$0.0150** |
| gather | opus-5 | 6,800 | 520 | $0.0340 + $0.0130 = **$0.0470** |
| diagnose | opus-5 | 9,000 | 800 | $0.0450 + $0.0200 = **$0.0650** |
| propose | opus-5 | 6,400 | 480 | $0.0320 + $0.0120 = **$0.0440** |
| verify | opus-5 | 3,100 | 140 | $0.0155 + $0.0035 = **$0.0190** |
| **Total** | | **27,400** | **2,120** | **$0.1900** |

Two observations that shape everything:

- **Input is 72% of the cost** ($0.137 of $0.190) despite output being priced 5× higher, because there is 13× more input. Cost engineering is mostly input engineering.
- **`diagnose` is 34% of the run** and `triage` is 8%. Optimize where the money is. People start with the prompt they find annoying, not the one that costs.

At 75% success, cost per successful task is $0.190 / 0.75 = **$0.253**.

```python
# price.py — do it in code once you have done it by hand
PRICES = {"claude-opus-5": (5e-6, 25e-6),
          "claude-sonnet-5": (2e-6, 10e-6),
          "claude-haiku-4-5": (1e-6, 5e-6)}

def cost(model: str, usage) -> float:
    cin, cout = PRICES[model]
    return (usage.input_tokens * cin
            + usage.output_tokens * cout
            + getattr(usage, "cache_creation_input_tokens", 0) * cin * 1.25
            + getattr(usage, "cache_read_input_tokens", 0) * cin * 0.1)
```

**Cache tokens are billed separately and are not included in `input_tokens`.** A cost function that
ignores them under-reports a cached workload by 40–80%, always in the flattering direction. This is
the single most common bug in home-made cost dashboards.

<Accordion title="Checkpoint — 5 questions before you continue">

1. **Why is input usually the dominant cost despite output being priced higher?** There is far more of it — 13× in our run — because you re-send system prompt, tools and history every step.
2. **Where do cache tokens appear?** In their own usage fields, not in `input_tokens`. Omitting them makes a cached workload look 40–80% cheaper than it is.
3. **What is cost per successful task?** Total spend divided by runs that met their acceptance criterion.
4. **Why does the cheapest model not always win?** Because failures are re-attempted or escalated, and the extra cost lands on the successful runs.
5. **Where should you start optimizing?** The node with the largest share of spend — `diagnose` at 34%, not `triage` at 8%.

### Attribution: cost per step, from traces

You instrumented this in Module 1. Now query it.

```sql
-- Cost per node per run, and each node's share. Ninety seconds of work, and it
-- decides where the next two weeks go.
WITH per_run AS (
  SELECT trace_id,
         node,
         sum(cost_usd)                             AS node_cost,
         sum(sum(cost_usd)) OVER (PARTITION BY trace_id) AS run_cost
  FROM   spans WHERE name LIKE 'chat %' GROUP BY trace_id, node)
SELECT node,
       count(*)                        AS runs,
       round(avg(node_cost)::numeric, 4)  AS avg_cost,
       round(avg(node_cost / run_cost)::numeric, 3) AS share,
       round(percentile_cont(0.95) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY node_cost)::numeric, 4) AS p95
FROM per_run GROUP BY node ORDER BY avg_cost DESC;
```

Add the p95 column and read it. Ours: `diagnose` averaged $0.065 with a p95 of $0.214 — a long tail
where the bounded retry loop ran three times. **The tail is often a bigger prize than the mean**, and
it is invisible in an average.

### The five levers, measured

Each applied cumulatively to the incident system, quality checked against the Week-31 120-case suite
after every step. "No regression" means within the suite's detectable difference (Week 31 M1: 11
points at 100 cases, so we also ran the 800-case nightly suite for anything that looked close).

| # | Lever | Cost/run | Success | **Cost per successful task** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Baseline | $0.190 | 75.0% | **$0.253** |
| 1 | Prompt caching on the stable prefix | $0.143 | 75.0% | **$0.191** |
| 2 | Route `triage` and `verify` to `claude-haiku-4-5` | $0.128 | 74.2% | **$0.173** |
| 3 | Evidence digests in context instead of full text | $0.104 | 75.0% | **$0.139** |
| 4 | Semantic cache on repeated incidents (31% hit rate) | $0.076 | 75.8% | **$0.100** |
| 5 | **Verify-and-retry on `diagnose`** | $0.084 | 86.1% | **$0.098** |
| — | (counter-example) route `diagnose` to haiku too | **$0.058** | 54.0% | **$0.107** |

**61% reduction in cost per successful task**, and note the two rows that break the pattern.

**Row 5 costs more per run and less per task.** Adding a verification retry raised run cost by
$0.008 (it fires on 18% of runs at about $0.045 each) and raised success from 75.8% to 86.1%. Anyone
optimizing cost per *call* would have deleted this. It is the single best change in the table.

**The counter-example row is the cheapest per call and the worst per task.** $0.058 a run looks like
a 69% saving. Success collapses to 54%, and $0.058 / 0.54 = $0.107 — worse than the $0.098 we
actually shipped, and vastly worse in product terms. This is the row to put in front of anyone who
proposes a blanket model downgrade.

Now the levers themselves.

**1. Prompt caching.** The stable prefix is the system prompt plus tool schemas: 2,800 tokens,
byte-identical across all five calls in a run.

```python
r = client.messages.create(
    model="claude-opus-5", max_tokens=2000,
    system=[{"type": "text", "text": STABLE_SYSTEM,        # frozen. No timestamps.
             "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral"}}],
    tools=sorted_tools,                                     # deterministic order (Week 29 M2)
    messages=history + [{"role": "user", "content": question}])
assert r.usage.cache_read_input_tokens > 0 or first_call_in_run   # verify it, always
```

Uncached: 5 × 2,800 × $5/1M = $0.070. Cached: one write at 1.25× ($0.0175) plus four reads at 0.1×
($0.0056) = $0.0231. **Saving $0.047 per run, 25% of the total, for a five-line change.** The assert
matters: a single varying byte silently takes you back to $0.070 and nothing else tells you.

**2. Routing.** Send mechanical steps to a cheap model, and *measure the step*, not the system.

```python
NODE_MODELS = {
    "triage":   "claude-haiku-4-5",   # classification against a fixed taxonomy
    "verify":   "claude-haiku-4-5",   # did the signal recover? yes/no
    "gather":   "claude-opus-5",      # tool selection over 11 tools — needs the strong model
    "diagnose": "claude-opus-5",      # the judgement step. Do not touch this.
    "propose":  "claude-opus-5",      # produces an action a human approves
}
```

The rule that emerged from measuring each node separately: **route steps whose output is a
constrained choice; keep the strong model where the output is free-form judgement.** Triage picks
one of four severities and haiku matched opus on 118 of 120 cases. Diagnose writes a causal
explanation and haiku matched on 71 of 120.

**3. Context trimming.** `gather` was putting full retrieved documents into the prompt. Replacing
them with 400-character digests plus an id the model can expand on demand cut input tokens on
`gather` and `diagnose` by 38%, saving $0.024 a run, with success unchanged at 75.0%. The
expansion tool was used in 9% of runs, which is the honest cost of the trade and is included above.

**4. Semantic cache.** Week 28's machinery: embed the incoming request, look for a stored answer
within a cosine threshold, serve it if found.

```python
def cached_or_run(request: str) -> tuple[str, bool]:
    vec = embed(request)                                   # bge-m3, 1024-dim
    hit = vector_store.nearest(vec, threshold=0.93)
    if hit and not is_stale(hit, max_age_minutes=30):      # incidents go stale FAST
        return hit.answer, True
    answer = run_agent(request)
    vector_store.put(vec, answer, ttl_minutes=30)
    return answer, False
```

**31% hit rate** on our traffic, because incident alerts repeat. Saving $0.028 a run on average. Two
things that must be stated: the **1.2% false-hit rate** at threshold 0.93 — cases where a
similar-looking but materially different incident got a stale answer — and the fact that a 30-minute
TTL is what keeps that acceptable. We measured the threshold sweep and 0.93 was where false hits
crossed our 1% tolerance. **A semantic cache is a classifier and it needs the Week 31 M5 treatment:
a threshold, an error rate, and a stated budget.**

**5. Verify-and-retry.** Week 29 M1's arithmetic, now as a cost lever. The groundedness validator
from Week 31 M3 runs on `diagnose`'s output; a failure triggers one retry with the specific problem
fed back. Fires on 18% of runs, costs $0.045 when it fires, raises success 10.3 points.

### War story

A team was told to cut LLM spend 40%. They moved every call from the strong model to a mid-tier one
and hit the target on the invoice in a fortnight. Nobody measured quality, because there was no eval
suite.

Over the next six weeks the support queue grew 22% and the assistant's thumbs-down rate went from
4% to 11%. Cost per *call* was down 47%. Cost per successfully-resolved ticket — reconstructed later
from the ticketing system — was **up 19%**, because more tickets needed a second interaction and
more escalated to a human, and a human costs about 400× a model call.

The recovery took a quarter and its first step was building the eval suite that should have existed
first. The final configuration used the strong model on two of five nodes and a cheap model on
three, plus caching, and beat the original on both cost and quality. **The direction was right and
the method was wrong**: they optimized the number they could see instead of the number that mattered,
and they had no instrument that would have told them.

<Accordion title="Design checkpoint — with model answer">

**Question.** Your PM says the LLM bill must halve by the end of the quarter. Where do you start?

**Model answer.** Three questions before any change, and then a specific order.

First: **halve which number?** If it is the invoice, that is achievable and possibly harmful. If it is
cost per successful task, that is the right target and I would get it written that way, because it
protects both of us later.

Second: **is there an eval suite?** If not, that is week one, non-negotiable. Every lever below is a
change whose quality effect must be measured, and without the suite I would be doing what the war
story did.

Third: **where is the money?** Per-node cost attribution from traces, with a p95 column. In our
system 34% was one node and the p95 was 3.3× the mean, so there were two distinct problems — the
mean and the tail — and the tail turned out to be a retry loop, which was a correctness issue
wearing a cost costume.

Then, in this order, because it is free-wins-first: **caching** (25% for five lines, no quality
risk); **context trimming** (13%, and it usually improves quality slightly by removing distractors —
Week 31 M3's precision finding); **routing the constrained-choice steps** (8%, measured per node,
never blanket); **semantic caching** if your traffic repeats (15%, and it needs a threshold and an
error budget); and only then anything that trades quality.

What I would *not* do first, and would push back on: a blanket model downgrade. It is the most
visible lever and, per the counter-example row, it can raise cost per successful task while lowering
the invoice. If the pressure is severe I would offer the counter-example table as the reason, and
offer the target in the right units instead.

</Accordion>

### Unit economics: the frontier is yours, not the industry's

Plot your own configurations. Ours, eleven of them, on the 120-case suite plus a 400-run production
sample:

| Configuration | Cost/run | Success | **Per successful task** | On the frontier? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All haiku | $0.058 | 54.0% | $0.107 | No — dominated |
| All haiku + caching | $0.041 | 54.0% | $0.076 | **Yes** |
| All sonnet | $0.094 | 68.5% | $0.137 | No — dominated |
| Baseline (all opus) | $0.190 | 75.0% | $0.253 | No — dominated |
| Opus + caching | $0.143 | 75.0% | $0.191 | No — dominated |
| Mixed routing + caching | $0.128 | 74.2% | $0.173 | No — dominated |
| + trimming | $0.104 | 75.0% | $0.139 | No — dominated |
| + semantic cache | $0.076 | 75.8% | $0.100 | **Yes** |
| **+ verify-and-retry** | $0.084 | 86.1% | **$0.098** | **Yes** |
| + critic-refiner (Week 30 M2) | $0.171 | 89.4% | $0.191 | **Yes** |
| + critic on every run, no cache | $0.294 | 89.4% | $0.329 | No — dominated |

Six of eleven configurations are **dominated** — something else is both cheaper and better. That is
the normal state of an unexamined system and it is why plotting the frontier is worth an afternoon:
most of the wins are not trades at all.

The three frontier points are the actual menu: cheap-and-adequate at $0.076 per success, the
balanced $0.098, and quality-at-a-price at $0.191. Which you choose is a product decision, and the
useful thing you can hand a product owner is those three points rather than a recommendation.

**Success rate enters the denominator, so it is a cost lever with unusually strong leverage.** Going
from 75.8% to 86.1% while adding $0.008 of spend improved cost per success by 2%. Going from 86.1%
to 89.4% cost $0.087 and made it 95% worse. There is a point where buying quality with money stops
paying, and it is specific to your acceptance criteria — find it rather than assuming.

### Capacity planning and rate limits

Cost is not the only budget. At 4,200 runs a day with a p50 of 27,400 input and 2,120 output tokens:

```
input:  4,200 × 27,400  = 115.1 M tokens/day  = 3.45 B/month
output: 4,200 ×  2,120  =   8.9 M tokens/day  = 0.27 B/month
peak:   traffic is 3.1x the mean between 09:00 and 11:00 local
        peak input rate = 115.1M / 86400 × 3.1 ≈ 4,130 tokens/second
```

Two things follow. **Your rate limit must cover peak, not mean** — sizing on the daily average
under-provisions by 3.1× and the failure appears as 429s during your busiest two hours, which is
when it is most visible. And **queue with back-pressure rather than retrying blindly**: a 429 storm
where every client retries immediately makes the limit permanent. Module 4 covers the queue; the
point here is that the capacity calculation is a cost-engineering output, because reserved or
committed capacity is priced differently from on-demand and the decision needs this number.

Caching interacts with this pleasantly: our 25% input-token reduction from prompt caching was also a
25% reduction in peak token rate, which moved us from 92% of our limit at peak to 69%.

### The build-versus-buy crossover, recomputed

Week 26 asked whether to serve your own model. Now recompute it with real numbers, for one step
rather than the whole system.

**Candidate: the `triage` step**, a four-way classification, currently `claude-haiku-4-5` at
$0.0030 per run.

Self-hosted alternative: an 8B model fine-tuned on 4,000 labelled triage examples, served on vLLM on
one A10G at $0.62/hour on-demand. The crossover below is **derived** — arithmetic over a published
hourly price and a token-throughput figure; the throughput itself is a **reference figure** for an
A10G (modelled, see [About the numbers](/start/about-the-numbers)), so re-run the division with your own measured tokens/second before you
take this to a budget meeting.

```
Fixed cost:      $0.62/h × 730 h = $452.60/month  (single instance, no redundancy)
Throughput:      ~2,300 tokens/s at batch 16 for an 8B model on A10G
Triage tokens:   2,100 in + 180 out = 2,280 → ~1.0 s of GPU time per run
Marginal cost:   $0.62 / 3600 ≈ $0.00017 per run
Crossover:       452.60 / (0.0030 − 0.00017) = 159,900 runs/month ≈ 5,300/day
```

**We run 4,200/day — 21% below the crossover.** So the honest answer today is: do not self-host this
step. At our growth rate we cross it in about five months, and the decision is diarized rather than
argued about.

Three costs the arithmetic above omits, and you should name them when you present it:

- **Redundancy.** One instance means a single point of failure. Two instances doubles the fixed cost and moves the crossover to 10,600 runs/day.
- **The fine-tune itself.** 4,000 labelled examples, roughly three engineer-days, plus about $40 of GPU. Amortized over a year that is another ~$150/month equivalent, moving the crossover again.
- **Ongoing ownership.** Model updates, dependency upgrades, quality drift on a model you now own. Call it two engineer-hours a month.

With all three, the real crossover is nearer 12,000 runs/day. **The naive crossover flatters
self-hosting by about 2.3×**, and that gap is where most "we should host our own" proposals live.
What genuinely changes the answer: a data-residency requirement that makes hosted impossible, or a
latency requirement that a local model meets and a hosted one does not (our triage p50 was 240 ms
hosted against 90 ms local).

### Budget alerts that are worth having

| Alert | Threshold | Why this one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost per successful task, 24 h rolling | above 1.3× the 7-day median | Catches quality regressions and cost regressions with one number |
| Spend in the last 5 minutes | above 4× the 5-minute median | The Week 30 M4 fleet guard. Catches runaway loops in minutes, not at month end. |
| Cache read ratio | below 60% of the 7-day median | A silent cache invalidation is a 25% cost increase with no other symptom |
| p95 cost per run | above 3× the mean | The expensive tail — usually a retry loop, i.e. a correctness bug |
| Semantic cache false-hit rate | above 1% on the sampled audit | A cache serving wrong answers is worse than no cache |

The first one is the important one and it is the module's thesis as an alert: **a single metric that
moves when either cost or quality degrades**, so nobody has to remember to look at both.

### Papers and primary sources

- **Anthropic pricing and prompt-caching documentation.** Takeaway: cache reads are ~0.1× and writes ~1.25×, billed in separate usage fields — so a cost function that only reads `input_tokens` under-reports a cached workload by 40–80%, and always in the direction that makes you look good.
- **Chen et al., 2023 — *FrugalGPT: How to Use Large Language Models While Reducing Cost and Improving Performance*.** Takeaway: a cascade that tries a cheap model first and escalates on a learned confidence signal can cut cost substantially at equal accuracy — the important subtlety being that the escalation *scorer* is the hard part, and a bad one turns the cascade into paying twice.
- **Ong et al., 2024 — *RouteLLM: Learning to Route LLMs with Preference Data*.** Takeaway: routing can be learned from preference data rather than hand-written rules, and their results show most of the gain comes from correctly identifying the small fraction of genuinely hard queries — which is why our per-node routing beat a blanket downgrade.
- **Week 26's serving material and vLLM's throughput documentation.** Takeaway: the self-hosting crossover is dominated by fixed cost and utilization, so a step you call 4,200 times a day and a step you call 400,000 times a day are entirely different decisions with the same model.

### Stretch challenge

Cut your system's cost per successful task by at least 50% with no measured quality regression on the
Week-31 suite, and publish the before/after table with every intermediate step.

Beyond that: **plot your full cost/quality frontier** with at least eight configurations and identify
the dominated ones. Then answer the question that decides your product's economics: at what success
rate does buying quality with money stop paying? For us, spending $0.087 to move from 86.1% to 89.4%
made cost per success 95% worse — but if a failure costs a human 20 minutes at $60/hour, that is $20
of human time per failure, and the arithmetic reverses completely: at 4,200 runs/day the extra 3.3
points of success saves 139 failures a day, about $2,780 of human time, against $365 of extra model
spend.

**So the honest unit is not cost per successful task either — it is total cost per task including the
cost of failure.** Compute that for your system: what does a failure actually cost, in human time,
in customer churn, in an incident? Then re-plot the frontier with that number in it and see which
configuration wins. We suspect, and cannot prove, that most teams optimizing model spend are
optimizing the smaller of two numbers. Find out for yours, and show your working — including how you
estimated the cost of a failure, which is the part that will be argued with.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Common mistakes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Invoice falls, support load rises | Optimized cost per call, not per successful task | Every table gets the per-success column; gate every change on the eval suite |
| Cost dashboard reads 40% low | Cache read and write tokens not priced | They are separate usage fields at 0.1× and 1.25× |
| Caching stops working silently | A varying byte in the prefix, or tool order changed | Assert `cache_read_input_tokens > 0` after the first call of a run |
| Blanket model downgrade regresses quality | Routing decided globally rather than per node | Route constrained-choice steps; keep judgement steps on the strong model, measured per node |
| Semantic cache serves a wrong answer | No threshold sweep, no TTL | Sweep to a stated false-hit budget; TTL matched to how fast your domain goes stale |
| Optimizing the wrong node | No per-node attribution | Attribute from traces; ours was 34% in one node and 8% in the one people wanted to fix |
| A cost spike is found at month end | No short-window spend alert | 5-minute rolling spend at 4× the median, plus the Week 30 M4 fleet guard |
| Self-hosting proposal looks obviously right | Naive crossover ignoring redundancy, the fine-tune and ownership | Ours flattered self-hosting by 2.3×; recompute with all three |
| 429s during the busy two hours | Rate limits sized on the daily mean | Size on peak; ours is 3.1× the mean |
| The p95 cost is 3× the mean and nobody looks | Averages hide retry loops | Alert on p95 cost per run; it is usually a correctness bug |

## Assessment

<AccordionGroup>
<Accordion title="8 concept questions (with answers)" icon="circle-question">

1. *Why cost per successful task rather than per call?* Failures are re-attempted or escalated, so their cost lands on the successful runs; the cheapest per call can be the most expensive per outcome.
2. *A model at one-fifth the price drops success 75% → 40%. Better or worse?* $0.038/0.40 = $0.095 against $0.253 — better on this metric, and you must then ask what a failure costs beyond model spend.
3. *Why is input usually the dominant cost?* Volume: 27,400 input against 2,120 output in our run, because prompt, tools and history are re-sent every step.
4. *What does prompt caching cost and save?* ~1.25× to write, ~0.1× to read; 25% of our run cost for five lines.
5. *Which steps can be routed to a cheap model?* Ones whose output is a constrained choice. Haiku matched Opus on 118/120 triage cases and 71/120 diagnosis cases.
6. *What makes a semantic cache dangerous?* It is a classifier with a false-hit rate — ours 1.2% at threshold 0.93 — so it needs a threshold, an error budget and a TTL.
7. *Why did verify-and-retry improve cost per successful task while raising cost per run?* It moved the denominator more than the numerator: +$0.008 for +10.3 points of success.
8. *What does the naive self-hosting crossover omit?* Redundancy, the fine-tune amortization and ongoing ownership; together they moved ours from 5,300 to ~12,000 runs/day.

</Accordion>

## Sources & further reading

**Primary docs**
- [Anthropic pricing and prompt-caching documentation](https://docs.claude.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-caching) — read the usage-field definitions; the separate cache fields are where home-made cost dashboards go wrong.
- [vLLM throughput documentation and Week 26's serving material](https://docs.vllm.ai/) — read this before any self-hosting crossover calculation; utilization dominates the answer.

**Papers**
- [Chen et al. 2023, *FrugalGPT*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176) — cascades cut cost at equal accuracy, and the escalation scorer is the hard part; a bad one means paying twice.
- [Ong et al. 2024, *RouteLLM*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665) — routing learned from preference data, with most of the gain coming from identifying the small fraction of genuinely hard queries.

**Go deeper**
- Week 31 M1 on statistical power, re-read here — "no quality regression" is a claim your suite may not be able to support, and knowing the smallest difference you can detect is what makes the claim honest.
