## The ticket

> **NEX-1101 · ML Platform · P2**
>
> New engineers keep shipping numerical bugs that our tests do not catch: cosine similarity computed
> on unnormalised vectors, a custom loss whose gradient is off by a constant factor, a PCA fitted on
> unscaled features. Every one of these produced a *plausible* training curve and a broken model.
>
> Build `nanomath`: a small, dependency-free numerical library with the operations these bugs live in,
> every one tested against NumPy to 1e-6, plus a `gradcheck` harness. It is a teaching artefact first
> and a utility second — the point is that whoever writes it will never ship these bugs again.
>
> Deliver the library, the test suite, a benchmark report, and a one-page "maths for ML" cheat sheet
> written by you, not copied.

<Warning>
The cheat sheet is not filler. Writing down every formula you needed this week, in your own words, with
one example each, is the highest-retention exercise in Phase 2 — and it is the artefact you will
actually reopen during Weeks 21, 26 and 27.
</Warning>

<Tabs>

<Tab title="basic">

**Vectors and matrices, with tests.**

1. `dot`, `norm(v, p)` for p = 1, 2, ∞, `cosine`, `normalise`, `project`.
2. `transpose`, `matvec`, `matmul`, `identity`.
3. `euclidean` distance, and a demonstration that cosine and Euclidean disagree on unnormalised
   vectors and agree on normalised ones.
4. Tests against NumPy to 1e-6 on 1,000 random inputs each.

**Acceptance criteria**

- `cosine(v, c*v) == 1.0` (to 1e-9) for random `v` and random positive `c`.
- For unit vectors, `‖u−v‖² == 2 − 2(u·v)` to 1e-9 — proving cosine and Euclidean rank identically.
- `norm` with a zero vector raises a clear error rather than returning `nan`.
- Every function has a docstring stating its input shapes.

<Info>
About 60% is written for you in `starter/`. The `TODO(you)` markers are on `cosine`, `project`,
`matmul` and the zero-vector guard.
</Info>

</Tab>

<Tab title="standard">

**The full library. This is the Week 11 portfolio artefact.**

Everything in `basic`, plus:

1. **`inverse`** via Gauss-Jordan elimination with partial pivoting, raising a clear error on singular
   input.
2. **`power_iteration`** and `top_k_eigen` (with deflation) for symmetric matrices.
3. **`pca`** via SVD: centring, components, explained-variance ratios, and inverse transform.
4. **`low_rank(A, k)`** plus a verification that the error equals the Eckart–Young bound.
5. **`numeric_gradient`** and **`gradcheck`** with relative-error reporting.
6. **A ~90-line reverse-mode autodiff engine**: `Value` with `+`, `*`, `**`, `relu`, `exp`, `log`,
   `tanh`, topological sort and `backward()`.
7. **The five optimizers** — GD, momentum, Nesterov, RMSProp, Adam — with a Rosenbrock race.

**Acceptance criteria**

- Every function matches NumPy to **1e-6**, verified on at least 500 random inputs.
- `gradcheck` on the autodiff engine returns worst relative error below **1e-7** across at least seven
  functions, including a 5-deep chain and fused softmax + cross-entropy.
- `low_rank` error equals `sqrt(Σ_{i>k} σᵢ²)` to 1e-8 (the Eckart–Young bound).
- Adam reaches `f < 1e-3` on Rosenbrock from `(−1.2, 1.0)` within 5,000 steps; at least two other
  optimizers do not, and your report says why.
- `REPORT.md` contains: the matmul benchmark table, the image-compression table, the gradcheck table,
  the optimizer race, and the LoRA parameter arithmetic for a real model.

<Info>Spec and tests only. You will reuse this library in Phase 3 — write it as if you will.</Info>

</Tab>

<Tab title="hard">

**Two constraints the standard solution fails.**

1. **Tiled matmul beating naive by ≥ 10×.** The `standard` `matmul` is a triple loop. Implement a
   blocked version, sweep the tile size, and report GFLOP/s at n = 128, 256, 512 for naive, tiled and
   BLAS. Explain the remaining gap to BLAS in terms of interpreter overhead, SIMD width, cache
   blocking and register reuse — with a number attached to each factor.
2. **Autodiff supporting a 2-layer network trained end to end.** Extend `Value` to arrays (or build a
   minimal tensor type) with correct broadcasting in the backward pass — a broadcast forwards is a
   **sum over the broadcast axes** backwards. Train a 2-layer MLP on two-moons or MNIST to
   convergence, and compare the loss curve against PyTorch on identical initialisation and data order.
3. **A benchmark report** covering both, with the measured numbers and an honest account of where your
   implementation is slower and why.

**Acceptance criteria**

- Tiled matmul is at least 10× faster than naive at n = 256, with the optimal tile size reported and
  predicted from your machine's cache sizes.
- The MLP reaches above 95% training accuracy on two-moons, and the first ten loss values agree with
  PyTorch to 1e-5 on identical initialisation.
- Broadcasting gradients are gradchecked, not assumed.

<Warning>
The broadcasting backward pass is the constraint that breaks the standard solution. A scalar autodiff
engine cannot train a network in reasonable time, and the naive array extension gets broadcasting
wrong in a way that passes small tests and fails on any real batch.
</Warning>

</Tab>

## Ship it

<Card title="This is the part people skip" icon="rocket" horizontal>
A public repo with `nanomath`, the full test suite, `REPORT.md` with every measured table, and
`CHEATSHEET.md` — one page, written by you, covering every formula from this week with one worked
example each.
</Card>

**Write the cheat sheet by hand first, on paper, from memory.** Then check it against the modules and
fix what you got wrong. The gaps you find are exactly what you have not learned, and finding them on
Saturday is much cheaper than finding them in Week 21.

Suggested sections: norms and cosine; the three matmul readings and the shape rule; determinant, rank
and what `det = 0` means; SVD and `k(m+n+1)`; the five derivative rules and the chain rule; the five ML
gradients (MSE, sigmoid, ReLU, softmax, softmax+CE); the `2/L` threshold; Adam's update and its
16 bytes/param.

## Rubric

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Correctness | tests fail | vectors and matrices match NumPy | decompositions and autodiff correct too | gradcheck below 1e-7 on all functions; Eckart–Young verified; singular inputs raise clearly |
| Code quality | works | readable | typed, docstrings state shapes, one module per topic | a reviewer would approve unchanged; the library is genuinely reusable in Phase 3 |
| Performance | untested | matmul benchmarked | tiled beats naive by ≥ 10× | the gap to BLAS decomposed into four factors with a number for each |
| Explanation | none | a README | `REPORT.md` with all five tables | the cheat sheet is complete, in the student's own words, and connects each idea to the week that uses it |

**The Explanation column is where this lab is won**, and specifically the forward links: a cheat sheet
that says "cosine similarity → Week 27 retrieval" and "SVD → Week 26 LoRA" demonstrates that the week
was understood rather than completed.

## Common ways submissions lose points

1. **`gradcheck` run in float32.** The numerical gradient's noise floor is around 1e-3 there, so the
   check cannot distinguish a correct gradient from one that is 0.01% wrong. Use float64.
2. **Gradients assigned rather than accumulated.** `self.grad = ...` instead of `+=` passes every test
   where each value is used once, and silently drops half the gradient in a diamond-shaped graph.
3. **PCA without centring.** The first component becomes the direction of the mean. Assert that the
   centred data has column means below 1e-12.
4. **The matmul benchmark run at one size.** The interesting content is how the gap changes with `n`
   and why (interpreter-bound versus memory-bound).
5. **A cheat sheet copied from the modules.** Recognisable instantly, and it defeats the purpose. Write
   it from memory first.
6. **No singular-input handling.** `inverse` of a singular matrix must raise, not return numbers.
