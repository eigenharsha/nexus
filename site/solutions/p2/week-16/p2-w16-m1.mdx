## Reference solution — `P2-W16-M1`

The twelve-item shape-debugging drill, with the diagnosis and the fix for each.

```python
A = np.random.random((32, 10))
B = np.random.random((10, 32))
v = np.random.random(10)
w = np.random.random(32)
```

| # | Expression | Error | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `A + B` | broadcast (32,10) (10,32) | right-align: 10 vs 32 | `A + B.T` |
| 2 | `A @ A` | matmul (32,10) (32,10) | inner dims 10 and 32 | `A @ A.T` → (32,32), or `A.T @ A` → (10,10) |
| 3 | `A + w` | broadcast (32,10) (32,) | right-align: 10 vs 32 | `A + w[:, None]` — one value per row |
| 4 | `A * v[:, None]` | broadcast (32,10) (10,1) | `v` is length 10; `[:, None]` makes it (10,1), aligning 10 against 32 | `A * v` — already broadcasts across columns |
| 5 | `np.concatenate([A, B])` | (32,10) vs (10,32) on axis 0 | non-concatenation dims must match | `np.concatenate([A, B.T])`, or `axis=1` after transposing |
| 6 | `A[np.array([True, False])]` | boolean index of length 2 for axis of length 32 | mask must match the axis length | `A[np.zeros(32, dtype=bool)]` |
| 7 | `A.reshape(10, 32).T @ v` | matmul (32,10) (10,) works — **no error**; the trap is that it is meaningless | `reshape` reinterprets the buffer; it does **not** transpose | `A.T @ w` if you wanted the real transpose |
| 8 | `np.dot(v, w)` | (10,) and (32,) | different lengths | they are not conformable; you wanted `A @ v` or `A.T @ w` |
| 9 | `A.sum(axis=2)` | axis 2 out of bounds for 2-D | `A` has axes 0 and 1 | `A.sum(axis=1)` |
| 10 | `A[:, :, 0]` | too many indices | three indices for a 2-D array | `A[:, 0]` |
| 11 | `np.vstack([A, v])` | (32,10) and (10,) | `vstack` promotes `v` to (1,10) — this **works**; the trap is the silent promotion | be explicit: `np.vstack([A, v[None, :]])` |
| 12 | `A @ v[:, None] + w` | (32,1) + (32,) → (32,32) — **no error, wrong answer** | right-align: 1 vs 32 broadcasts into an outer product | `A @ v + w`, or `A @ v[:, None] + w[:, None]` |

**Items 7, 11 and 12 are the important ones because they do not raise.** Item 12 in particular
silently produces a (32,32) outer product where a (32,) vector was intended, and it will flow
downstream until something else complains about a shape — by which time the cause is three functions
away. **Broadcasting turns some shape mistakes into wrong answers rather than errors**, which is why
asserting shapes at API boundaries is worth the lines.

### The measurements

| Operation | n | Python loop | NumPy | Speedup |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `x * 2.5 + 1` | 10,000 | 0.346 ms | 0.027 ms | **13×** |
| `x * 2.5 + 1` | 1,000,000 | 33.914 ms | 0.302 ms | **112×** |
| dot product | 1,000,000 | 25.039 ms | 0.161 ms | **156×** |

**The speedup grows with n**, from 13× to 156×. Below a few hundred elements NumPy's per-call
overhead — argument parsing, dtype dispatch, output allocation — dominates and a Python loop can
genuinely win. **A submission reporting one number has missed the shape of the result.**

| Selecting every other element of a 400 MB array | Time | Allocates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `a[::2]` — basic slice | **1.46 µs** | **0 bytes** |
| `a[::2].copy()` | 20.6 ms | 200 MB |
| `a[idx]` — fancy indexing | 70.1 ms | 200 MB |
| `a[mask]` — boolean mask | 116.1 ms | 200 MB |

**The slice is ~80,000× faster than the mask for the same elements**, because it does no work: it
returns a new array object with `strides=(16,)` instead of `(8,)` pointing at the same buffer.

| 4000×4000 traversal | C order | F order | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Iterate rows | **5.0 ms** | 37.5 ms | **7.48× — C wins** |
| Iterate columns | 38.1 ms | **5.2 ms** | **7.26× — F wins** |

**Perfectly symmetric, and the symmetry is the lesson.** Neither layout is better; each suits one
access pattern.

| n copies of 0.1, `float32` | Naive sequential | NumPy pairwise | Naive relative error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000,000 | 100,958.34 | 100,000.09 | **0.96%** |
| 10,000,000 | 985,123.91 | 999,989.44 | **1.49%** |

**Naive `float32` accumulation loses 1.5% of the answer.** NumPy's `sum` is pairwise —
`O(log n · ε)` error instead of `O(n · ε)` — and it is free. `arr.mean(dtype=np.float64)` removes
the problem entirely; `math.fsum` is exact and ~10× slower, which is what you want for money.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Why predict before running.** The drill's value is the gap between your prediction and the actual
error. Running first and reading the message afterwards teaches you to read messages, which you can
already do.

**Why print shapes, not values.** `print(f"{A.shape=} {v.shape=}")` at the top of every debugging
session. Ninety percent of NumPy bugs are visible in the shapes alone, and values fill the screen
with noise.

**Why "the axis you name is the one that disappears" rather than "axis 0 is rows".** The rows/columns
formulation stops working at three dimensions, which is where you will be in week 21. Read it off the
shape: `(2,3).sum(axis=0)` → `(3,)`; the 2 is gone.

**Why validate shapes at your own API boundaries.** NumPy's error names the shapes and nothing else —
not which argument was wrong, not what was expected. Raising
`expected (n_samples, 3), got (10, 5)` converts a ten-minute debugging session into three seconds,
and you will be extremely grateful for the habit in week 21 where every bug is a shape bug.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Correctness** — all twelve diagnosed, **including the three that do not raise**. A
submission that reports "items 7, 11 and 12 don't error" has answered the exercise better than one
that quietly skipped them.

**3/3 on Performance** — the speedup measured at three sizes so the growth is visible, plus at least
one of the view/copy, memory-order, or precision results.

**3/3 on Explanation** — the three mechanisms behind vectorization (contiguous typed memory, C loop,
SIMD), and why the speedup is small at small n.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **Reporting a single vectorization speedup.** 13× at 10⁴ and 156× at 10⁶ is the finding; one
   number is a data point.
2. **Missing that items 7, 11 and 12 produce wrong answers rather than errors.** These are the
   dangerous ones and the drill exists for them.
3. **Not measuring anything from Layer 2 or 3.** The drill alone is the Layer 1 exercise; the module
   asks for a measurement too.

**On the stretch challenge (shape diagnoser).** The interesting question is whether the suggestion is
*unique*. Several transformations often make the shapes work and only one is correct — for item 3,
both `w[:, None]` and `A.T + w` produce valid shapes and only one is what was meant. A submission
that reports its suggestion accuracy, and admits where it is ambiguous, has done the real work.
