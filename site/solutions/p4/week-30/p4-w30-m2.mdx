## Reference solution — `P4-W30-M2`

### Running an honest head-to-head

The experiment is the deliverable, and there are four ways to make it dishonest without meaning to.

**1. Tuning one configuration more than the others.** You will spend three hours on the multi-agent
prompt and twenty minutes on the baseline. Then the baseline loses and you conclude something false.
Fix: fixed time budget per configuration, written down before you start. Ours was 90 minutes each.

**2. Omitting the control.** Single agent asked to review its own work in one conversation. It is
the strongest cheap configuration on many tasks — 80% of the critic's gain at 71% of its cost in our
run — and leaving it out inflates the case for multi-agent.

**3. One scorer.** Two, with Cohen's kappa reported. Ours was 0.71, which is "substantial" and still
means roughly one disagreement in seven. Week 31 M2 explains why a single unvalidated scorer is not
evidence.

**4. Cost per run instead of cost per successful task.** Multi-agent configurations fail differently
— often more expensively — so per-run cost flatters them.

### Reference numbers

| Configuration | Success | Cost per successful task | p50 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single strong agent | 75.0% | $0.253 | 34 s |
| Single agent + self-review, one conversation | 80.0% | $0.300 | 44 s |
| Critic-Refiner, 2 rounds | 82.5% | $0.412 | 61 s |
| Orchestrator + 3 specialists, isolated context | 77.5% | $0.400 | 71 s |
| Mixture of 3 + aggregator | 77.5% | $1.110 | 71 s |

Failure attribution over the orchestrator runs: **8 of 9 failures were coordination, not
capability.** Individually the specialists were right 37 times in 40.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Obvious:** more agents, more capability. Week 29 M1's arithmetic disagrees before you run
anything. Three specialists at 92.5% plus an orchestrator at 87.5% is 0.925³ × 0.875 = 69.3%
expected against a single loop's 75%. We computed that before measuring and the measurement agreed.

**Obvious:** shared context so every agent "has the full picture". It triples token cost and turns
one agent's wrong intermediate conclusion into everyone's premise. Isolated briefs took the
orchestrator pattern from $0.71 to $0.31 with no quality change — but they move the failure mode to
"answered the wrong question perfectly", which is why `must_return` and the `unanswered` check exist.

**Obvious:** three refinement rounds because two seems few. Measured per-round quality delta: +0.41,
+0.07, −0.02. A critic asked to find problems in an acceptable draft finds some, and acting on them
makes it worse.

## The two wrong turns worth knowing about

1. **No `ACCEPT` token.** With no explicit accept, 0 of 40 runs stopped before the round cap. Critics are helpful by disposition; you must give them a way to say "done".
2. **Prose feedback instead of structured fixes.** Prose reached acceptance in 2.6 rounds, structured `Fix` objects in 1.7 — a 35% cost reduction from changing the shape of one hand-off, because the writer knew which sentence to change rather than rewriting everything.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Measurement:** four configurations including the control, two scorers with agreement
reported, three repetitions, cost per successful task, and a failure taxonomy separating capability
from coordination.

**3/3 on Explanation:** a stated conclusion with a recommendation, and the willingness to recommend
the single agent. A submission that concludes multi-agent won without the control condition scores 1.

**Common losses:** no control; one scorer; per-run cost only; and — most often — a mixture-of-agents
implementation running one model three times, which does not reproduce the paper's mechanism at all.
