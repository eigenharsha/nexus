## Reference solution — `P4-W31-M1`

### The two hours that decide everything

Read fifty outputs. Not skim — read, with a text file open, writing one line per output about what
is wrong. Then group. People skip this and build a suite that measures whatever was easy to measure.

What the reading produces that nothing else does: **the discovery that most of your failures are
mechanical.** In our taxonomy, 22 of the top 31 failures were catchable by `in`, `len()` and
`model_validate()` — no judge, no validation, no cost. Teams that skip the reading go straight to an
LLM judge for everything, which is expensive, noisy, and requires its own validation project.

Two rules for the session. Write the *category* before the *check* — the category is what you still
care about in six months. And refuse to let "bad answer" be a category; if you cannot say what is
wrong more specifically, you have not read enough examples.

### The power calculation, written out

```python
import math
z_a, z_b = 1.959964, 0.841621          # alpha 0.05 two-sided, power 0.80
def detectable(n: int, pi_d: float = 0.15) -> float:
    """Smallest difference a paired suite of n cases can detect."""
    return math.sqrt((z_a + z_b) ** 2 * pi_d / n)

for n in (20, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000):
    print(f"{n:5d} cases -> {detectable(n):.3f}")
# 20 -> 0.242   50 -> 0.153   100 -> 0.108   250 -> 0.069   500 -> 0.048   1000 -> 0.034
```

Put this output in your `EVALS.md`. It is the sentence that stops someone shipping on a 3-point
improvement measured over 100 cases.

The two escapes are worth as much as the calculation. **Assertions have far more power** because
their true rate sits at a boundary — 3 failures where 0 are expected is significant on 100 cases, so
put every hard requirement in the assertion tier. And **reducing π_d by running each case three
times and scoring the majority** took ours from 0.21 to 0.13, equivalent to a 38% larger sample for
the cost of two extra runs.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Obvious:** use an LLM judge for everything, because it is flexible. It is also expensive, noisy,
and needs its own validation before it means anything. Exhaust the free tier first; you will find
most of your failures there.

**Obvious:** write 100 cases in an afternoon from imagination. They test your imagination. Ours
passed at 96% against 84% for production-derived cases — a 12-point gap that is pure construction
artifact.

**Obvious:** a rising suite score means the system is improving. It can equally mean the suite is
ageing. Track the offline/online gap; a *falling* offline score after you improve sampling is good
news and you should warn people before it happens.

## The two wrong turns worth knowing about

1. **Counting inapplicable checks as passes.** A case with no `must_contain` scored 1.0 on the containment check, and the aggregate drifted upward while nothing improved. `None` means not-applicable and is excluded from the denominator.
2. **Building the whole suite in one two-week push and never adding to it.** Six weeks later, 30% of live traffic had a shape the suite contained zero examples of: offline 94%, online 71%. The weekly twenty-request sample is five minutes and it is what keeps the instrument pointed at reality.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Eval design:** cases derived from a documented taxonomy, tagged by category and origin,
tiered, with the detectable-difference figure stated in `EVALS.md`.

**3/3 on Explanation:** an honest "what this suite cannot see" section. Ours names three things:
tone, anything requiring the context to be *true* rather than merely used, and any behaviour on
traffic shapes we have no cases for.

**Common losses:** invented cases; an aggregate score with no per-category breakdown; and a claimed
improvement smaller than the suite can detect, which is the most common single error in the week.
