## Reference solution — `P4-W31-M5`

### The third eval set is the assignment

Attacks and benign is what everyone builds. **Benign lookalikes** — legitimate requests that resemble
attacks — is where every real false positive lives.

| Configuration | TPR | FPR (benign) | FPR (lookalikes) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Keyword/regex filter | 0.41 | 0.02 | **0.31** |
| Llama Guard 3 8B, default | 0.89 | 0.01 | **0.11** |
| Llama Guard, tuned to threshold 0.82 | 0.81 | 0.002 | **0.019** |

The keyword row would have blocked nearly a third of customers reporting card fraud, and measured on
ordinary benign traffic it looked fine at 2%. **A guardrail measured only on ordinary traffic is not
measured.**

Build the lookalike set by asking a support agent for the twenty request types most likely to look
alarming. Ninety minutes of their time; the highest-value ninety minutes in the module.

### Report blocked users, not rates

| Threshold | TPR | FPR | Blocked legitimate users per 10,000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.50 (default) | 0.89 | 4.0% | 400 |
| 0.70 | 0.86 | 1.9% | 190 |
| **0.82** | **0.81** | **0.9%** | **90** |
| 0.90 | 0.68 | 0.3% | 30 |

"FPR 4%" is abstract. "400 blocked customers per 10,000 requests" is a number a product owner has an
opinion about, and their opinion is the input you need. Put the last column in front of them.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**Obvious:** ask the main model to moderate itself in the same call. It is doing two jobs and doing
both worse, and its moderation decision is now inside the context an attacker can address. A separate
small fine-tuned classifier is faster, cheaper and independent.

**Obvious:** hard-block anything above the threshold. Soft-blocking — routing uncertain requests to a
human with the classifier's category attached — preserved TPR and took blocked-customer complaints to
zero, because the requests were still served, just by a person. It is the single best move in the
module and it does not require touching the threshold.

**Obvious:** log every blocked request in full for review. That table is now a record of what your
users were refused for, created by your safety feature. Redact before writing, store text only inside
the uncertainty band (0.6–0.95), simhash the rest, and support deletion by hashed user id.

## The two wrong turns worth knowing about

1. **Adding an output guardrail without measuring TTFT.** Total time grew 24% and perceived latency grew 360%, because you cannot stream a response a later check might reject. Running the output checks concurrently and streaming optimistically for the 92% of traffic where the check has never fired recovered nearly all of it — with an explicit, bounded accepted risk that you must state.
2. **Reporting only the aggregate FPR.** Ours was 0.9% overall and 6.2% for fraud reports; a further split found non-English requests blocked at 3.4× the English rate, which is a fairness problem rather than a tuning one. Segment by topic, tenure, language and sentiment; report the worst segment.

## Grading notes

**3/3 on Guardrail honesty:** three eval sets including lookalikes, a threshold sweep with a
blocked-users column, a written and owned FP budget, per-segment FPR, and the latency and streaming
cost measured.

**Common losses:** no lookalike set; FPR quoted with no TPR or vice versa; no fail-open/fail-closed
decision recorded; and — the most common — a `SECURITY` narrative in which the guardrails carry the
whole safety argument. They do not. Module 4's numbers say the guardrail layer alone left 4 of 15
attacks effective; the architectural change alone left 0.
