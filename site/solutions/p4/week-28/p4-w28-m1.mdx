## Reference solution — `P4-W28-M1`

### The task

Find 10 queries where BM25 beats dense and 10 where dense wins, and categorise the pattern.

### The solution that works

The comparison is trivial; the categorisation is the exercise. Automate the disagreement extraction,
then read them:

```python
rows = []
for item in eval_set:
    d = recall_at_k([h.id for h in dense_search(item["query"], 10)],
                    set(item["relevant_chunk_ids"]), 10)
    s = recall_at_k([i for i, _ in sparse.search(item["query"], 10)],
                    set(item["relevant_chunk_ids"]), 10)
    rows.append((item["query"], d, s, s - d))

rows.sort(key=lambda r: r[3])
print("DENSE WINS BIGGEST:"); [print(f"  {r[3]:+.2f}  {r[0]}") for r in rows[:10]]
print("BM25 WINS BIGGEST:");  [print(f"  {r[3]:+.2f}  {r[0]}") for r in rows[-10:]]
```

### Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious conclusion from "dense 0.88, BM25 0.71" is that BM25 is worse and can be skipped.**
The aggregate is the wrong object. The two systems fail on *disjoint* query sets — the per-query
success correlation is 0.31 — and that disjointness is what makes Module 2's fusion work. A
comparison that reports only aggregates cannot see it, and the decision it leads to (drop BM25) is
the war story in Module 1.

**The second obvious mistake is treating Postgres FTS as BM25.** `ts_rank_cd` is a cover-density
ranker with no saturation and no corpus IDF, and it lands 7 points below real BM25 on the same
corpus. If a colleague says "we already have BM25 in Postgres", this is the number to bring.

### The two wrong turns worth knowing about

**Wrong turn 1: a tokenizer that splits on non-alphanumerics.** `[a-z0-9]+` turns `ORA-01555` into
`ora` and `01555`, and identifier recall goes from 0.96 to 0.41. The bare number then collides with
page numbers, dates and version fragments across the corpus, so it carries almost no IDF. One regex
character — keeping `-` and `.` inside the token class — is the whole fix.

**Wrong turn 2: applying stemming to everything.** Stemming buys +0.03 overall and costs 0.07 on
identifiers. Indexing twice and combining with `np.maximum` (not a sum) gets both: a document with an
exact identifier hit should win on that field without being dragged down by a weak stemmed score.

### Grading notes

**3/3** requires the segment table, the analyzer comparison including the identifier column, the
`k1`/`b` grid with a CI, and a written pattern — not just a list of queries. **The three most common
point losses:** reporting only aggregates, which hides the entire finding; a tokenizer that destroys
identifiers, visible immediately in the segment column; and presenting the `k1`/`b` gain as a result
when its CI touches zero.
