## Reference solution — `P3-W19-M5`

The task: produce a slice report showing a model that is strong overall and weak on a high-value segment, then write the ship/no-ship recommendation.

## The baseline ladder

| Model | Accuracy | ROC-AUC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| majority class | 0.5035 | 0.5000 |
| stratified random | 0.4997 | 0.4996 |
| **best single feature** | **0.8014** | **0.8804** |
| logistic regression | 0.8917 | 0.9327 |
| gradient boosting | 0.8991 | 0.9754 |

The third row is the one that matters and the one most submissions omit. **A single feature and a threshold reaches 0.8014.** The entire modelling effort bought 9.8 accuracy points over one `if` statement — a real gain, and a completely different sentence from "our model is 90% accurate".

Compute it correctly: loop over every column and score `roc_auc_score(y, X[:, j])` **and** `roc_auc_score(y, -X[:, j])`. Forgetting the second sign under-reports this baseline, which flatters your model.

## The slice table

| Slice | Accuracy | ROC-AUC | n | Share of rows | Share of revenue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **overall** | 0.8991 | 0.9754 | 21,000 | 100% | 100% |
| self-serve | 0.9548 | 0.9931 | 17,845 | 85.0% | 70.4% |
| **enterprise** | **0.5842** | **0.6215** | 3,155 | 15.0% | **29.6%** |

And the number that makes it land with a commercial audience:

```
unweighted accuracy       0.8991
revenue-weighted accuracy 0.8457      <- the headline overstates by 5.3 points
```

**The model is close to a coin flip on 29.6% of revenue.** Nothing in the headline said so, because the easy slice is 85% of the rows and dominates the average.

## The paired bootstrap

| Comparison | A (HistGB) | B (logreg) | Difference | 95% CI |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| all rows | 0.9754 | 0.9327 | +0.0426 | [+0.0397, +0.0457] |
| enterprise slice only | 0.6215 | 0.5886 | +0.0331 | [+0.0150, +0.0519] |

Both exclude zero, so both are real. The instructive part is the **widths**: ±0.003 on 21,000 rows against ±0.018 on 3,155. **A difference of the same size is decisive overall and marginal on the slice**, purely because of sample size. That contrast is the argument for reporting intervals rather than numbers, and it should be called out explicitly.

Pairing is not optional: draw the resample indices **once** and score both models on them. An unpaired comparison adds the "which rows were drawn" variance twice when it cancels, and produces an interval roughly twice as wide — often wide enough to cross zero and turn a real difference into "not established".

## The recommendation

The deliverable is not the table. It is this:

> **Ship to the self-serve segment only. Do not route enterprise accounts through this model.**
>
> On self-serve (85% of rows, 70% of revenue) the model reaches 0.9931 AUC against 0.8804 from
> the best single feature. On enterprise (15% of rows, **29.6% of revenue**) it reaches 0.6215,
> which is not usefully better than the rep's own judgement, and revenue-weighted accuracy is
> 0.8457 against an unweighted 0.8991.
>
> **What would change this:** if the enterprise segment's share of revenue rises above ~40%, the
> revenue-weighted number falls below the incumbent and the scoping decision should be revisited.
> The enterprise gap is a data problem, not a modelling one — procurement cycle, champion
> turnover and competitive presence are not in the feature set. The next project is collecting
> them, not tuning this.

Three properties of that paragraph are what earn the marks: it **leads with a decision**, it **scopes rather than rejects**, and it **names the assumption that would flip it**.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious solution reports the overall metric and recommends shipping.** It is defensible from the numbers it shows and it is how the war story in the module happened: the model shipped, the enterprise reps quietly stopped using it, and nobody noticed for five weeks.

Two more wrong turns:

1. **Recommending "do not ship".** Almost always wrong, and it is the reflex when a slice looks bad. The model is genuinely excellent on 85% of rows and 70% of revenue; throwing that away because it fails elsewhere is worse than scoping it. **Scoping is the senior move and it is rarely the one proposed**, because it feels like an admission rather than what it is.
2. **Weighting by rows when the business is denominated in money.** Accuracy weights every row equally; nothing about the business does. Whatever the mistakes cost — revenue, claim value, patient-days, exposure — weight by that and report both numbers side by side.

One statistical wrong turn to avoid: **slicing by ten features and reporting whichever slice looks worst.** Fifty intervals at 95% will produce two or three exclusions by chance. Pre-specify the slices the business names — those are hypotheses you were always going to test, not multiple comparisons — and treat the rest as exploratory, reporting the full sorted table so the reader can see how many you looked at.

## Grading notes

**3/3 looks like:** all five baseline rungs including the best single feature with both signs tried; a slice table sorted worst-first with `n`, row share, weight share and 95% intervals, with `min_rows` enforced; the revenue-weighted comparison; a paired bootstrap overall **and** on the worst slice, with the width difference called out; and a recommendation that scopes, leads with the decision, and names the flipping assumption.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **No single-feature baseline.** Without it there is no evidence the model earned its complexity.
2. **A slice metric with no interval.** A 40-row slice scoring 0.42 is not a finding until you show the interval, and the interval will be enormous.
3. **A recommendation with no scope condition and no named assumption.** "Ship it" and "do not ship it" are both worse answers than "ship it here, not there, and here is what would change my mind".
