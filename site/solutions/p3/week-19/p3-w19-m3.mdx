## Reference solution — `P3-W19-M3`

The task: run adversarial validation on a train/test pair and identify the shifting features.

## The procedure, and the step everybody skips

```python
# scikit-learn 1.9.0
X_all   = np.vstack([X_train, X_other])
is_other = np.r_[np.zeros(len(X_train)), np.ones(len(X_other))]
auc = cross_val_score(RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0, n_jobs=-1),
                      X_all, is_other, cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0),
                      scoring="roc_auc").mean()
```

**The skipped step is the control.** Before you interpret any number, split your *training* data into two random halves and run the same classifier on them. That AUC is your floor, and it is not exactly 0.5 — it is 0.5 plus whatever your model and sample size can manufacture.

Measured:

```
train vs shifted set  AUC = 0.7902 +- 0.0079
control, no shift     AUC = 0.5070 +- 0.0083
```

Without the control, 0.79 is a number. With it, 0.79 is 0.79 against a floor of 0.51.

## Naming the features

Permutation importance on the adversarial model, on held-out data:

```
f3  0.2544      f7  0.1061      f0  0.0250      f5  0.0018      f9  0.0018
```

Ground truth: features 3, 7 and 0 were the ones shifted. All three identified, in the right order of magnitude, with the remaining nine sitting at 0.0018 — a clean noise floor that makes the cut obvious.

That noise floor is the reason to report the *whole* ranking rather than the top three. A gap of two orders of magnitude between f0 (0.0250) and f5 (0.0018) is what tells a reader where to draw the line; a truncated list does not.

## The step that turns it into a decision

Detection is not the deliverable. For each drifting feature, cross-reference against the **predictive** model's own permutation importance and put it in one of four boxes:

| | drifted | did not drift |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **model relies on it** | **stop.** the model's reported metrics do not apply. fix the feature or retrain on recent data | fine |
| **model ignores it** | note it and move on — a detectable shift in an irrelevant feature is harmless | fine |

The top-left box is a go/no-go issue. The bottom-left is a footnote. Reporting an adversarial AUC of 0.79 without saying which box the drifting features fall into leaves the reader unable to act, which is the most common failure of an otherwise correct submission.

## Two ways to make the technique useless

**Leaving identifiers in.** Any row id, timestamp, ingestion sequence or monotonically increasing counter makes the adversarial AUC 1.0 trivially. Drop them before running, and if your AUC comes back above about 0.98, suspect this before suspecting a genuine shift.

**Interpreting a high AUC as automatically bad.** If you deliberately split by time or by site, the two sets *should* differ — that is what the split means. The question is never "do they differ" but "do they differ in features the model depends on".

## Suggested alert thresholds

From the measured control floor and practical use:

| Adversarial AUC | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| below 0.60 | indistinguishable; no action |
| 0.60 – 0.75 | investigate; check which features and whether the model uses them |
| above 0.75 | the training data no longer describes production; the model's reported metrics do not apply |

Calibrate the lower bound against your own control run rather than adopting these blind — a high-dimensional dataset with a powerful adversarial model will show a higher floor.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious solution trains the adversarial classifier, reports the AUC, and concludes there is or is not drift.** It is half the method. Without the control you cannot say whether 0.62 is drift or is what your setup produces on identical distributions; without the feature ranking you cannot act; without the cross-reference to predictive importance you cannot decide.

One more wrong turn worth knowing: **using accuracy instead of AUC.** With unequal set sizes, accuracy is dominated by the base rate — a 90/10 train/test split makes "always predict train" 90% accurate and tells you nothing. Always score the adversarial model with ROC-AUC, and stratify the folds.

## Grading notes

**3/3 looks like:** the control run reported first; identifiers excluded, with a sentence saying so; the full permutation-importance ranking with the noise floor visible; every drifting feature cross-referenced against the predictive model's importance and assigned a decision; and alert thresholds justified against the measured control rather than quoted.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **No control run.** The headline AUC then has no scale and the whole analysis is uninterpretable.
2. **Identifiers left in.** AUC 1.0, a confident conclusion, and nothing learned.
3. **Detection without a decision.** A list of drifting features with no statement of which ones matter is an observation, not a finding.
