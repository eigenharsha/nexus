## Reference solution — `LAB-P3-W20`

## The progression

284,807 transactions, 492 frauds (0.1727%). 75/12.5/12.5 stratified split at `random_state=42`: 170,884 train (295 positives), 56,961 validation (98), 56,962 test (99).

| Model | Average precision | ROC-AUC | Brier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| majority baseline | 0.00174 | 0.50000 | 0.0017350 |
| `Amount` alone | **0.00199** | **0.46493** | — |
| logistic regression | 0.77456 | 0.96981 | 0.0006587 |
| XGBoost, `max_depth=4`, 276 trees | **0.87315** | **0.97427** | **0.0003953** |

Two rows deserve attention before anything else.

**`Amount` alone scores AUC 0.46493 — worse than random.** The single most intuitive feature in the problem is anti-predictive. Every intuition about this dataset has to come from measurement, and a submission that dropped `Amount` for that reason has thrown away the feature that defines the *cost matrix* even though it is useless as a *predictor*.

**ROC-AUC separates logistic regression from XGBoost by 0.0045; average precision separates them by 0.0986.** At 0.17% prevalence ROC-AUC is nearly flat and hides a 12.7% relative improvement. If your report leads with AUC you have chosen the metric that conceals your own result.

## The cost matrix is per-row

This is what makes the lab more than an exercise. `c_FN` is the transaction amount, so the optimal threshold varies by row:

```
act on row i  when   p_i * amount_i > (1 - p_i) * c_FP
              i.e.   p_i > c_FP / (c_FP + amount_i)
```

A €5 transaction needs `p > 0.375`; a €1,000 transaction needs `p > 0.003`.

Measured on the test set (`c_FP` = €3; test-set fraud amounts: median €19.04, mean €99.74, max €1,218.89):

| Threshold | How chosen | Test cost | FP | FN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | the default | €2,590 | 10 | 21 |
| 0.016813 | argmin **on the test set** | €2,467 | 94 | 10 |
| 0.222664 | argmin on **validation**, applied to test | €2,588 | 12 | 17 |
| **per-row** `c_FP/(c_FP + amount_i)` | closed form, no tuning | **€2,583** | 127 | 18 |
| — | no model | €9,874 | — | — |
| — | review everything | €170,589 | — | — |

**Three findings, and the second is the one that separates grades.**

The test-set argmin of €2,467 is **not achievable** — choosing and reporting on the same data is Week 19 Module 3's selection bias. The honest global figure is €2,588.

**The per-row threshold wins by €5 — 0.2%.** It is theoretically correct and practically almost irrelevant here, because the loss is dominated by a handful of large frauds that any reasonable threshold catches. The per-row thresholds span 0.000232 to 1.0 with a median of 0.1209 — an enormous range producing a negligible effect. **Reporting that honestly is worth more than a confident claim in either direction**, and it is the kind of null result the week has been training you to state.

At `c_FP = €10` the cost-optimal threshold is **0.615, above 0.5**, because a €10 review is expensive against a €19 median fraud. "Rare class means a low threshold" is not a rule.

## The ticket's actual question

Ana asked whether to hire a second reviewer. The recall-versus-budget curve answers it:

| FP budget (test set) | Threshold | Recall | Precision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50 | 0.032571 | 0.8788 | 0.6397 |
| 100 | 0.015460 | 0.8990 | 0.4709 |
| 200 | 0.008264 | 0.8990 | 0.3080 |
| 500 | 0.003389 | 0.9091 | 0.1528 |

**Recall moves from 0.879 to 0.909 — three percentage points — while the queue grows tenfold and precision falls by a factor of four.**

The answer is: **do not hire the second reviewer.** One reviewer at ~320 cases a day captures essentially all the recall the model can deliver; the second one would spend the day on the 85% of their queue that is legitimate. Deliver that in one sentence with the number attached, and note the more useful alternative — spend the headcount on better features or on reducing review time per case.

## Do not resample

Acceptance criterion A4 exists because this is the reflex at 0.17% positive. Module 4 measured every treatment as significantly worse on average precision with intervals excluding zero, and calibration degraded by up to 18.9x. Here calibration matters directly: **the per-row cost threshold multiplies `p` by an amount in euros**, so a model calibrated to a resampled 50% base rate produces expected-loss numbers that are wrong by orders of magnitude.

If a submission uses SMOTE or `scale_pos_weight`, it must show the paired bootstrap against the untreated model. Expect the interval to exclude zero on the wrong side.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

**The obvious solution trains a good model, picks the threshold that minimises cost on the test set, and reports €2,467.** Everything about it is competent and the number is not achievable. Selecting the threshold on validation gives €2,588, and the difference — €121, about 5% — is exactly the optimism Week 19 warned about, appearing in euros.

Three more wrong turns:

1. **Reporting AUC as the headline.** At this prevalence it is flat and it hides the result.
2. **Dropping `Amount` because it is anti-predictive.** It is the cost weight. Keep it in the feature set *and* in the cost matrix; they are different jobs.
3. **A report containing "average precision".** Ana asked for euros and reviews. Half the value of this lab is the translation, and it is the half that generalises to every job.

- **Performance (3):** p99 under 10 ms per request, with a profile — Module 5 measured a single-row `predict_proba` at 5.127 ms of which most is Python overhead, so the interesting part is what you removed.
- **Explanation (3):** a one-page cost analysis in euros that answers the second-reviewer question with a number, and names the assumption that would flip it — here, the fraud amount distribution, whose mean of €99.74 against a median of €19.04 means the saving is dominated by a few large frauds and a quiet quarter will look like a failure.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **Reporting the test-set-optimal cost.** €2,467 is not a number you can promise.
