## Reference solution — `P3-W17-M1`

The task: frame five messy business requests as precise ML problems, or reject them, with written justification. Two of the five should be rejected or reduced. Here is the full worked answer.

## 1. "Predict which of our users will become power users." — **accept, with a re-grain**

| Field | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Unit of prediction | one user, scored on day 7 after signup (not "a user" in general) |
| Label SQL | `SELECT user_id, (COUNT(DISTINCT active_date) FILTER (WHERE active_date BETWEEN signup+8 AND signup+37) >= 20) AS y FROM daily_activity GROUP BY user_id` |
| Label horizon | 30 days after the prediction moment |
| Prediction moment | 00:00 UTC on signup day + 7 |
| Rejected features | `total_sessions` (aggregated to today, not to day 7), `plan_tier` if upgrades happen after day 7, `nps_score` (only exists for surveyed users, and surveying is correlated with engagement) |
| Decision | onboarding team sends a curated setup call invitation; capacity 120/week |
| Baseline | rank by sessions in days 0–6, descending |
| Worthwhile gain | +8 precision points at 120 |

**Why the re-grain matters.** "Power user" without a scoring moment is unbuildable: the same user is a non-power-user on day 7 and a power user on day 90, so the label depends on when you ask. Fixing day 7 fixes the feature window at days 0–6, which is what makes the dataset well-defined.

**The subtlety worth catching:** the most recent 30 days of signups have no label. If your training set silently includes them as negatives, you have manufactured a class of guaranteed false negatives and your model will learn "recent signup implies not a power user".

## 2. "Tell us which support tickets are urgent." — **reject as stated, redefine**

There is no label. Nobody records urgency. Everything you can reach is a proxy, and each proxy is a different product:

| Proxy | What you would actually be predicting | Is it what they want? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agent-assigned priority | which agent triaged it and what mood they were in | no — you would be automating existing inconsistency |
| Time to first response | agent capacity and shift patterns | no — this is a staffing model |
| Ticket escalated within 24 h | escalation, which is a real, recorded, consequential event | plausibly yes |
| Customer churned within 60 days of the ticket | churn given a ticket | this is the business outcome, but is very sparse |

**The rejection is not "no".** It is: "urgency is not a column in any table you own. Pick one of these four, and understand that you are now building that thing." In practice the escalation proxy is usually right, and it needs to be said out loud in writing, because six months later somebody will complain that the model is not predicting urgency.

**Secondary problem:** agent-assigned priority is downstream of the decision. Training on it and then using the model to set priority creates a feedback loop where the model reproduces last year's triage behaviour and the training data increasingly agrees with the model regardless of reality.

## 3. "Forecast next quarter's revenue." — **reject as ML, accept as a forecasting problem**

This is not a supervised learning problem with rows. It is a single time series with roughly 20–40 usable historical points at quarterly grain. You cannot cross-validate a random split on it, you have almost no data, and the dominant structure — a handful of enterprise renewals — is not statistical.

The honest answer: **bottom-up, not a model.** Sum committed contracted revenue (known exactly), plus pipeline weighted by historical stage-conversion rates (a small table, not a model), plus a churn allowance from a cohort table. Then, if you want the ML project, the real one is "predict close probability per open opportunity", which *does* have rows (one opportunity), a label (`closed_won`), a horizon (the quarter) and a decision (which deals get an exec sponsor).

Reframing a bad ML request into an adjacent good one is the highest-value move in this whole module.

## 4. "Flag fraudulent sellers before they list." — **accept, with an explicit selective-label warning**

| Field | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Unit of prediction | one seller account, at the moment of first listing submission |
| Label SQL | `SELECT seller_id, TRUE AS y FROM enforcement_actions WHERE reason IN ('counterfeit','payment_fraud') AND action_ts <= registration_ts + INTERVAL '180 days'` (left-joined to all sellers, absent means 0) |
| Label horizon | 180 days |
| Prediction moment | listing submission, so **before** any transaction, review or dispute exists |
| Rejected features | `chargeback_rate`, `dispute_count`, `avg_review_score`, `gmv` — every one of them requires the seller to have traded, which is after the prediction moment |
| Decision | route to manual review; capacity 300/week |
| Baseline | the existing 14-rule engine; precision 0.22 at its current volume |

**The hard part is the label, and it is a selective-label problem.** You only observe fraud among sellers who were *allowed to trade*. Sellers blocked by the existing rules never generate enforcement actions, so they are labelled 0. Your model will learn to reproduce the rules' blind spots.

The fix costs money and must be argued for: let a randomised **1%** of would-be-blocked sellers through, monitored. That 1% is the only unbiased sample you will ever have, and without it every offline number you report is conditional on last year's policy.

## 5. "Recommend which of our 12 pricing plans a lead should be offered." — **accept, but not as classification**

The naive framing is 12-class classification with the label being "the plan they bought". That framing has two defects:

1. **The label is downstream of the offer.** People bought the plan they were shown. You would be modelling your sales team's habits.
2. **The decision is not "guess what they will buy", it is "what should we offer".** Those differ whenever the offer changes the outcome — which is the entire reason for the project.

The correct framing is **uplift / policy learning**: for each (lead, plan) pair, estimate expected 12-month revenue if that plan is offered, then pick the argmax. Which requires variation in what was offered. If the sales team has always offered plan by company size, there is no variation and the problem is unidentifiable from historical data.

**Practical answer:** ship a rules baseline (by seat count and industry), instrument it, and run a randomised offer experiment on a slice — 5% of leads get a randomly perturbed plan recommendation. Six weeks of that produces the dataset that makes the ML project possible. Say this in week one, not month six.

## Why this solution and not the obvious one

The obvious solution accepts all five and starts building. The scoring here is deliberately weighted toward **rejections and re-frames**, because that is the skill that separates a data scientist from a model-fitter, and it is the skill that is never practised in tutorials where the dataset arrives pre-labelled.

Three specific decisions worth defending:

- **Request 3 is rejected as ML but not rejected as work.** "Not an ML problem" is not the same as "not our problem", and an ML team that only says no gets no requests. Offering the adjacent problem (opportunity close probability) is what keeps you in the room.
- **Request 5 is accepted but re-specified as a different learning problem entirely.** The tell is question 5 of the framing checklist — "what decision does this drive" — combined with the observation that the decision *changes* the outcome. Any time that is true, outcome prediction is the wrong tool.
- **Request 4's 1% randomised slice is a cost, and you must say the number.** At 300 blocked sellers a week and a 1% release, that is 3 sellers a week of deliberate exposure. Quantifying it turns an abstract methodological worry into a decision a director can actually make.

## Grading notes

**3/3 looks like:** all five have a completed `Framing` object; at least two are rejected or materially re-framed with a written reason; every rejected feature has a one-line justification tied to the prediction moment; every accepted request names a baseline **with a number**; and at least one selective-label or feedback-loop risk is identified.

**The three most common ways submissions lose points:**

1. **Accepting all five.** If nothing was rejected, the checklist was applied as paperwork rather than as a filter. Request 2 and request 3 both fail on the label question in ways that cannot be engineered around.
2. **Naming a baseline without a number.** "We would compare against a simple heuristic" is worth zero. "Rank by sessions in days 0–6; precision@120 is 0.34 on Q3 data" is the whole point — it is the number the project has to beat, and if you cannot compute it you cannot justify the project.
3. **Listing rejected features without saying *why*.** The reason is always one of three: it is the label in disguise, it is measured after the prediction moment, or it is missing-not-at-random for one class. Name which.
