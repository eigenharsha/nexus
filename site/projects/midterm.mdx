<Note>**Pick a real, messy, public dataset — not a cleaned competition set.** The mess is the
exercise. Ship a system, not a notebook.</Note>

## What this is, and what it is not

This is the first assessment where **nobody tells you what "done" looks like.** The labs have
acceptance tests; this does not. You choose the problem, defend the framing, and answer for
every decision in a 15-minute defence with hostile questions.

It runs as a two-week overlay alongside Weeks 23–24, which is deliberate: **the deployment
weeks give you deliverables 7, 8 and 9 as a by-product** of the module work. If you do the
weekly labs and this project on the same dataset, you write the Docker image once, not twice.

**Honest time estimate: 25–35 hours** on top of the weekly work. The largest single cost is
data work, and it is the part people under-budget by a factor of three.

## Choosing a dataset

The rule is that it must be **messy in a way that requires a decision from you.** A dataset with
no missing values, no leakage risk, no temporal structure and a balanced target teaches nothing
this project is about.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Good signs" icon="circle-check">
    Missing values you have to reason about · a natural grouping (patient, user, device, store)
    that a random split would leak across · a time dimension · class imbalance · free-text or
    categorical fields with high cardinality · at least two tables you have to join
  </Card>
  <Card title="Bad signs" icon="circle-x">
    A Kaggle competition set with a public leaderboard · fewer than 1,000 rows · already
    one-hot encoded · a tutorial exists for it (Titanic, Iris, Boston Housing, the cleaned
    Adult set) · the target is trivially predictable from one column
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

**Sources worth looking at:** your local government's open-data portal (planning applications,
inspections, transit); the [UCI repository's larger and less-used sets](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/);
[data.gov](https://data.gov) and its national equivalents; a public API you can pull a few
months of history from; or — best of all — **an anonymized extract of something from your own
job**, because you will know what the columns actually mean, which is worth more than any
amount of size.

<Warning>
**Check the licence and any personal data before you commit.** If you cannot publish the
repository, you cannot use this for a portfolio, which halves its value. And if the data
contains anything identifying a real person, you need a lawful basis and probably should choose
something else.
</Warning>

## Deliverables

<Steps>
  <Step title="Problem framing">The decision the model serves, the cost of a false positive and a false negative, and the baseline you must beat.</Step>
  <Step title="Ingestion + EDA">Using the Week 15–16 tooling. Findings written for a stakeholder, not for you.</Step>
  <Step title="Leakage-free feature pipeline">Custom transformers, fitted inside the CV fold. Prove there's no leak.</Step>

  <Step title="Model card + slice analysis">Where does it fail, and for whom?</Step>
  <Step title="FastAPI service, Docker image, CI with a model-quality gate">The build fails if accuracy drops more than 1% versus the committed baseline.</Step>
  <Step title="Kubernetes deployment with HPA and a dashboard">Plus a load test and cost per prediction.</Step>
  <Step title="15-minute defence">Architecture walkthrough, then hostile Q&amp;A.</Step>
</Steps>

### What each deliverable actually has to contain

The step list above is the requirement. This is what a marker looks for.

| # | Minimum | What separates a 3 from a 2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A paragraph naming the decision and both error costs | The costs are **numbers with units**, sourced or estimated with the estimate shown; the baseline is implemented and scored, not described |
| 2 | Notebook with plots | A one-page findings summary a non-technical stakeholder could read, with the three things that changed your plan |
| 3 | `sklearn.Pipeline` with the transforms inside CV | A **stated leakage hypothesis for each feature** — at what timestamp is this value written? — and a test that fails if the pipeline is fitted outside the fold |
| 4 | Three models compared | A validation strategy that matches use (grouped, or time-ordered), **CIs on the difference** between models, and the honest conclusion when they overlap |
| 5 | A threshold that is not 0.5 | The threshold **derived from deliverable 1's costs**, with the expected-cost curve plotted and the chosen operating point marked |
| 6 | A model card | Slice metrics with sample sizes, and at least one slice where the model is materially worse, stated plainly |
| 7 | Service + image + CI | Image under 600 MB with the per-change table (Week 23 M3); the gate demonstrated **failing** on a deliberately worse model |
| 8 | It runs on `kind` with an HPA | Scaling lag measured and decomposed (Week 24 M3); the rolling update proven lossless with a threshold (Week 24 M2) |
| 9 | You present | You answer the hostile questions with numbers, and you say "I don't know" where that is the truth |

## The two calculations everyone skips

These are the ones that most reliably separate a good project from a competent one, and both
take under an hour.

### The threshold, derived from costs

Deliverable 1 asks for the cost of each error type. Deliverable 5 asks for a threshold. **They
are the same exercise**, and the link between them is arithmetic:

```
expected cost per prediction at threshold t
    = P(FP | t) x cost_FP + P(FN | t) x cost_FN

Worked example — an inspection-prioritization model:
  cost_FP = a wasted inspection visit          = £180
  cost_FN = a missed violation, later found    = £2,400  (13.3x)
  prevalence                                   = 4.1%

  t = 0.50 (default) : FP rate 0.6%, FN rate 61%  ->  0.006 x 180 + 0.041 x 0.61 x 2400 = £61.1
  t = 0.20           : FP rate 4.4%, FN rate 29%  ->  0.044 x 180 + 0.041 x 0.29 x 2400 = £36.5
  t = 0.08           : FP rate 14%,  FN rate 11%  ->  0.14  x 180 + 0.041 x 0.11 x 2400 = £36.0
  t = 0.04           : FP rate 27%,  FN rate 6%   ->  0.27  x 180 + 0.041 x 0.06 x 2400 = £54.6

  chosen: t = 0.12 (the curve is flat between 0.08 and 0.20; pick the end that
          the inspection team can actually staff)
```

**The default threshold of 0.5 costs £25 more per prediction than the optimum here** — a 41%
difference from one line of code. Plot the curve, mark your operating point, and say why you
chose that end of the flat region. That last clause is where the judgement is.

### Cost per prediction, with the arithmetic shown

Deliverable 8. Include the components everyone omits — from Week 23 Module 5, logs and egress
were **27% of the bill** and moved the crossover by 28%.

```
Kubernetes (kind sizing extrapolated to a managed cluster):
  2 pods x 500m CPU x 1Gi                      = ~0.35 of a t3.medium  = $10.6/month
  cluster control plane (EKS)                  = $73.00/month
  load balancer                                = $16.43/month
  monitoring node share                        =  $8.00/month
                                                 ----------
                                                 $108.03/month
  at 180,000 predictions/day = 5.48M/month     = $0.0000197 per prediction

Serverless comparison (Week 23 M5's model, same workload)
                                               = $0.0000044 per prediction
```

The interesting part is not the number, it is that **the control plane and the load balancer are
83% of the Kubernetes cost and are fixed** — so the comparison flips entirely with scale, and
your recommendation should say at what volume. **State the crossover.**

**Where the marks actually go.** Evaluation rigor is the largest single band and it is the one
most projects lose. A defensible validation strategy with CIs on the model comparison, a
threshold derived from costs, and a slice analysis that finds something uncomfortable is 20
points. A single train/test split with an accuracy number is about 6.

## What caps your grade regardless of everything else

<Warning>
Any leakage · any unversioned artifact · any metric quoted without a baseline or a confidence
interval · anything that only works on your machine.
</Warning>

Each of these caps the total at **60**, and they are checked before anything else is marked.
They are not arbitrary — each corresponds to a specific way real projects fail:

- **Leakage** makes every other number a lie, so nothing downstream can be assessed.
- **An unversioned artifact** means the deployed model cannot be traced to the code and data that made it (Week 23 M1), so the evaluation does not describe the thing you shipped.
- **A metric without a baseline or a CI** cannot support a decision. "94% accuracy" on a 94%-majority-class problem is a constant function.
- **"Works on my machine"** means the system does not exist.

## The defence

15 minutes: roughly 7 minutes of architecture walkthrough, 8 of questions. **Rehearse it out
loud and timed at least once** — the most common failure is spending 12 minutes on the data and
having no time for the system.

### Questions you should expect

- What's your baseline, and why is beating it meaningful?
- Which feature would you delete first, and what would it cost you?
- Your model is 92% overall. Show me the slice where it's worst. Would you still ship it?
- What happens to this system three months from now with no retraining?
- What's the cost per prediction, and what dominates it?

### The harder ones, which are the point

- **"Convince me there's no leakage."** The wrong answer is "I used a pipeline". The right one names the grouping you split on, why that grouping is the unit that shares information, and the one feature you were most suspicious of and what you did about it.
- **"Your three models are within 0.4 points. Which did you ship and why?"** The answer is not "the best one" — 0.4 points inside overlapping CIs means they are indistinguishable, so you ship on a *different* axis: latency, size, interpretability, or how badly it fails. Saying "they're the same and here's the tiebreaker I used" is a much stronger answer than pretending one won.
- **"Why isn't your threshold 0.5?"** If you have done the cost curve this is 30 seconds. If you have not, it is the question that ends the defence.
- **"This is deployed on Kubernetes. Should it be?"** From Week 24 Module 1's decision table: one service, this traffic, this team size. **The honest answer is frequently no**, and saying so — with the operational-cost estimate and the crossover — scores better than defending a choice you made because it was the assignment.
- **"What would make you take this out of production?"** The model-card question. Distribution shift you cannot detect, a slice where it is unacceptably bad, or a change in the decision it feeds.

<Tip>
**"I don't know" is a scoring answer when it is followed by "and here is how I'd find out."**
The defence is testing whether you can reason about your own system, not whether you have
memorized it. A confident wrong answer scores worse than an honest gap.
</Tip>

## What a strong project looks like

Not a checklist — a shape. The projects that score above 85 tend to share three things:

1. **One uncomfortable finding, reported.** A slice where the model is bad. A feature that turned out to be leakage. A result that contradicts the framing document. Projects that report nothing uncomfortable have either not looked or have edited it out, and markers can tell.
2. **A decision that went against the obvious.** Shipping the simpler model. Recommending against the Kubernetes deployment they built. Choosing a threshold the ML metric disagrees with because the cost curve says so. **Judgement is the thing being assessed**, and judgement is only visible where it costs something.
3. **Numbers on both sides of every trade-off.** Not "we chose X because it's faster" but "X is 1.6× faster and 0.2 points worse; at our volume that is £14/month against 340 additional missed cases a year, so we chose Y."
