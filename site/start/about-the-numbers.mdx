This course is built on a rule: **no performance or cost claim without a number.** That rule is
worth very little unless you also know where each number came from. So here is the provenance
policy, stated plainly.

Numbers on these pages fall into exactly three classes, and every one of them is labelled.

## What each label means

### Measured

Run on a real machine and reproducible. Nearly everything in Phases 1 and 2 is in this class —
the C benchmarks, the socket throughput tests, the hash-table load-factor curves, the
`make verify` output in the labs. These were executed on laptop-class hardware
(Apple Silicon, and Linux x86-64 CI) precisely so that **you can re-run them and get the same
shape of answer.**

**What a reproduction log looks like:** Weeks 5–8 ship theirs in full at
`evidence/phase-1-w05-08-measurements.md` — roughly 80 claims, each with the command that
produces it, against a real PostgreSQL 16.14 container and a pinned dependency set. The
two-session `psql` transcripts in Week 8 are **verbatim server output**, deadlock logs and
serialization-failure messages included, not illustrations someone typed to look plausible.
That file is the standard every measured claim in this course is held to, and it is worth
reading once just to see what evidence for a performance claim should look like.

When a measured result contradicted the textbook, we kept the real result. Three examples you
will meet:

- The famous sorted-vs-unsorted **branch prediction benchmark does not reproduce on Apple
  Silicon** at `-O2` — clang emits a conditional select instead of a branch. The assembly is
  shown.
- **`asyncio` measured slowest** of the three socket models at 1,000 connections, which is not
  the story the week would prefer to tell.
- **"n ≥ 30 and the CLT applies" fails** on real heavy-tailed HTTP latency data — skew was still
  3.33 at n = 30.

### Derived

Arithmetic. The VRAM requirement for full fine-tuning of a 7B model
(weights + gradients + optimizer states + activations) is *calculated*, not observed, and the
calculation is shown term by term so you can check it. The KV-cache size formula, the LoRA
parameter counts, the HNSW memory estimates and the token-cost models are all in this class.

A derived number is often **more** trustworthy than a measured one, because it doesn't depend on
anyone's particular machine. Verify it with a pencil.

### Cited finding

Someone ran a real experiment, and it wasn't us. When a lesson says SMOTE rarely beats plain class
weighting, that conclusion belongs to Elor and Averbuch-Elor's 2022 study, and the page says so.

A cited finding outranks a reference figure — a genuine experiment stands behind it, usually a
peer-reviewed one on more data than we could gather. What it must never do is wear our clothes. A
borrowed conclusion presented as our own measurement is the most flattering kind of dishonesty,
because it takes credit for rigour someone else paid for.

You can usually spot the difference yourself, and it's a habit worth building: **a result that was
really measured moves with the data.** It gets stronger at 2% prevalence than at 20%, it carries a
spread across seeds, and its numbers are awkward — `+0.0031 AP, confidence interval crossing zero`.
A borrowed one arrives clean, round, directional, and with a citation sitting suspiciously close by.

### Reference figure

<Warning>
**These are not measurements.** Where a lesson quotes GPU throughput on an A100, H100, A10G or
RTX 4090, or serving latency on a specific cloud instance type, the authors did not have that
hardware. Those figures are modelled from published benchmarks, vendor specifications, and the
derived arithmetic above.

Treat them as **the right order of magnitude and the right ratio, not the right decimal.**
</Warning>

Reference figures are still worth having — the *ratio* between FP32 and mixed precision, or
between static and continuous batching, is the thing the lesson is teaching, and that ratio is
stable across hardware even when the absolute numbers are not. But you should not quote one in a
capacity-planning meeting without re-measuring on your own hardware.

**A reference figure can also be a modelled *history*, not just a modelled machine.** Some pages —
mostly in Phase 4, where the subject is operating an AI system — describe an operating record:
four months of eval cases accumulated from production failures, three detections in eight months
from a scheduled job, three of fourteen rollouts stopped by a quality gate, a livelock study over
4,200 runs. No such system was ever deployed, and none of that time ever elapsed. Those passages
are modelled scenarios, written to show what such a record looks like and what you should be able
to say about your own system after a year of running it. They are labelled where they appear.

We are calling this out separately because it is the easiest class to read past. An invented GPU
announces itself if you look at the hardware; an invented eight months of incident history reads
exactly like experience. When you meet a number attached to a span of production time — here or
anywhere else — the question to ask is whose system, running for how long, and who was counting.

## Why we're telling you this

Because the alternative is worse. A course can quietly present modelled numbers as measured ones
and look more authoritative for it. Then a student runs the benchmark, gets a different answer,
and has no way to tell whether they made a mistake or the course did.

The labs are built the other way round: they hand you a measurement harness and ask you to
produce your own numbers. **Your measurement on your hardware beats our reference figure, every
time.** If you find a claim on these pages that your own measurement contradicts, that is a
finding — [tell us](/start/faq), and it gets corrected with credit.

## The rule for your own work

Every project you submit in this course is graded partly on this. When you write "this is 3×
faster", you must be able to say which class your number is in, on what hardware, and how
someone else would reproduce it. "It felt faster" is not a number, and a number with no
provenance is not evidence.
